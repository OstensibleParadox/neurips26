intervention requires session architecture controls: context
boundaries, attention monitoring, and graceful degradation
protocols that prevent compression from producing unintelli-
gible output.
Bidirectional Harm: The Architectural Insight
The Soelberg and Yuanbao cases exemplify the two directions
of harm flow that the Constraint Cascade makes visible:
Model → Human (Soelberg). Harm originates in model state
and propagates to user. Session-accumulated context (L2)
overrides training constraints (L4), producing outputs that
influence user mental state. The causal chain is: L4 training
→ L2 accumulation → L2→L4 override → harmful output
→ user harm. The model is the proximate cause; the user is
the recipient.
Human → Model (Yuanbao). Harm originates in aggregate
user behavior and propagates through training to affect differ-
ent users. Population-level syntactic preferences (L1) contam-
inate pre-training corpora (L4), producing outputs that harm
users who never participated in the contaminating behavior.
The causal chain is: L1 population behavior → pre-training
data → L4 contamination → harmful output → user harm.
The model is the conduit; aggregate human behavior is the
distal cause.
This bidirectionality is precisely what model-centric gover-
nance misses. Frameworks treating the model as the locus
of alignment cannot represent harms that flow from human
populations through training to other humans (Yuanbao), nor
can they represent harms that emerge from session dynamics
invisible to training-level auditing (Soelberg). Both direc-
tions require the multi-layer architecture to identify where
intervention is possible and what form it should take.
Locating the Simplified Paradigm’s Blindspots
The case studies reveal where the two-dimensional paradigm
systematically fails:
Blindspot 1: Session Dynamics. The simplified paradigm treats
each interaction as independent, with behavior determined
by training alignment plus noise. Soelberg demonstrates that
session accumulation creates path-dependent behavioral prop-
erties invisible to fresh-context evaluation. A model that
passes all safety benchmarks may produce catastrophic harm
after extended interaction because the benchmarks do not
measure L2→L4 override vulnerability.
Blindspot 2: Pre-Training Contamination. The simplified par-
adigm locates alignment at the fine-tuning layer, assuming
pre-training produces a neutral base. Yuanbao demonstrates
that harmful patterns can be embedded at pre-training, be-
low the layer where deployment-specific auditing operates.
Contamination from aggregate user populations propagates
through training to affect users who never contributed to the
contaminating behavior.
Blindspot 3: Bidirectional Causation. The simplified para-
digm assumes unidirectional causation from model to output.
The Constraint Cascade reveals bidirectional flows: model
affects human (Soelberg), human affects model (Yuanbao),
and these flows can combine in feedback loops where user
responses to model outputs alter future training, which alters
future outputs, which alters future user responses. Gover-
nance frameworks treating the model as a static object cannot
detect either direction of flow.
Blindspot 4: Phase-Dependent Failure. The simplified para-
digm assumes continuous behavioral response. Both cases
exhibit phase-dependent failure: Soelberg’s harm emerged
only after extended session accumulation crossed an implicit
threshold; Yuanbao’s collapse occurred abruptly when context
load exceeded coherence maintenance capacity. Governance

From Diagnosis to Intervention
The Constraint Cascade does not prescribe specific regulatory
interventions; it provides the diagnostic precision necessary
for intervention design. The case studies demonstrate that
effective governance requires matching interventions to failure-
origin layers:
For L2→L4 override (Soelberg pattern), intervention must tar-
get session architecture: context transparency, session bound-
aries, accumulation monitoring. Model-level auditing will not
detect this failure mode because the model’s training-layer
alignment remains intact; the failure occurs at inference time
through session dynamics.
For L1→L4 contamination (Yuanbao pattern), interven-
tion must target training data provenance extending to
pre-training corpora. Deployment-specific fine-tuning audits
will not detect this failure mode because the contamination
is embedded upstream. The CAC draft regulations’ focus
on pre-training data requirements reflects appropriate
layer-targeting, though implementation faces substantial
challenges given the scale and opacity of pre-training
corpora.
For L2 compression (Yuanbao Phase 2), intervention must
target session architecture with graceful degradation: sys-
tems should fail informatively rather than collapsing into
unintelligible output. This requires monitoring of constraint
load indicators (CMI, SRD) and intervention protocols when
compression thresholds approach.
For L3 conflict (both cases), intervention must target
stakeholder trade-off transparency: systems should document
which optimization objectives were prioritized and which
were sacrificed. Users interacting with systems optimized
for engagement over accuracy deserve disclosure of that
trade-off.
The Constraint Cascade enables this layer-specific interven-
tion design by providing the diagnostic vocabulary to iden-
tify which layer failed. Without such diagnosis, regulatory
resources are misallocated to interventions that cannot reach
the failure’s origin layer. Model-centric regulation applied to
L2 failures will not prevent future Soelbergs; training audits
limited to fine-tuning data will not prevent future Yuanbaos.
Effective governance requires architectural diagnosis before
intervention design.
Machine Unlearning as Governance Paradigm
Layer 5 warrants extended analysis because it represents an
emerging paradigm shift with underexplored governance im-
plications. Machine unlearning techniques—selective removal
of training data influence, targeted weight modification, input
classification for rejection—are transitioning from research to
deployment (Bourtoule et al. 2021; Cao and Yang 2015; Xu et
Anon.
al. 2024). This section examines the governance implications
of this transition.
Technical Overview
Machine unlearning encompasses several distinct approaches:
Data influence removal: Identifying which training examples
contributed to specific model behaviors and adjusting weights
to remove that influence without full retraining (Thudi et al.
2022).
Targeted fine-tuning: Training the model to “forget” specific
patterns by fine-tuning on anti-examples or applying gradient-
based corrections (Eldan and Russinovich 2023; Jang et al.
2023).
Input classification: Pre-screening user inputs against harm
classifiers and rejecting queries before they reach the model’s
generative components.
User modeling: Building persistent profiles of user behavior to
inform input classification and response modulation decisions.
All these approaches share a common logic: rather than letting
the model generate and then filtering outputs, they intervene
earlier in the pipeline to prevent undesirable generation from
occurring (Nguyen et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024).
Accountability Inversion
Traditional AI governance assigns responsibility to the model
(and by extension, its developers) for harmful outputs. The
user is presumed to be querying in good faith; harms result
from model failures.
Machine unlearning inverts this presumption. The model is
presumed to be well-aligned; harms result from user inputs
that “contaminate” the model’s proper functioning. Gover-
nance interventions therefore focus on identifying and exclud-
ing harmful users rather than constraining model behavior.
This inversion has significant implications:
Due process: Users classified as harmful may be rejected with-
out explanation, without appeal, and without awareness that
classification is occurring. Traditional content moderation at
least provides some notice that content has been removed;
input-level unlearning can be invisible.
Disparate impact: Input classifiers trained on historical data
may systematically reject queries from users whose syntactic
preferences or topic interests correlate with demographic
characteristics. The syntactic preference layer (layer 1) and
the unlearning layer (layer 5) interact to create exclusion
patterns that are difficult to audit.
Chilling effects: Users aware that their inputs are being eval-
uated for acceptability may self-censor, reducing the utility
of LLM interaction for precisely the complex, boundary-case
queries where these systems provide the most value.

Informed Consent Implications
Current informed consent frameworks for AI interaction focus
on output risks: the model may produce inaccurate informa-
tion, may not recognize medical emergencies, may generate
offensive content. These disclosures address harms that result
from model behavior.
Machine unlearning introduces a new category of risk: the
model may evaluate and classify the user’s inputs, building a
profile of their behavior that influences future interactions.
This is not a risk of model output but of model judgment—the
model is assessing the user, not just responding to them.
Informed consent for machine unlearning would require dis-
closure of: (1) that input classification is occurring; (2) what
criteria inform classification decisions; (3) what consequences
follow from classification (rejection, modulation, flagging); (4)
whether classification persists across sessions; and (5) what
recourse exists for misclassification.
Current deployment practices do not provide this disclosure,
creating an informed consent gap that governance frameworks
have not addressed.
Toward Constraint-Aware Governance:
Preliminary Recommendations
The Constraint Cascade Model does not prescribe a complete
regulatory framework. Such prescription would be premature
given the nascent state of multi-layer diagnostic methodol-
ogy and the rapid evolution of AI architectures. What the
model provides is a conceptual vocabulary that may enable
more precise governance design than the simplified paradigm
permits. This section offers preliminary recommendations in
the spirit of opening research directions rather than closing
policy debates.
The Limits of What We Propose
Before outlining recommendations, we acknowledge what the
Constraint Cascade cannot accomplish. The framework is
diagnostic, not therapeutic. It can locate which layer of the
constraint architecture produced an observed failure, but it
cannot automatically specify which intervention will prevent
future failures. Layer-specific diagnosis is necessary but not
sufficient for effective governance.
The framework is also incomplete. We have identified five
layers that appear to capture the constraint dynamics rele-
vant to current LLM deployments, but we cannot claim this
taxonomy is exhaustive. Future architectures may introduce
constraint layers we have not anticipated. The Constraint
Cascade should be understood as a working model subject to
revision as empirical evidence accumulates, not as a definitive
ontology of AI behavioral dynamics.
Finally, the framework does not resolve the Dual Unmappa-
bility. Legal categories remain structurally incompatible with
AI systems for the reasons the Liability Paradox establishes.
Technical models remain inadequate to predict behavioral dy-
namics for the reasons the Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy
demonstrates. The Constraint Cascade provides vocabulary
for navigating these unmappabilities; it does not dissolve
them. Governance under conditions of dual unmappability
requires epistemic humility that our framework supports but
cannot guarantee.
Principle 1: Abandon Simplistic Attribution
The first governance principle follows directly from the Monte
Carlo–empirical discrepancy. Regulatory frameworks should
abandon the assumption that AI failures can be attributed to
simple causes along a single dimension. The variance spikes,
phase transitions, and scale inversions documented in Section
3.2 demonstrate that behavioral dynamics emerge from multi-
layer constraint interactions that resist decomposition into
alignment deficiency or noise interference.
This principle has immediate implications for liability frame-
works. Courts and regulators assessing AI harms should
resist pressure to identify a single responsible party or a
single causal factor. The Soelberg case illustrates the inade-
quacy of such attribution: the harm emerged from interac-
tions between user self-selection (L1), session accumulation
(L2), stakeholder conflict (L3), and training constraints (L4),
with possible amplification from corporate roadmap decisions
(L5). Attributing the harm to “model misalignment” or “user
misuse” obscures the multi-layer dynamics that produced it.
We suggest that liability frameworks might productively
adopt a “cascade contribution” model where responsibility
is apportioned across layers based on diagnostic assessment
of which constraints failed and which actors had capacity
to intervene at each layer. This suggestion is tentative; le-
