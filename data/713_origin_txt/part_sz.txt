Summary Of Contribution:
At a high level, this paper claims to contribute a "translation layer" that helps legal and policy analysis come to grips with AI architecture dynamics. I don't it succeeds -- but that's what it claims.

En route to that goal, the paper claims to make the follow sub-contributions. The paper does a very poor job of citing prior art to prove that each of these is really a contribution. There are only 22 citations in the bibliography --- woefully inadequate for a paper with so many moving parts --- and in some cases, entire concepts are introduced without any reference to existing literature (e.g., Liability Paradox). The onus is ultimately on the authors to provide this context, in the paper. The authors should not make the readers go scrambling to find the relevant prior art.

Before introducing the five sub-contributions, I also want to point out that in many cases they seem highly disconnected: like a random assortment of ideas throw together in a paper and inorganically connected with a paper-thin premise.

That said, the five sub-contributions the authors claim are:

(1) A "Constraint Cascade Model" taxonomy with five layers (syntactic preference, session accumulation, stakeholder divergence, training forks, alignment roadmaps) that tries to capture the ways that LLM behavior emerges from constraints in architecture and not in model weights alone. It's an interesting read but I'm not sure any of what is here is novel or that it is collected in a way that adds new value to the literature (which, again, is largely uncited). Isn't each layer in the cascade covered by existing literature? If you collect them in a way that doesn't neatly tie them together, is that a contribution?

(2) The Liability Paradox: Here the authors try to formalize the (seemingly already established) impossibility that mens rea attribution is hard for AI systems. This paradox seems to have a been spelled out in an earlier paper (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3709095) and the subject of many subsequent research efforts (e.g., https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/online-archive/law-ai-law-risky-agents-without-intentions) so it's not clear what the contribution is here beyond tying to a formalization that arguably rests on a lot of limiting assumptions and arguably doesn't add much value anyway. What can this be used for?

(3) Bidirectional Failure Taxonomy: The authors claim to introduce formal definitions that claim to distinguish Model→Human and Human→Model harm directions, thereby enabling more targeted governance. I don't see how what is here is any different than the well-trod paradigm in AI safety literature of human vs. model-caused harm. E.g.: https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/understanding-ai-harms-an-overview/

(4) Diagnostic Metrics: Seemingly the sole technical contribution, this Agency Index claims to provide "quantifiable signatures" of constraint stability and layer-specific failures. I think this is the most promising contribution but unfortunately the authors are light on the technical details of how it works, how it is distinguished from prior work (e.g, in the model explainability space), or the details of its evaluation. This is an area of potential improvement to increase value of contribution.

(5) Judicial Connection: Here the authors attempt to map the “products not speech” evolution in a single recent court case to the Constraint Cascade Framework they introduce. I'll be blunt, this mapping just does not seem to make any logical sense and seems like an arbitrary bolting-on of a recent court case to try to make the work seem relevant. The main takeaway seems to be that the single court case here upholds the Liability Paradox, but that paradox does not seem to be a novel contribution of this paper. So I don't see any novel contribution here, even if the connection was logical and lucid.
