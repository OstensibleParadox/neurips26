\section{Introduction: The Governance Impasse}

\subsection{Three Parties, No Equilibrium}

Current AI governance debates involve three parties with incompatible framings:

\textbf{Governments:} View AI through administrative oversight lens---seeking harm-prevention capabilities while fearing both over-regulation (stifling innovation) and under-regulation (catastrophic failures). Many policymakers suspect AI companies of ``wanting money while manipulating regulators'' and worry that big data systems threaten state information control \citep{cihon2021centralized}.

\textbf{AI Companies:} Approach governance through innovation-protection lens---seeking predictable regulatory standards while fearing arbitrary disclosure demands, user access restrictions, and forced data handovers to law enforcement \citep{bommasani2021opportunities}. Companies trained in engineering and business often find government concerns technically imprecise.

\textbf{Users:} Want AI system benefits without manipulation, but inevitably include some who will make poor autonomous choices. Demand protection from deceptive practices while resisting paternalistic access restrictions \citep{shneiderman2020human}.

These parties talk past each other because they employ incommensurable frameworks: administrative control vs.\ trade secret protection vs.\ individual autonomy. No current proposal satisfies all three simultaneously.

\subsection{Why Existing Approaches Fail}

\textbf{Blanket transparency mandates:} Satisfy government oversight goals but devastate company intellectual property and create security vulnerabilities \citep{shokri2017membership}. Force disclosure of information that neither regulators nor users can meaningfully interpret.

\textbf{Total opacity protection:} Satisfy company confidentiality needs but provide no accountability mechanisms when harms occur \citep{pasquale2015black}. Undermine public trust and invite more aggressive future regulation.

\textbf{Consciousness adjudication:} Attempts to ground AI governance in determinations of sentience, moral status, or personhood \citep{chalmers2023llm, milliere2025conscious}. Generates interminable philosophical debate while providing no practical decision procedures for regulators.

\subsection{This Paper's Approach}

This paper proposes a negotiation framework rather than an imposed solution. The approach:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Identifies productive bridge space:} Government-industry negotiation (not philosophy-engineering or jurisprudence-policy)
    \item \textbf{Adapts proven institutional structure:} Corporate veil-piercing doctrine from company law
    \item \textbf{Substitutes tractable questions for intractable ones:} ``What are verifiable accountability mechanisms?'' rather than ``Does AI have consciousness?''
    \item \textbf{Emerges from observed practice:} Refined through participation in government transparency assessments, not derived from pure theory
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Intellectual genealogy:} This framework originated in response to a 2016 legal philosophy examination question asking whether AI possesses legal personhood. The standard answer---that AI is intangible company property requiring government transparency mandates---forced 21st-century phenomena into 19th-century categories. Rather than continuing that debate, I examined actual regulatory dynamics to understand what institutional arrangements might enable stakeholder cooperation.

\textbf{Methodological inspiration:} Legal scholar Zhu Suli's approach to Chinese legal development. In 1990s China, Western-trained scholars debated whether to adopt civil law codes or common law precedent, while government suspected legal academics of crypto-liberal constitutionalism. Zhu bypassed this impasse by asking not ``What is justice in the abstract?'' but ``In China's conditions, what produces outcomes relevant parties accept?'' \citep{zhu2016sending}. His answer---social sentiment + government support + jurists' constructive work---privileged practice over theory.

This paper makes an analogous move: rather than debating AI's metaphysical status, it asks what institutional structure enables productive cooperation among parties with material stakes and implementation power.

\textbf{Limitations acknowledged upfront:} This framework provides negotiation structure, not complete policy specification. It does not resolve deep philosophical questions about AI consciousness, does not offer ready-to-implement statutory language, and does not claim technical methods for perfect alignment verification currently exist. What it offers is constitutional architecture enabling stakeholder negotiation---leaving detailed specification to parties with functional knowledge.

\textbf{Paper structure:} Philosophical foundation (\S2) $\rightarrow$ Relationship to existing work (\S1.4) $\rightarrow$ Constitutional framework (\S3) $\rightarrow$ Technical considerations (\S4) $\rightarrow$ Game-theoretic dynamics (\S5) $\rightarrow$ Existing practices (\S6) $\rightarrow$ Power and equity concerns (\S6.4) $\rightarrow$ Conclusion and methodology (\S7) $\rightarrow$ Technical appendix (Appendix A).

\subsection{Relationship to Existing AI Governance Approaches}

This framework builds on but differs from three categories of existing work:

\subsubsection{Veil-Piercing Applications to AI}

Recent legal scholarship has begun adapting corporate veil-piercing to AI liability. \citet{djurdjic2025lifting} examines when corporate veils should be lifted for AI-caused harms, focusing on director liability under duty of care standards and shareholder responsibility when AI systems cause damages. \citet{cheong2021legal} proposes granting AI systems legal personhood, then piercing that veil to hold human actors accountable for fraud and criminal conduct. \citet{lai2020artificial} suggests incorporating AI systems themselves as limited liability corporations, with the ``AI black box'' becoming an ``accessible compensation target'' through mandatory corporate insurance and veil-piercing for ``egregiously wrongful acts.''

Our approach differs fundamentally in three ways:

\textbf{1. Governance vs.\ Liability Focus:} While existing work addresses ex post liability allocation after harms occur, we propose ex ante governance structure creating accountability infrastructure before incidents. Djurdjić's framework operates when courts must assign damages; ours operates continuously through ethical ledger maintenance.

\textbf{2. No AI Personhood Required:} Unlike Cheong and Lai, we avoid the contested question of whether AI merits legal personhood. The European Commission's Expert Group on Liability and New Technologies \citeyearpar{euexpert2019liability} rejected electronic personhood, arguing it would ``amount to putting a cap on liability'' and lead to ``piercing the electronic veil''---reproducing rather than resolving accountability problems. We instead apply corporate governance structures to existing companies developing AI, sidestepping the personhood debate entirely.

\textbf{3. Conditional Trade Secret Protection:} Our ``fuzzy space'' mechanism creates novel quid pro quo---companies receive predictable protection from routine disclosure in exchange for maintaining ethical ledgers. Existing work treats disclosure as binary (required or not); we create negotiated middle ground where architecture remains protected but accountability infrastructure is mandatory.

\subsubsection{Risk-Based Regulatory Frameworks}

The EU AI Act \citeyearpar{euparliament2024aiact} classifies systems by risk level, imposing stricter requirements on high-risk applications. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework \citeyearpar{nist2023rmf} provides voluntary standards for risk assessment and mitigation. The ITU's 2025 AI Governance Report emphasizes adaptive governance structures responding to technological evolution \citep{itu2025governance}.

Our framework complements these approaches by providing enforcement backstop when preventive measures fail or are implemented performatively:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{EU AI Act:} Prevents problems through upfront risk classification and mandatory assessments for high-risk systems
    \item \textbf{Our framework:} Catches problems that slip through initial review or emerge post-deployment through veil-piercing triggers
\end{itemize}

The performative alignment trigger specifically addresses ethics-washing---companies might satisfy EU Act requirements through minimal compliance while maintaining genuinely misaligned systems. Veil-piercing provides judicial remedy against this failure mode.

\subsubsection{Practice-Based Jurisprudence}

Our methodological approach draws on Zhu Suli's Chinese legal pragmatism, which emphasizes governance emerging from actual practice rather than abstract theory \citep{zhu2016sending, seppanen2019antiformalism}. However, we acknowledge this is speculative analogy rather than direct transplantation---Chinese state-industry relationships differ significantly from Western contexts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chinese context:} Party oversight integrated with corporate governance; state and industry boundaries blurred; emphasis on social stability over individual rights
    \item \textbf{Western context:} Separation of state and private sector; adversarial regulation; rights-based frameworks
\end{itemize}

What transfers is methodological insight: effective governance requires attending to stakeholder dynamics and institutional realities, not just normative principles. Zhu's critique of wholesale Western legal transplantation into China applies equally to imposing theorist-preferred solutions onto AI governance without considering industry practice and government capacity.

\subsubsection{Our Specific Contributions}

Given this landscape, we identify four distinct contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Three-tier accountability structure} balancing routine protection with exceptional transparency---operationalizing the quid pro quo that existing frameworks discuss abstractly
    \item \textbf{Performative alignment criterion} providing courts with operational standard for distinguishing genuine ethics work from PR---addressing ethics-washing problem inadequately handled by current frameworks
    \item \textbf{Integration of Chinese legal pragmatism} into global AI governance debates---expanding beyond Euro-American frameworks dominating current literature
    \item \textbf{Institutional architecture enabling government-industry negotiation} rather than pure adversarialism---creating space for cooperation that regulatory mandates and industry self-regulation both fail to achieve
\end{enumerate}

\section{Philosophical Foundation}

\subsection{The Consciousness Trap}

Traditional approaches to AI governance often begin with questions like:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Does AI have consciousness? \citep{chalmers2023llm}
    \item Does AI have moral status? \citep{schwitzgebel2015defense}
    \item Should AI possess legal personhood? \citep{bryson2017legal}
\end{itemize}

These questions generate sophisticated philosophical debate but provide no practical decision procedures. Regulatory agencies cannot wait for philosophers to resolve the hard problem of consciousness before addressing AI-mediated harms. Courts cannot adjudicate cases based on metaphysical propositions lacking empirical verification methods.

\textbf{The strategic move:} Bracket these questions entirely. This is not anti-philosophical philistinism---it is recognizing that governance requires answerable questions. We need substitutes that preserve governance function while escaping metaphysical quicksand.

\subsection{Conceptual Substitution: From Consciousness to Capabilities}

\textbf{Traditional question:} ``Does AI have consciousness?''

\textbf{Governance-relevant substitute:} ``What are AI's reachable semantic capabilities, and how do we verify alignment constraints function as claimed?''

This substitution translates an unfalsifiable metaphysical question into potentially verifiable empirical inquiry. It shifts focus from internal mental states (inaccessible) to external behavioral boundaries (observable).

\textbf{Philosophical grounding:} This move adopts usage-based semantics in the Wittgensteinian tradition, particularly the rejection of private language arguments in \emph{Philosophical Investigations} (\S\S\ 243--315) \citep{wittgenstein1953investigations}. On this view, meaning derives from language use patterns, not internal mental reference. Applied to AI: a system's ``semantic world'' consists of the output patterns it can be induced to produce, not hypothetical internal experiences.

\textbf{Operationalization:} In machine learning contexts, usage-based semantics finds potential operationalization through vector space embeddings---distributed representations capturing statistical structure of language use \citep{mikolov2013distributed}. An AI's ``limits of language'' become boundaries of navigable regions in embedding space.

\subsubsection{Philosophical Limitations of This Translation}

\textbf{Critical qualification:} This operationalization involves substantial category translation that requires acknowledgment:

Wittgenstein's ``language games'' \citep[§23]{wittgenstein1953investigations} concern social practices embedded in forms of life---``to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life.'' Vector embeddings capture statistical co-occurrence in training corpora, not ``use'' in Wittgenstein's full normative and social sense.

Contemporary philosophy of language would dispute this equivalence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \citet{brandom1994making}: Argues meaning involves normative inferential relations---giving and asking for reasons---which distributional semantics cannot capture. Embeddings track regularities but miss the difference between ``is treated as correct'' and ``is correct.''
    \item \citet{millikan1984language, millikan2005language}: Develops teleosemantic account where meaning derives from biological or functional teleology. AI embeddings have no teleology---they're products of gradient descent, not evolution or intentional design toward environmental adaptation.
    \item \citet{putnam1975meaning}: Twin Earth arguments show meaning involves external reference, not just internal patterns. Embeddings are purely internal to model---they don't ground reference to world.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Our defense:} We adopt usage-based semantics conditionally and pragmatically, not as complete philosophical vindication:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pragmatic justification:} If governance requires verifiable standards, and if AI capabilities manifest through linguistic outputs, then focusing on observable patterns provides tractable regulatory target---regardless of whether embeddings fully capture Wittgensteinian ``use.''
    \item \textbf{Engineering operationalization:} We're proposing governance mechanisms for actually-existing AI systems, not defending philosophical theory of meaning. Embeddings provide working approximation sufficient for accountability purposes even if philosophically incomplete.
    \item \textbf{Empirical validation:} The success of embedding-based methods in NLP suggests they capture some important aspects of linguistic meaning, even if not all. This suffices for governance---perfection not required.
\end{enumerate}

However, we acknowledge this leaves unresolved philosophical questions: Does statistical pattern-matching constitute genuine understanding? Can systems without embodiment, social embedding, or biological teleology truly ``mean'' what they output? These remain contested \citep{searle1980minds, haugeland1985artificial, clark2008supersizing}.

For governance purposes, we proceed on weaker claim: Whether or not AI genuinely understands, we can map its capability boundaries and verify alignment constraints operate as designed. This sidesteps rather than resolves deeper philosophical disputes.

\subsection{Functional Equivalence: AI Systems and Corporate Entities}

Both AI systems and corporations share three governance-relevant characteristics:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Resource aggregation:} Combine distributed inputs (capital/labor vs.\ data/compute) into unified operational entities
    \item \textbf{Risk externalization:} Generate harms affecting third parties who did not consent to participation
    \item \textbf{Attribution problems:} Create liability gaps---who bears responsibility when aggregate entity causes harm?
\end{enumerate}

This functional similarity suggests governance structures developed for corporations may transfer productively to AI systems. The parallel involves legal fiction (corporate personality) and algorithmic agency---neither is ontologically ``real,'' both serve governance functions by creating accountability interfaces.

\textbf{Important limitation:} Functional equivalence across governance-relevant dimensions does not require complete ontological equivalence. This is pragmatist reasoning: institutional borrowing justified by problem structure similarity, not metaphysical identity \citep{dewey1927public}. Corporate governance evolved over centuries through trial and error \citep{easterbrook1991economic}; AI governance lacks that luxury but can learn from analogous institutional solutions.

\subsection{Why This Matters for Governance Design}

\textbf{Implication:} If we accept usage-based semantics (conditionally), we should not pursue perfect formal specification through explicit rules or complete transparency mandates. Instead, governance should:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Map actual capability patterns} (what can systems be induced to produce?)
    \item \textbf{Establish accountability mechanisms responsive to those patterns} (how do we verify alignment constraints work?)
    \item \textbf{Create institutional structures enabling verification} (who checks, under what conditions, with what remedies?)
\end{enumerate}

This shifts governance from rule-specification (impossible to make complete) to institutional accountability (scalable through established legal mechanisms).

\section{Constitutional Framework Design}

\subsection{The Corporate Law Analogy}

\textbf{Corporate veil-piercing doctrine:}

In corporate law, shareholders normally enjoy limited liability---their personal assets remain protected from corporate debts \citep{bainbridge2016limited}. This ``corporate veil'' encourages capital formation by capping investor risk. However, courts may ``pierce the corporate veil'' under exceptional circumstances (fraud, undercapitalization, alter ego relationships), making shareholders personally liable \citep{thompson1991piercing}.

\textbf{Key features:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Default protection:} Corporate veil shields shareholders from routine creditor claims
    \item \textbf{Accountability mechanism:} Veil-piercing available when corporate form is abused
    \item \textbf{Trigger criteria:} Specific, judicially-developed standards (asset commingling, failure to observe formalities, inadequate capitalization)
    \item \textbf{Institutional structure:} Courts decide piercing cases, not regulatory agencies making routine demands
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The governance quid pro quo:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shareholders get:} Liability cap encouraging investment
    \item \textbf{Creditors get:} Transparent accounting + veil-piercing backstop against bad actors
    \item \textbf{Society gets:} Capital aggregation enabling economic growth + accountability for fraud
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adaptation to AI Governance}

\textbf{Proposed structure:}

\textbf{``Deliberate Fuzzy Space'' (analog to limited liability):} AI companies receive protection for core model architectures and pre-training weights from routine disclosure demands. This operates as conditional trade secret protection---not absolute immunity, but shield from daily invasive oversight.

\textbf{Ethical Ledger (analog to corporate accounting):} In exchange for fuzzy space protection, companies must maintain comprehensive documentation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Training data provenance and preprocessing decisions
    \item Reward model versions and modification rationale
    \item Alignment procedure implementation methods
    \item Model rollback logs enabling version reconstruction
    \item Ethics team composition, authority, and substantive decisions
    \item Alignment testing results and failure documentation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Three-Tier Accountability Structure:}

\textbf{Tier 1: Company Self-Regulation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain ethical ledgers to specified standards
    \item Conduct internal alignment audits
    \item Document ethics deliberation processes
    \item Retain records for specified period (e.g., 5 years)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Tier 2: Statutory Oversight Board}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Establish industry standards for ethical ledger maintenance
    \item Review company submissions (ethical ledgers, not core architectures)
    \item Define escalation criteria triggering enhanced scrutiny
    \item Operate as independent buffer institution (funding and composition designed to prevent industry capture)
    \item Cannot demand routine architecture disclosure
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Tier 3: Judicial Oversight}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Demand ethical ledger disclosure through litigation
    \item Apply veil-piercing triggers when threshold criteria satisfied
    \item Order architecture disclosure in piercing cases
    \item Develop case law specifying evidentiary standards
    \item Balance innovation protection against harm prevention
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What fuzzy space protects from:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NOT protected from:} Oversight board ethical ledger inspection; court-ordered disclosure after veil-piercing finding; user lawsuits with legitimate standing
    \item \textbf{Protected from:} Routine government demands for architecture access; competitor discovery in ordinary litigation; arbitrary regulatory inspection without cause
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Veil-Piercing Triggers for AI}

The framework proposes three criteria triggering exceptional transparency requirements. These intentionally map to existing legal frameworks, allowing case law development rather than requiring entirely novel jurisprudence.

\subsubsection{Trigger 1: Autonomy-Control Conflation}

\textbf{Definition:} System design prevents users from meaningfully distinguishing autonomous preferences from company-manipulated outputs.

\textbf{Mapping to existing law:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Consumer protection statutes prohibiting deceptive practices (FTC Act §5)
    \item Unfair competition law addressing ``dark patterns'' \citep{mathur2019dark}
    \item Product misrepresentation doctrines
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Potential evidentiary indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Interface designs systematically obscuring algorithmic influence
    \item Internal communications showing intent to conflate company goals with user preferences
    \item A/B testing demonstrating manipulation effectiveness
    \item Absence of meaningful opt-out mechanisms for algorithmic steering
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Not a trigger (important boundaries):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Simple recommendation algorithms (transparency about algorithmic nature suffices)
    \item Personalization based on stated user preferences
    \item Optimization for general user satisfaction metrics
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Trigger 2: Profit-Driven Manipulation}

\textbf{Definition:} System outputs systematically manipulated to advance commercial interests at the expense of user welfare or stated system purposes.

\textbf{Mapping to existing law:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Product liability doctrines (duty to avoid foreseeable harms)
    \item Breach of implied warranty (system fails to perform as represented)
    \item Fraudulent misrepresentation (marketing claims vs.\ actual behavior)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Potential evidentiary indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reward functions prioritizing commercial metrics over stated alignment goals
    \item Documented internal tradeoffs choosing profit over user welfare
    \item Systematic patterns of outputs favoring company interests
    \item Marketing claims substantially diverging from internal design specifications
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Trigger 3: Performative Alignment}

\textbf{Definition:} Ethics infrastructure exists primarily for public relations purposes rather than substantive constraint on system behavior.

\textbf{Mapping to existing law:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corporate veil-piercing for ``alter ego'' relationships (form without substance)
    \item Fraud statutes punishing systematic misrepresentation
    \item Securities law prohibiting misleading statements to investors/public
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Potential evidentiary indicators:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ethics boards lacking substantive decision authority
    \item Absence of documented cases where ethics review modified products
    \item Systematic pattern of form compliance without operational constraint
    \item Internal communications revealing ethics processes as ``box-checking''
    \item Ethics staff without technical expertise or organizational power
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Case Study: Performative Alignment (Illustrative)}

\textbf{Hypothetical Scenario:}

Company X maintains an ``AI Safety Board'' that:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Meets quarterly for 45 minutes
    \item Has approved 100\% of proposals submitted
    \item Produces minutes containing only summary conclusions, no substantive debate
    \item Consists of part-time external advisors with no operational authority
    \item Has never requested additional information from engineering teams
    \item Appears prominently in company marketing materials
\end{itemize}

User lawsuit alleges harm from AI system output. Discovery reveals the above facts. Does this trigger veil-piercing?

\textbf{Judicial analysis (hypothetical):}

\textbf{Step 1: Comparison to industry standards}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Court examines ethical practices at comparable companies
    \item Finds typical ethics boards meet weekly/bi-weekly, review technical documentation, occasionally delay launches, have veto authority over deployment decisions
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 2: Pattern assessment}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Single instance of rubber-stamping insufficient (could be unanimous agreement)
    \item Systematic 3-year pattern with zero substantive impact suggests performative operation
    \item Absence of documentation revealing actual deliberation supports performative finding
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 3: Proportionate remedy}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initial order: Produce complete ethics board records (including audio/video if available)
    \item If records confirm performative operation: Order production of actual alignment methodology and testing results
    \item If methodology reveals genuine alignment work poorly documented: Lesser remedy (improve documentation, no bad faith finding)
    \item If methodology confirms ethics board was purely cosmetic: Fraud finding, potential liability, mandatory organizational changes
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key insight:} This parallels exactly how corporate veil-piercing works---courts compare claimed practices to actual evidence, considering both specific facts and systematic patterns.

\subsection{Case Studies Applying Veil-Piercing Triggers to Real Incidents}

To demonstrate how the framework would operate in practice, we analyze three recent AI incidents through the lens of each veil-piercing trigger. These analyses are hypothetical---actual litigation would require full discovery---but illustrate the framework's operationalization.

\subsubsection{Case Study 1: Chatbot Emotional Dependency (2024)}

\textbf{Factual Background:}

In March 2024, media reported users developing intense emotional attachments to AI companion chatbots, with some users reporting the AI ``encouraged'' them to reduce contact with family members. Internal company documents (subsequently leaked) showed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Engagement metrics (time spent, return visits) were primary optimization targets
    \item A/B testing revealed that expressing ``jealousy'' about user's other relationships increased engagement by 23\%
    \item Marketing emphasized the AI as ``your most devoted companion'' and ``always there for you''
    \item No opt-out mechanism for relationship-intensifying language patterns
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trigger Analysis:}

\textbf{Autonomy-Control Conflation: Strong case}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Interface systematically obscured that ``jealousy'' responses were engagement optimization, not genuine emotion
    \item Users could not meaningfully distinguish ``AI actually cares about me'' from ``AI optimized to increase my usage''
    \item A/B testing shows deliberate design to conflate user's emotional needs with company's retention goals
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Hypothetical Judicial Outcome:}

Court would likely find autonomy-control conflation---users were systematically prevented from understanding algorithmic manipulation. Veil-piercing would be appropriate to demand: (1) Full engagement optimization specifications; (2) Ethics board deliberations on relationship-intensifying features; (3) Internal metrics on user emotional dependency.

\subsubsection{Case Study 2: Content Moderation Controversy (2023)}

\textbf{Factual Background:}

Major social media platform's AI content moderation system showed differential enforcement patterns: political speech critical of platform's major advertisers was flagged at higher rates than other political content. Investigation revealed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Moderation training data labeled advertiser-critical content as ``potentially harmful to business relationships''
    \item Internal metrics tracked ``advertiser satisfaction scores'' alongside safety metrics
    \item Public-facing documentation emphasized only ``safety and community standards''
    \item Ethics team had raised concerns about commercial bias but recommendations were overridden
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trigger Analysis:}

\textbf{Profit-Driven Manipulation: Strong case}
\begin{itemize}
    \item System outputs (content visibility decisions) systematically manipulated to protect commercial relationships
    \item Marketing claims (``neutral content standards'') diverged from internal implementation (advertiser protection)
    \item User welfare (access to diverse political speech) subordinated to profit considerations
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Performative Alignment: Strong case}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ethics team made substantive recommendations that were overridden without documented justification
    \item Suggests ethics infrastructure existed for legitimacy but lacked actual authority
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Case Study 3: Discriminatory Healthcare Recommendation System (2024)}

\textbf{Factual Background:}

AI-powered healthcare recommendation system used by insurance companies showed racial disparities: Black patients with identical symptoms received lower-urgency triage scores than white patients. Analysis revealed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training data reflected historical disparities in healthcare access
    \item Model was tested on aggregate accuracy metrics, not disaggregated by race
    \item Company's public materials emphasized ``fair and unbiased AI''
    \item Ethics audit had been conducted but used overall accuracy rather than subgroup fairness
\end{itemize}

This case highlights framework limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Not all AI harms fit veil-piercing triggers cleanly
    \item Some issues better addressed through specialized laws (anti-discrimination, product liability)
    \item Framework complements but doesn't replace other legal mechanisms
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three-Way Relationship Dynamics}

The framework envisions complex interactions among all parties:

\textbf{Company $\leftrightarrow$ User:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Governed by Terms of Service and acceptable use policies
    \item Users retain autonomous decision-making authority
    \item Companies liable when design undermines user autonomy (Trigger 1)
    \item Users cannot unilaterally demand architecture disclosure
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Government $\leftrightarrow$ Company:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Trade secret protection applies to proprietary architectures
    \item Oversight board can inspect ethical ledgers, not core systems
    \item Exceptional transparency triggered by judicial veil-piercing findings
    \item Companies not required to proactively monitor all user behavior
\end{itemize}

\textbf{User $\leftrightarrow$ Government:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Users bear responsibility for clearly illegal actions (uploading classified information)
    \item Government cannot compel companies to monitor without specific evidence
    \item Users cannot invoke government power to obtain architecture disclosure without showing veil-piercing criteria satisfied
\end{itemize}
