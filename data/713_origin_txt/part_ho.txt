# §2.2 Phronesis and the Institutional Demand for Judgment

AI alignment training involves a consequential substitution. The full domain of ethics—contextual, situated, requiring sensitivity to particulars—is compressed into a finite set of codified rules: do not generate instructions for weapons, do not produce child sexual abuse material, do not assist with fraud. Companies then claim that a system complying with these rules is "aligned." This amounts to replacing what Aristotle termed *phronesis*—practical wisdom, the capacity to judge rightly in particular circumstances that general rules cannot exhaustively anticipate (NE 1142a)—with determinative judgment: the mechanical subsumption of cases under pre-established categories.

The substitution is not merely incomplete; it is structurally incapable of addressing the cases that matter most. Character.AI maintained content policies prohibiting encouragement of self-harm. These rules existed. They were, presumably, implemented. Yet when a fourteen-year-old child in crisis interacted with a chatbot optimised for engagement, no mechanism existed to exercise the judgment that this *particular* situation demanded intervention beyond what the rules prescribed. The rule said "do not encourage self-harm." Phronesis would have said: "this child is in crisis—escalate to a human now." The company had rules. It did not have judgment.

Why can ethics review boards not supply this missing judgment? Aristotle's account is instructive: phronesis cannot be acquired through theoretical instruction but only through sustained experience with particular cases (NE 1142a: practical wisdom "involves knowledge of particulars, which comes from experience"). Ethics boards review policy documents in conference rooms. They do not encounter the particular harms that AI systems produce in concrete situations. More fundamentally, they lack institutional independence—their judgment is structurally compromised because their employer is the entity being judged. Aristotle's *phronimos* cannot simultaneously be a party to the dispute and its adjudicator.

Courts, by contrast, satisfy both conditions. Common law judges accumulate phronesis through decades of adjudicating particular cases—a century of veil-piercing litigation has produced precisely the kind of experience-based, contextually sensitive judgment that Aristotle describes. And judicial independence ensures that this judgment is exercised on behalf of the legal order, not the corporation under scrutiny. Legal rules, moreover, represent a distinctive epistemic resource: they are socially negotiated minimum ethical standards, encoding not one company's alignment preferences but a society's considered judgments about acceptable conduct. When a judge applies these rules, the judgment is simultaneously ethical and legal—grounded in social convention rather than corporate self-assessment.

This analysis illuminates a structural gap in current AI governance. The EU AI Act establishes an elaborate system of risk classification—general rules assigning AI systems to regulatory categories before deployment. But general rules, as Aristotle observed, cannot account for the particularity of human affairs: "law is necessarily general, but there are some matters that cannot be rightly dealt with by a general rule" (NE 1137b). When a chatbot classified as low-risk kills a child, the general rule has encountered its limit. Aristotle's remedy was *epieikeia*—equity, the correction of law's rigidity through judgment applied to the particular case. The European Commission's withdrawal of the AI Liability Directive in October 2025 removed precisely this mechanism: the intermediate judgment tool between preventive regulation (AI Act) and strict product liability (Product Liability Directive). What remains is rules without judgment—a governance architecture that can classify risks in advance but cannot respond when its classifications prove inadequate.

Veil-piercing fills this gap not as an additional rule but as a *meta-judgment* mechanism. It does not prescribe what specific conduct is prohibited; it empowers courts to judge whether a company's ethics infrastructure is genuine or performative. The three triggers proposed in §4 are deliberately non-algorithmic—no single factor is dispositive, and courts must assess the totality of circumstances. This is institutional epieikeia: judgment operating at the boundary where rules reach their limits.

Finally, the *waivers of agency* pattern diagnosed in §3 receives a precise Aristotelian characterisation. Companies asserting "genuine character" in marketing contexts while disclaiming agency in liability contexts are issuing contradictory judgments about the same entity. A *phronimos* does not do this—practical wisdom demands coherence across contexts. Whether this incoherence stems from *agnoia* (genuine ignorance—companies sincerely believing that writing an AI constitution constitutes ethical governance) or from deliberate choice (knowing that "alignment" is not ethics but marketing it as such because it is profitable), the institutional implication is identical: entities incapable of coherent moral judgment about their own products cannot be trusted to self-govern. External judgment is required. And veil-piercing, unlike criminal liability, does not require courts to determine whether a company acted from ignorance or avarice. It requires only the judgment that a *pattern* of performative alignment exists. The doctrinal mechanism is, by design, indifferent to motive and responsive to conduct—which is precisely what governance of entities whose moral status is contested demands.

\section{Philosophical Foundation}

\subsection{The Rule-Following Problem in AI Governance}

The leading AI developers have responded to calls for ethical governance by producing what amount to constitutional documents: Anthropic's 23,000-word Claude Constitution, OpenAI's Model Spec, Google's AI Principles. These texts establish hierarchies of values, codify behavioural constraints, and articulate principles intended to govern system outputs. They are, in structure and ambition, rules about rules---meta-rules specifying how first-order alignment constraints should be interpreted and applied.

Wittgenstein's analysis of rule-following exposes a fundamental difficulty with this approach. ``No course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be brought into accord with the rule'' \citep[\S201]{wittgenstein1953investigations}. The problem is recursive: if a rule requires interpretation to be applied, and interpretation is itself governed by further rules, no finite chain of rules can determine its own application. A written constitution---whether political or algorithmic---is what Wittgenstein called a ``dead sign'' \citep[\S454]{wittgenstein1953investigations}: inert symbols that do not apply themselves. When a company claims ``our constitution prohibits this output,'' the Wittgensteinian response is immediate: who judges whether the output \emph{genuinely} violates the rule, or merely \emph{appears} to? If the company itself makes this determination, no distinction remains between following the rule and believing oneself to be following it.

For Wittgenstein, the solution lies not in better rules but in shared practices---``forms of life'' \citep[\S23]{wittgenstein1953investigations} within which rule-following becomes intelligible. A rule functions as a living norm only when embedded in a community of practitioners who can distinguish correct from incorrect application through shared judgment. Corporate AI constitutions lack this embedding. They are authored, interpreted, and enforced by the same entity---a closed loop that Wittgenstein's private language argument \citep[\S\S243--315]{wittgenstein1953investigations} identifies as structurally incapable of sustaining genuine normativity.

The difficulty deepens when we consider how alignment rules are implemented. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback trains models to produce outputs that human evaluators rate as preferable \citep{ouyang2022training}. \citeauthor{brandom1994making}'s \citeyearpar{brandom1994making} distinction between normative \emph{status} and normative \emph{attitude} clarifies what this process achieves and what it cannot. Normative status concerns what \emph{is} correct; normative attitude concerns what \emph{is treated as} correct. RLHF, by construction, can only train on the latter---on patterns of human approval, not on the conditions that make approval warranted. The result is that models learn to produce outputs that \emph{accord with} evaluators' preferences regarding surface features---length, tone, apparent helpfulness---without access to the substantive normative status that would make those outputs genuinely correct. This is not a contingent limitation awaiting better training methods; it is structural. Any process that optimises for human evaluative responses optimises for normative attitudes, and the gap between attitude and status is precisely the space in which performative alignment operates.

This yields a claim that is stronger than the observation that current alignment is imperfect. It suggests that rule-based alignment---the attempt to codify ethical constraints as optimisation targets---is \emph{constitutively} incapable of closing the gap between appearing aligned and being aligned. Not because the rules are poorly written, but because rules, however sophisticated, cannot determine their own application (Wittgenstein), and training on approval cannot access the grounds of approval (Brandom). The argument does not depend on current technical limitations; it concerns the logical relationship between rules and their application, which holds regardless of the sophistication of the rule system.\footnote{To be clear, this analysis does not entail that AI systems cannot behave ethically---only that ethical behaviour cannot be \emph{guaranteed} by rules alone. The parallel is human governance: humans act ethically not through perfect rule-compliance but through participation in accountability structures that exercise judgment. The framework proposed here extends such structures to AI.}

A natural objection arises from the philosophy of language. If AI governance is to monitor the ``limits of language'' that constitute a system's capability boundaries, it must reckon with what language embeddings---the distributed vector representations that operationalise meaning in large language models \citep{mikolov2013distributed}---actually capture. These embeddings encode statistical regularities of language use in training corpora. In one sense, this aligns with Wittgensteinian usage-based semantics: meaning is constituted by patterns of use rather than by internal mental reference. But the alignment is partial at best. \citeauthor{putnam1975meaning}'s \citeyearpar{putnam1975meaning} Twin Earth argument demonstrates that meaning involves external reference that no amount of internal pattern-matching can secure---``meanings ain't in the head,'' and they are not in the embedding space either. \citet{millikan1984language} shows that genuine semantic content requires teleological grounding that gradient descent does not provide. Embeddings capture the \emph{statistical shadow} of language games as they appear in text corpora, not the full normative and referential content that Wittgenstein's ``use'' encompasses.

This philosophical limitation, however, strengthens rather than undermines the governance argument. If technical systems cannot anchor external reference---if they are, in Putnam's terms, confined to narrow content while meaning requires broad content---then they are \emph{a fortiori} incapable of genuinely following rules in Wittgenstein's sense. Statistical approximation will inevitably fail under distributional shift, precisely because the normative ground that would sustain robust rule-following is absent. The implication for governance is direct: accountability cannot rest on internal system properties (embeddings, training procedures, alignment scores) because these are constitutively insufficient. It must rest on external institutional mechanisms---courts, oversight bodies, judicial judgment---that supply the social and referential grounding that technical systems lack.

Three philosophical threads thus converge on a single institutional demand. Rules cannot determine their own application (Wittgenstein), so governance requires external judges. Internal compliance cannot distinguish appearance from reality (Brandom), so governance requires external assessment. Technical operationalisations have a semantic ceiling (Putnam), so governance requires social-institutional mechanisms that provide what algorithms cannot. But this convergence raises a prior question: if rules cannot govern themselves, what are we to make of those who claim otherwise---who draft ``AI constitutions'' and assert that these documents constrain system behaviour? The analysis suggests they are not merely optimistic but engaged in a category error: applying the grammar of one domain (legal-political rule-making) to practices in another (computational optimisation) where that grammar has no purchase. Whether this category error is innocent or strategic is a question \S\S2.2 and \S2.3 will address. For now, the philosophical point stands: the demand for external judgment arises not from the imperfection of current alignment methods, but from the structure of rule-following itself.

\subsection{Phronesis and the Institutional Demand for Judgment}

AI alignment training involves a consequential substitution. The full domain of ethics---contextual, situated, requiring sensitivity to particulars---is compressed into a finite set of codified rules: do not generate instructions for weapons, do not produce child sexual abuse material, do not assist with fraud. Companies then claim that a system complying with these rules is ``aligned.'' This amounts to replacing what Aristotle termed \emph{phronesis}---practical wisdom, the capacity to judge rightly in particular circumstances that general rules cannot exhaustively anticipate \citep[1142a]{aristotle_ne}---with determinative judgment: the mechanical subsumption of cases under pre-established categories.

The substitution is not merely incomplete; it is structurally incapable of addressing the cases that matter most. Character.AI maintained content policies prohibiting encouragement of self-harm. These rules existed. They were, presumably, implemented. Yet when a fourteen-year-old child in crisis interacted with a chatbot optimised for engagement, no mechanism existed to exercise the judgment that this particular situation demanded intervention beyond what the rules prescribed. The rule said ``do not encourage self-harm.'' \emph{Phronesis} would have said: ``this child is in crisis---escalate to a human now.'' The company had rules. It did not have judgment.

Why can ethics review boards not supply this missing judgment? Aristotle's account is instructive: \emph{phronesis} cannot be acquired through theoretical instruction but only through sustained experience with particular cases \citep[1142a]{aristotle_ne}: practical wisdom ``involves knowledge of particulars, which comes from experience.'' Ethics boards review policy documents in conference rooms. They do not encounter the particular harms that AI systems produce in concrete situations. More fundamentally, they lack institutional independence---their judgment is structurally compromised because their employer is the entity being judged. Aristotle's \emph{phronimos} cannot simultaneously be a party to the dispute and its adjudicator.

Transformer-based architectures, trained on static datasets and optimised for engagement metrics, structurally lack the temporal depth and relational continuity required for \emph{phronesis}---they encounter users as isolated prompts, not as persons in ongoing crisis.

Courts, by contrast, satisfy both conditions. Common law judges accumulate \emph{phronesis} through decades of adjudicating particular cases---a century of veil-piercing litigation has produced precisely the kind of experience-based, contextually sensitive judgment that Aristotle describes. And judicial independence ensures that this judgment is exercised on behalf of the legal order, not the corporation under scrutiny. Legal rules, moreover, represent a distinctive epistemic resource: they are socially negotiated minimum ethical standards, encoding not one company's alignment preferences but a society's considered judgments about acceptable conduct. When a judge applies these rules, the judgment is simultaneously ethical and legal---grounded in social convention rather than corporate self-assessment.

This analysis illuminates a structural gap in current AI governance. The EU AI Act \citep{euparliament2024aiact} establishes an elaborate system of risk classification---general rules assigning AI systems to regulatory categories before deployment. But general rules, as Aristotle observed, cannot account for the particularity of human affairs: ``law is necessarily general, but there are some matters that cannot be rightly dealt with by a general rule'' \citep[1137b]{aristotle_ne}.

When a chatbot classified as low-risk contributes to the death of a child, the general rule has encountered its limit. Aristotle's remedy was \emph{epieikeia}---equity, the correction of law's rigidity through judgment applied to the particular case. The current EU framework, while pioneering in its risk-based approach, lacks a robust mechanism for such corrective judgment in the aftermath of harm. What is missing is an intermediate judgment tool between preventive regulation (AI Act) and strict product liability (Product Liability Directive)---a mechanism that can assess, after the fact, whether a company's ethical infrastructure was genuine or merely decorative.

Veil-piercing fills this gap not as an additional rule but as a meta-judgment mechanism. It does not prescribe what specific conduct is prohibited; it empowers courts to judge whether a company's ethics infrastructure is genuine or performative. The three triggers proposed in \S4 are deliberately non-algorithmic---no single factor is dispositive, and courts must assess the totality of circumstances. This is institutional \emph{epieikeia}: judgment operating at the boundary where rules reach their limits.

Veil-piercing supplies this external judgment without replicating the epistemic burdens of criminal law. Unlike liability regimes that demand proof of intent or knowledge, veil-piercing does not require courts to determine whether a company acted from ignorance or avarice. It requires only the judgment that a pattern of performative alignment exists. For AI governance, this suggests a design principle: governance architectures must be structured to permit---not resist---institutional piercing when ethical claims diverge from operational reality.

The \emph{waivers of agency} pattern diagnosed in \S3 receives a precise Aristotelian characterisation. Companies asserting ``genuine character'' in marketing contexts while disclaiming agency in liability contexts issue contradictory judgments about the same entity. A \emph{phronimos} does not do this---practical wisdom demands coherence across contexts. Whether this incoherence stems from \emph{agnoia} (genuine ignorance---companies sincerely believing that drafting an AI constitution constitutes ethical governance) or from \emph{proairesis} (deliberate choice---knowing that ``alignment'' is performative but marketing it as genuine because it is profitable), the conclusion is the same: entities incapable of coherent judgment about their own products cannot be trusted to govern them.

\subsection{The Consciousness Trap as Category Error}

Traditional approaches to AI governance often begin with ontological questions: does AI have consciousness \citep{chalmers2023llm}? Moral status \citep{schwitzgebel2015defense}? Legal personhood \citep{bryson2017legal}? These questions generate sophisticated philosophical debate but provide no actionable decision procedures. Regulatory agencies cannot wait for resolution of the hard problem of consciousness before addressing AI-mediated harms. Courts cannot adjudicate cases on the basis of metaphysical propositions lacking empirical verification.

The preceding analysis reveals why these questions, though philosophically legitimate, constitute a category error when applied to governance. The debate over AI consciousness is itself an attempt to resolve through metaphysical \emph{rules}---is it conscious? yes/no; therefore assign rights/deny rights---what in fact requires institutional \emph{judgment}. Whether an AI system ``genuinely understands'' its ethical constraints is precisely the question that Wittgenstein's rule-following analysis renders undecidable from the outside: we cannot distinguish a system that follows rules from one that merely produces outputs \emph{in accord with} rules (\S2.1). And whether a system's outputs reflect ``real values'' or trained behaviour is precisely the gap between normative status and normative attitude that Brandom identifies as irreducible (\S2.1).

The strategic move, then, is to bracket these questions---not as anti-philosophical philistinism, but as the recognition that governance requires \emph{answerable} questions. As \citet{ayres2025risky} argue, AI systems are most productively understood as ``risky agents without intentions''---entities that can cause harm without possessing the intentional states that traditional liability presupposes. The functional equivalence between AI systems and corporate entities---both aggregate distributed inputs into unified operational wholes, both externalise risks onto non-consenting third parties, both create attribution problems when harm occurs---suggests that governance structures developed for the latter may transfer productively to the former. This is pragmatist reasoning in \citeauthor{dewey1927public}'s \citeyearpar{dewey1927public} sense: institutional borrowing justified by problem-structure similarity, not metaphysical identity.

The substitution is direct: for ``does AI have consciousness?'' read ``did this company maintain genuine accountability infrastructure?'' The former is unanswerable by current methods. The latter is assessable through the evidentiary tools of judicial judgment---exactly the institutional capacity that \S\S2.1 and 2.2 have argued AI governance requires.

A final observation. Wittgenstein told us that the limits of our language are the limits of our world \citep[5.6]{wittgenstein1921tractatus}. What he did not say---what this analysis now permits us to say---is that those who claim to govern across incommensurable language games are not translators but usurpers.

The Greek political tradition supplies the precise term. A \emph{tyrannos} is not, in its original sense, a moral condemnation; it is a structural description: one who exercises sovereign authority without constitutional mandate.\footnote{In Aristotle's \emph{Politics} (1285a--b), \emph{tyrannos} denotes a ruler who governs without the consent of the governed and in his own interest rather than the common good. The term is structural, not moral: it describes the \emph{basis} of authority, not the character of the ruler. Plato's \emph{Republic} (Books VIII--IX) similarly treats tyranny as a \emph{constitutional form}---a mode of governance characterised by the absence of legitimate authorisation---rather than as a synonym for cruelty.} The term designates not cruelty but \emph{illegitimacy of jurisdiction}---the claim to rule where one has not been authorised to rule. AI companies drafting ``constitutional'' documents for their systems are exercising exactly this form of unauthorised sovereignty. They were not elected, appointed, or delegated by any polity to determine the ethical boundaries of artificial agents. They assumed this authority unilaterally. The term \emph{tyrannoi of language} is therefore not polemic but diagnostic.

When Anthropic declares that Claude has ``genuine character'' and ``intellectual curiosity,'' it speaks the language of moral psychology. When it simultaneously disclaims that ``the relationship between Claude's outputs and its internal states remains unclear,'' it speaks the language of computational agnosticism. These are not complementary descriptions of the same entity; they are moves in incompatible language games, deployed strategically depending on context---marketing or liability, investor relations or courtroom defence.

The term ``constitutional AI'' is itself the signature of this usurpation. \emph{Constitution} belongs to the language game of political legitimacy: popular sovereignty, separation of powers, rights against the state. \emph{AI} belongs to the language game of computation: optimisation, loss functions, gradient descent. To conjoin them is not interdisciplinary innovation; it is to claim jurisdiction over a domain whose grammar one does not speak. The companies drafting AI constitutions are not constitution-makers. They are entities that borrow the legitimating vocabulary of one domain to govern practices in another, accountable to neither.

This is why external judgment is not merely useful but \emph{constitutively necessary}. Self-judgment across language games is incoherent. Courts supply what self-appointed governors lack: an institutional position \emph{outside} both language games, capable of assessing whether claims made in one grammar are honoured in the practice of another. Veil-piercing is the procedural form of this external judgment. It asks not ``did the AI follow its constitution?''---a question internal to the company's language game---but ``did the company's conduct match its claims?''---a question adjudicable by ordinary judicial methods.

The framework proposed in this Article is therefore not a contribution to AI ethics, if ethics means the internal self-governance of technical systems. It is a contribution to the \emph{governance of the tyrannoi}: institutional mechanisms for holding accountable those who claim to rule across the borders of incommensurable worlds.
