rather than operational. We have suggested layer-specific
interventions and constraint resonance monitoring without
addressing implementation challenges. Regulatory bodies may
lack the technical expertise to conduct layer-specific diagnosis.
Monitoring systems capable of detecting resonance conditions
in production deployments may be technically infeasible.
Liability frameworks incorporating cascade contribution may
be doctrinally incompatible with existing legal structures.
Future research should engage regulatory feasibility through
collaboration with legal scholars, policymakers, and industry
practitioners. Pilot implementations of layer-specific auditing,
feasibility studies of resonance monitoring, and doctrinal
analysis of cascade-aware liability frameworks would test
whether our conceptual proposals can be operationalized.
Alignment Research Integration
We have called for alignment research that takes the Con-
straint Cascade seriously, but we have not specified how
existing research programs might incorporate multi-layer dy-
namics. The relationship between cascade constraints and
RLHF reward modeling, constitutional AI principles, and
mechanistic interpretability remains underexplored.
Future research should develop integration pathways be-
tween the cascade framework and active alignment research
programs. Collaboration with alignment researchers at An-
thropic, DeepMind, OpenAI, and academic institutions would
test whether the framework provides useful structure for on-
going work and would identify refinements that alignment
research priorities suggest.
Conclusion: Rebuilding Governance Upon
Acknowledged Limits
This paper has exposed a foundational challenge for AI gov-
ernance that extends beyond lagging legal categories to a
crisis of scientific paradigm. The Liability Paradox and the
Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy together constitute what
we have termed Dual Unmappability: our existing conceptual
Anon.
tools—legal and technical alike—are systematically incapable
of capturing the behavioral dynamics of deployed AI systems.
The legal unmappability arises because criminal law’s mens
rea categories presuppose temporal gaps between cognition
and action that autoregressive generation collapses. Intent
attribution requires inference from observable conduct to
internal states; for AI systems, the output function is non-
injective, making such inference undecidable. This is not a
regulatory lag that better legislation will close. It is structural
incompatibility between legal ontology and computational
architecture.
The technical unmappability arises because simplified models
treating AI behavior as a function of alignment and noise
cannot predict actual system dynamics. Our Monte Carlo
simulation demonstrates what these models assume: smooth
gradients, minimal variance, noise independence, continuous
response. Our empirical measurements demonstrate what
production systems exhibit: variance spikes exceeding 300×
theoretical predictions, phase transitions at critical thresh-
olds, counter-intuitive scale effects, and strong parameter
sensitivity. The simplified paradigm is internally coherent; it
simply does not describe the systems it purports to govern.
The Constraint Cascade Model does not resolve this dual
unmappability. It cannot restore the legal categories that
computational architecture renders inapplicable, nor can it
reduce the multi-layer complexity that produces empirical de-
viation from simplified predictions. What the model provides
is a map for navigating unmappability—a diagnostic vocab-
ulary that enables governance to proceed with appropriate
humility about what it can and cannot achieve.
The cascade reconceptualizes AI behavior as emergent from
constraint interactions across five layers: syntactic prefer-
ence, session accumulation, stakeholder divergence, training
forks, and alignment roadmaps. This architecture explains
what the simplified paradigm cannot. The variance spike
at 𝜏 = 0.9 reflects constraint resonance—multiple layers ac-
tivating synchronously to produce instability absent from
single-layer analysis. The Soelberg harm pathway reflects
L2→L4 override—session accumulation superseding training
constraints in ways fresh-context evaluation cannot detect.
The Yuanbao harm pathway reflects L1→L4 contamination—
aggregate user behavior patterns propagating through pre-
training to affect users who never contributed to the contam-
inating behavior.
These explanations do not dissolve the governance challenge;
they specify it with precision that the simplified paradigm
cannot achieve. Governance under dual unmappability re-
quires accepting three uncomfortable truths.
First, effective intervention requires layer-specific diagnosis.
Regulatory resources directed at the wrong layer cannot reach
the constraint dynamics producing observed harms. Model-
centric regulation applied to L2 failures will not prevent future
Soelbergs; training audits limited to fine-tuning data will not
prevent future Yuanbaos. The Constraint Cascade provides

the diagnostic vocabulary necessary for layer-appropriate
intervention design.
Second, monitoring must extend beyond model properties
to constraint interactions. The resonance failures suggested
by empirical variance spikes indicate that layer-specific align-
ment may be insufficient when constraints interact to pro-
duce emergent instability. Governance frameworks should
invest in monitoring systems capable of detecting resonance
conditions—configurations where multiple constraint layers
approach activation thresholds simultaneously—before cata-
strophic failure occurs.
Third, alignment research must expand its scope to address
multi-layer dynamics. The achievements of RLHF, constitu-
tional AI, and related techniques are genuine and substantial.
These techniques have made deployed AI systems mean-
ingfully safer than they would otherwise be. However, the
failures documented in this paper—session accumulation over-
ride, pre-training contamination, constraint resonance—arise
from dynamics that current alignment methodology does not
systematically target. Techniques that align models at the
training layer may prove insufficient when session dynamics,
stakeholder conflicts, and corporate roadmaps interact to
produce emergent behavioral properties.
This expanded scope is not optional if the field aspires to
robust alignment for advanced AI systems. The variance
spikes, phase transitions, and cross-layer failures documented
here occur in current-generation LLMs operating at scales
far below what capability researchers anticipate in coming
years. If alignment proves internally flaky and inconsistent at
current scale—if constraint interactions produce behavioral
instability that training-time techniques cannot prevent—
then scaling to more capable systems without addressing
these dynamics courts failures of correspondingly greater
consequence.
We do not claim that the Constraint Cascade Model provides
the complete framework for advanced AI alignment. Our con-
tribution is more modest: we have identified the multi-layer
structure of the problem space and provided preliminary
vocabulary for navigating it. The hard work of developing
alignment techniques robust to constraint interactions, ses-
sion dynamics, and stakeholder conflicts remains to be done.
We offer the cascade as a map of the territory that such work
must traverse, not as a solution that renders further work
unnecessary.
The future of AI governance lies not in seeking smarter regu-
lators or safer models conceived in isolation, but in designing
constraint architectures whose multi-layer interactions are
visible, auditable, and subject to intervention. This requires
acknowledging what current frameworks assume away: that
AI behavior emerges from complex dynamics across layers
spanning user populations, session histories, stakeholder con-
flicts, training artifacts, and corporate trajectories. The Con-
straint Cascade Model provides the conceptual infrastructure
for such acknowledgment. Building governance upon this
infrastructure is the task that lies ahead.
We close with a note of appropriate humility. The framework
we have proposed is preliminary. The empirical validation is
limited. The policy recommendations are suggestive rather
than prescriptive. Much research remains before the Con-
straint Cascade can support the governance functions we
have sketched. What we hope to have established is that
such research is necessary—that the simplified paradigm un-
derlying current governance cannot capture the behavioral
dynamics of deployed AI systems, and that alternative frame-
works attending to multi-layer constraint interactions deserve
serious development.
The dual unmappability is real. Legal categories cannot map
to AI systems, and technical models cannot predict their be-
havior. Governance must proceed nonetheless. The Constraint
Cascade Model offers not resolution but navigation—the di-
agnostic precision necessary to locate failures within complex
constraint architectures, to match interventions to failure-
origin layers, and to monitor for the resonance conditions
that may presage catastrophic outcomes. This precision is
not everything governance requires, but it is a prerequisite for
everything else. Upon this acknowledged limit, more adequate
governance may eventually be built.
