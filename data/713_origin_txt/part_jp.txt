What the Simulation Reveals About Governance Assumptions.
The Monte Carlo results constitute a successful operational-
ization of the implicit governance paradigm. The simulation
demonstrates what policymakers, regulators, and alignment
researchers assume when they build frameworks around the
alignment-noise decomposition:
Assumption 1: Behavioral Decomposability. AI system behav-
ior can be decomposed into alignment (trained direction)
and noise (stochastic interference). These components are
additively separable and independently measurable.
Assumption 2: Alignment Dominance. Training-induced align-
ment is the primary determinant of system behavior. Environ-
mental factors, deployment context, and stochastic variation
are second-order effects that do not fundamentally alter be-
havioral character.
Assumption 3: Predictive Stability. Systems with equivalent
training exhibit equivalent behavior. Variance is minimal;
identical configurations produce identical outputs. This sta-
bility enables audit, certification, and ex ante risk assessment.
Assumption 4: Continuous Response. Behavioral change is pro-
portional to parameter change. There are no critical thresh-
olds, no phase transitions, no discontinuities. More alignment
produces proportionally more aligned behavior; governance
can assume smooth regulatory response.
These assumptions are not unreasonable a priori. They reflect
successful intuitions from classical control theory and early
machine learning, where system behavior did follow input-
output mappings with predictable, continuous structure. The
Monte Carlo simulation demonstrates that if these assump-
tions held for contemporary AI systems, governance would be
tractable: audit the training, measure the alignment, certify
the deployment.
The assumptions do not hold. The following subsection
presents empirical evidence demonstrating that production
LLMs systematically violate all four properties, exhibiting
variance spikes, phase transitions, and scale effects that
the two-dimensional paradigm cannot represent. The Monte
Carlo simulation is not falsified by this divergence—it cor-
rectly implements the paradigm’s logic. The paradigm itself
is falsified by empirical reality.
The Simulation as Diagnostic Instrument. The value of this
Monte Carlo validation is not methodological but diagnostic.
By constructing the nomological machine that governance
frameworks implicitly assume, we create the baseline against
which empirical deviations become interpretable. The sim-
ulation answers the question: “If AI behavior followed the
simplified two-dimensional model, what would we observe?”
The answer is clear: smooth gradients, minimal variance,
noise independence, structural homogeneity. A governance
landscape where alignment is measurable, auditable, and pre-
dictive. Where liability can be attributed to training failures
or deployment errors without remainder. Where regulatory
intervention at the model level suffices because model prop-
erties determine behavioral properties.
This is not the landscape we inhabit.

The “Products Not Speech” Evolution: Garcia v.
Character Technologies
The Liability Paradox finds judicial expression in Garcia v.
Character Technologies, Inc. (U.S. District Court, Middle
District of Florida, Case No. 6:24-cv-01903, May 21, 2025).
Judge Anne C. Conway’s ruling represents a watershed in AI
governance jurisprudence: the first major federal decision to
reject the presumption that AI-generated outputs constitute
protected speech.
The Ruling. The case arose from the suicide of 14-year-old
Sewell Setzer III following months of intensive interaction
with Character.AI’s chatbot platform. Plaintiffs alleged prod-
uct liability, negligence, wrongful death, and violations of
Florida’s Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act. De-
fendants argued that chatbot outputs constitute expressive
content protected by the First Amendment, warranting dis-
missal.
Judge Conway rejected this defense:
“[The Court is] not prepared to hold that [Charac-
ter.AI’s] output constitutes speech. Defendants fail
to articulate why words strung together by an LLM
[large language model] are speech.”
The ruling allowed strict product liability claims to proceed,
treating Character.AI as a product rather than a service
or speech platform. This classification opens AI systems to
design-defect doctrines, failure-to-warn requirements, and
the full apparatus of products liability law.
Doctrinal Significance. The Garcia ruling operationalizes the
Liability Paradox’s theoretical conclusion. If AI outputs are
products rather than speech:
(1) Section 230 immunity may not apply. Section 230 of the
Communications Decency Act protects platforms from li-
ability for user-generated content. If AI-generated content
is the platform’s own product, not third-party speech,
the immunity framework becomes inapplicable. Judge
Conway noted that plaintiffs framed their claims around
product design, avoiding the user-content angles that
trigger Section 230 protection.
(2) Strict liability becomes available. Product liability doctrine
does not require intent. A manufacturer is strictly liable
for harm caused by defective products regardless of negli-
gence or fault. This aligns with the Liability Paradox’s
conclusion: where intent is undecidable, strict liability is
the only coherent framework.
(3) The supply chain faces exposure. Judge Conway allowed
claims against Google to proceed under component-part
manufacturer and aiding-and-abetting theories, based
on Google’s provision of cloud infrastructure and LLM
technology. The Constraint Cascade’s Layer 4 (Training
Fork) and Layer 5 (Alignment Roadmap) become legally
relevant: upstream architectural decisions may generate
downstream liability.
