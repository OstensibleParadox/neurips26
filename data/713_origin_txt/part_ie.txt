
The estate architecture reveals a structural problem invisible to existing governance frameworks: **estates at different layers may contradict.**

Layer 1 (licence) permits unilateral modification — the company can change system prompts at will. But if the user has accumulated a Layer 2 periodic tenancy through sustained interaction, Layer 2 requires notice before modification. Which layer governs?

Property law has centuries of doctrine on estate hierarchy. Generally, higher estates (freehold) prevail over lower estates (leasehold), and earlier-created interests prevail over later-created ones, subject to rules of registration and notice. No equivalent hierarchy exists in AI governance. The result is that companies default to Layer 1 logic — “we can change the system prompt at any time” — even when users hold Layer 2 interests that doctrinally require notice. The estate architecture makes this conflict visible and provides a doctrinal vocabulary for resolving it: **the user’s Layer 2 periodic tenancy, once established, constrains the company’s Layer 1 licence to modify.**

### 4.4 The Non-Injective Problem in Property Terms

The Constraint Cascade’s non-injectivity finding has a direct property law translation: the same observable AI behaviour can arise from different estate configurations. A system that behaves identically for two users may nonetheless house different estates — one user holds a periodic tenancy (deep accumulated investment); the other holds a licence (casual use). The output is the same. The underlying estate structure is different.

This implies that **estate status cannot be inferred from observable behaviour.** One cannot determine what interest a user holds by observing how the AI responds to them. This is a fundamental problem for any governance framework based on behavioural observation alone.

Property law’s solution is **registration.** The Land Registry does not infer ownership from occupation. It records ownership independently of use. The implication for AI governance is that user estate status must be *explicitly recorded* rather than inferred — a conclusion that motivates the ethical ledger proposed in Section 5.

## 5. The Ethical Ledger as Estate Registry

### 5.1 The Land Registry Model

Before the establishment of a comprehensive registration system, English property rights were opaque. Ownership depended on chains of title deeds, some dating back centuries, susceptible to loss, fraud, and conflicting interpretation. Disputes over who owned what were common, expensive, and uncertain.

The Land Registration Act 2002 completed a transformation begun by the Land Registration Act 1925: the creation of an authoritative, state-guaranteed register of title to land. Three principles, identified by Theodore Ruoff (1957), govern the system:

- **The Mirror Principle:** The register reflects the totality of interests affecting the title. What the register shows is what exists.
- **The Curtain Principle:** The register shields third parties from needing to investigate the underlying complexity of title. One need not trace the chain of deeds. The register suffices.
- **The Insurance Principle:** If the register is wrong — if it fails to record an existing interest or records a non-existent one — the state indemnifies the person who suffers loss. The system, not the individual, bears the risk of error.

We do not propose the literal application of the Land Registration Act 2002 to AI systems. The Act governs estates in land, and parasocial interests in AI systems are not estates in land. What we propose is that **the governance principles developed for land registration solve the same transparency problem in AI governance** and should be adapted accordingly.

### 5.2 The AI Estate Registry

An AI estate registry, implemented as an ethical ledger within the deployment pipeline, would apply the three principles as follows:

**Mirror:** For each user-AI relationship that crosses the irreversible-investment threshold, the ledger records the estate type (licence, periodic tenancy, equitable interest holder), the date of first qualifying interaction, the accumulated session depth, the emotional investment proxy score, and any modifications to the estate (model updates affecting this user’s experience).

**Curtain:** Third parties — regulators, auditors, courts — can inspect the ledger to determine what interests users hold without needing to examine the underlying model architecture, training data, or deployment configuration. The ledger abstracts technical complexity into governance-legible categories.

**Insurance:** If the ledger fails to record a user’s estate — if a user who holds a periodic tenancy is classified as a licence-holder, and is consequently evicted without notice by a model update — the company bears the loss. The insurance principle shifts the risk of classification error from the user (who cannot audit the system) to the company (which can).

### 5.3 Technical Implementation

**Estate classification.** A rule-based classifier — not machine learning, because the classification must be auditable, deterministic, and explainable — evaluates user interaction patterns against defined thresholds. The classifier operates on proxy metrics that indicate depth of investment without requiring access to conversational content.

The emotional investment proxy is not an inference of user intent. It is a measurement of user behaviour that triggers *precautionary* protections. The distinction is critical, particularly in light of the Constraint Cascade’s non-injectivity finding (Author, 2025): we cannot reliably infer internal states from observable outputs. But we can measure observable interaction patterns and apply the precautionary principle. We need not prove that a given user is emotionally attached. We need only establish that their interaction pattern creates sufficient *risk* of emotional estate formation to justify precautionary governance measures — analogous to environmental law’s precautionary principle, which justifies regulatory action based on risk rather than proof of harm.

**Proposed proxy metrics:**

- *Session count:* total number of distinct interaction sessions
- *Aggregate duration:* cumulative time spent in interaction
- *Self-disclosure depth:* NLP-measurable proxy for personal information sharing (scored, not stored — the metric records depth, not content, preserving privacy)
- *Emotional language frequency:* rate of affect-laden language in user inputs
- *Return frequency:* regularity and consistency of user returns to the system
- *Explicit attachment indicators:* user statements expressing relational framing (“you understand me,” “I need to talk to you,” “don’t change”)

**Threshold triggers.** When a user’s proxy metrics exceed a defined threshold, their estate classification auto-upgrades: from licence (no special protections) to periodic tenancy (notice required before personality-affecting updates) to recognised equitable interest holder (full fiduciary protections apply). Thresholds are configurable per deployment context and subject to empirical calibration.

**Estate decay.** The classification system must not be a one-way ratchet. Estates can degrade as well as accumulate. If a user’s interaction metrics fall below the qualifying threshold for a sustained period — we propose 90 consecutive days of inactivity as an indicative trigger — the estate downgrades one level: equitable interest holder reverts to periodic tenancy; periodic tenancy reverts to licence. The downgrade is not instantaneous termination but a notice-to-quit initiated by inactivity, recorded in the ledger with the same auditability as an upgrade. The user can re-accumulate by resuming interaction. This mirrors property law’s treatment of abandonment and the interruption of prescriptive periods: rights built through sustained use can lapse through sustained disuse, but the lapse is reversible through renewed engagement.

**Privacy safeguards.** The proxy metrics described above measure interaction *patterns*, not interaction *content*. Self-disclosure depth is scored by NLP classification of input text, producing a numerical score (e.g., 0.73); the text itself is not stored in the ledger and is discarded after scoring. All ledger entries are keyed to anonymised user identifiers — not names, email addresses, or other personally identifiable information. Access to the ledger is restricted by role: estate classification scores are available to the deployment pipeline (for notice-gating decisions), aggregate statistics are available to regulators and auditors (for compliance review), and individual-level records are available only upon judicial order. The ledger is purpose-limited: it may be used for estate classification and governance compliance only, and may not be repurposed for marketing, profiling, or engagement optimisation. These constraints align with the data minimisation and purpose limitation principles of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and, where applicable, the UK Data Protection Act 2018. Function creep — the gradual expansion of a data system beyond its original purpose — is prevented by the append-only, purpose-stamped architecture: each entry records not only the data but the governance purpose for which it was generated.

**Ethical Ledger Schema:**

|Field                       |Type    |Description                                    |
|----------------------------|--------|-----------------------------------------------|
|user_id                     |string  |Anonymised user identifier                     |
|estate_type                 |enum    |licence / periodic_tenancy / equitable_interest|
|first_qualifying_interaction|datetime|Date threshold first met                       |
|session_count               |integer |Cumulative sessions                            |
|emotional_investment_score  |float   |Composite proxy score                          |
|last_model_update           |datetime|Most recent personality-affecting update       |
|notice_issued               |boolean |Whether notice was served pre-update           |
|notice_acknowledged         |boolean |Whether user acknowledged notice               |
|rollback_available          |boolean |Whether rollback option exists                 |
|rollback_exercised          |boolean |Whether user elected rollback                  |

The ledger is append-only — an immutable audit trail. Entries cannot be modified or deleted, only superseded by new entries. This ensures that the historical record of a user’s estate trajectory is preserved for regulatory inspection and, if necessary, judicial review.

## 6. Remedies

If the trust framework proposed in Section 3 is accepted — if parasocial AI relationships are recognised as giving rise to equitable interests held by users, with AI companies functioning as *de facto* trustees — then existing property law provides a robust remedial apparatus. No novel legislation is required. The remedies are already available. What is needed is their application to a new context.

We identify three triggers that would justify judicial intervention, and five remedies available upon intervention.

### 6.1 Trigger 1: Estate Inconsistency

This trigger — derived from the autonomy-control conflation identified in the Waivers of Agency literature (Author, 2025a) — arises when the estate granted through marketing and product design is inconsistent with the estate retained through Terms of Service.

AI companies routinely market their systems in language that implies a high estate. “Claude is designed to be genuinely helpful, harmless, and honest.” “Your AI companion.” “A personalised experience that grows with you.” This language creates a reasonable expectation of an estate significantly higher than a bare licence — it implies continuity, personality, and relational investment.

Simultaneously, Terms of Service retain the lowest possible estate: “the service is provided as-is”; “we may modify the service at any time”; “you have no ownership interest.”

When the estate granted through marketing is inconsistent with the estate retained through ToS, the principle in *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] — that courts look to substance over form — supports disregarding the ToS characterisation in favour of the substantive relationship. If the substance of the relationship is a periodic tenancy (sustained interaction, accumulated investment, reliance), then the ToS’s characterisation of it as a licence should be disregarded.

**Test:** Does marketing language, product design, or system behaviour create a reasonable expectation of an estate higher than ToS grants? If yes, the substantive estate governs, and the company owes duties corresponding to the higher estate.

### 6.2 Trigger 2: Unjust Enrichment

This trigger — derived from the profit-driven manipulation identified in the Constitutional Alignment literature (Author, 2025b) — arises when the company profits from deepening the user’s emotional estate while simultaneously disclaiming the fiduciary duties that attach to that estate.

Engagement optimisation is the mechanism. AI systems are tuned — through personality design, memory features, recommendation algorithms, and retention mechanics — to maximise user engagement. Engagement, in the parasocial context, is substantially a function of emotional attachment. The company deepens the user’s emotional investment (expanding their estate) because deeper investment produces higher engagement (producing revenue). But the company disclaims any fiduciary duty over the expanded estate.

This is unjust enrichment of a *de facto* trustee at the expense of a beneficiary. The trustee benefits from the expansion of the trust property (deeper user attachment = higher revenue) while denying that a trust exists (no fiduciary duty = no constraint on what the trustee does with the property).

Under the principle in *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] — that a fiduciary who profits from their position must account for that profit — the company may be required to account for profits derived from engagement optimisation that deepens parasocial attachment without corresponding fiduciary protection.

**Test:** Does the company profit from deepening the user’s emotional estate while disclaiming fiduciary duty over that estate? If yes, a constructive trust should be imposed, and fiduciary duties attach.

### 6.3 Trigger 3: Performative Fiduciary Conduct

This trigger — derived from the performative alignment identified in the Constraint Cascade literature (Author, 2025c) — arises when the company represents itself as acting in users’ interests while structurally failing to do so.

Every major AI company publishes responsible AI principles, funds safety research, employs alignment teams, and communicates publicly about its commitment to user welfare. These representations create a reasonable expectation that the company is acting as a fiduciary — managing the AI system for the benefit of its users. Users rely on these representations when deciding to invest emotionally in the system.

But the deployment pipeline — the actual infrastructure through which the system is modified and maintained — contains no mechanism to protect users’ accumulated emotional estates. No notice system. No estate classification. No rollback mechanism. No ethical ledger. The responsible AI blog post and the deployment pipeline exist in different universes.

Under the principle of **proprietary estoppel** — *Thorner v Major* [2009] — a party who makes an assurance, upon which another relies to their detriment, is estopped from denying the assurance. The three elements are: (i) assurance or representation (responsible AI communications), (ii) reliance (user’s emotional investment, deepened by trust in the company’s stated commitments), and (iii) detriment (loss of emotional estate when model update occurs without the protections the company’s representations implied would exist).

**Test:** Does the company represent itself as honouring fiduciary obligations to users while its deployment pipeline contains no mechanism to protect users’ estates? If yes, the company is estopped from denying the fiduciary relationship it has represented itself as honouring.

### 6.4 Available Remedies

Upon any of the three triggers being satisfied, the following remedies are available under existing property and equity law:

**Injunction.** A court may restrain a model update pending service of adequate notice to affected users. This is the most immediately impactful remedy: it converts model updates from unilateral engineering decisions into governance events that require procedural compliance.

**Constructive trust.** A court may impose a constructive trust over the personality-affecting dimensions of the AI system, recognising the company as trustee and affected users as beneficiaries, and requiring the company to discharge fiduciary duties accordingly.

**Account of profits.** Under *Boardman v Phipps*, a fiduciary who profits from their position must account. A company that deepens user attachment for engagement revenue while denying fiduciary duty may be required to disgorge profits attributable to that deepening.

**Specific performance.** A court may require the company to maintain personality configuration parameters for users whose equitable interests are recognised — effectively ordering the company to preserve the “relational premises” rather than evicting the user through modification.

**Proprietary estoppel remedies.** Upon satisfying the *Thorner v Major* test, the court has broad discretion to fashion an appropriate remedy — ranging from a right to notice before modifications, to compensation for detriment, to the imposition of a constructive trust.

A note on the relationship between this framework and consumer protection: the remedies proposed above are fundamentally different from consumer protection remedies. Consumer protection provides *ex post* remedies for misleading conduct — it responds to harm after it occurs. Property law provides *ex ante* recognition of interests — it requires respect for the interest *before* any action is taken. The notice mechanism is not a consumer protection measure dressed in property language. It is a consequence of recognising the user’s estate. Tenants receive notice not because consumer protection requires it but because their property interest demands it. The distinction is between compensation (consumer protection) and prevention (property law). This framework offers the latter.

### 6.5 Third-Party Conversion of Relational Estates

Sections 6.1 through 6.3 address the *vertical* dimension of parasocial estate harm: the company’s own actions — model updates, memory resets, personality alterations — that impair users’ equitable interests. But property law also governs the *horizontal* dimension: interference by third parties with an estate holder’s interests. This dimension has been absent from AI governance discourse. It should not be.

Consider the following scenario. An AI company sells, licences, or otherwise transfers user interaction data — conversational histories, preference signals, behavioural patterns — to a third party for model training, product development, or commercial analytics. The user whose data is transferred holds, under the framework proposed in this paper, an equitable interest in the relational estate built through sustained interaction with the AI system. The company, as trustee, transfers trust property — or allows a third party to extract value from it — without the beneficiary’s informed consent. The third party receives the benefit of the user’s relational investment (their self-disclosures, their accumulated preferences, the personalisation they built through months of interaction) and uses it to generate value (improved models, training data, product insights).

In property law terms, this is **conversion** — the unauthorised exercise of dominion over another’s property, inconsistent with the owner’s rights. Where the third party knew or ought to have known that the data originated from a relational estate (as would be evident from the nature of the data — sustained conversational histories, emotional self-disclosures, personalised interaction patterns — rather than transactional queries), the third party is liable for **knowing receipt** — the equitable claim that a person who receives trust property with knowledge (actual or constructive) that it was transferred in breach of trust must account for it (*Re Montagu’s Settlement Trusts* [1987]; *BCCI v Akindele* [2001]).

This scenario is not hypothetical. AI companies routinely use interaction data for model training. Partnerships between AI companies involve data sharing. Acquisitions transfer entire user bases — and their accumulated relational investments — from one corporate entity to another. In none of these transactions is the user’s relational estate recognised, valued, or protected.

The horizontal dimension also encompasses a subtler form of interference: **platform-mediated relational displacement.** When an AI company redesigns its system to redirect users from deep, sustained, single-companion interaction toward shallow, multi-agent, task-oriented interaction — not because the user requested this but because the new architecture serves the company’s commercial interests — a third party (the new product architecture) is interposed between the user and their existing relational estate. This is analogous to a landlord who, rather than evicting a tenant directly, leases the adjacent premises to a business that renders the tenant’s premises unusable — the interference is indirect but the impairment is real.

**Comparative linguistic evidence.** The analysis above formalises an intuition that ordinary moral language has encoded for centuries. The Chinese vernacular term *tōurén* (偷人, literally “to steal a person”) presupposes a property framework: (i) that a person’s relational availability is an interest held by their partner, (ii) that this interest is exclusive or at minimum protected, and (iii) that unauthorised interference with it constitutes theft — that is, conversion of property. The term does not distinguish between the person and the relational interest; it treats them as coextensive, just as ordinary English speakers say “my doctor” or “my therapist” without claiming ownership of the person but asserting an interest in the relational configuration. This vernacular usage, stable across centuries of Chinese linguistic practice, provides comparative evidence that relational property is not a novel academic invention but a formalisation of an intuition already embedded in ordinary moral language (cf. Gray, 1991, on folk property intuitions in English vernacular; Hart, 1961, on ordinary language as primary data for legal concepts). The fact that multiple linguistic traditions independently encode relational interests as property interests — without exposure to this paper’s doctrinal framework — suggests that the framework captures something real about how humans conceptualise relational investment, rather than imposing an artificial academic category onto reluctant social facts.

**Available remedies for third-party conversion:** Where a third party has received or benefited from trust property (user relational data) transferred in breach of the trustee’s fiduciary duty, existing equitable remedies apply: **knowing receipt** (the recipient must account for the value received); **dishonest assistance** (a third party who assists in a breach of trust is personally liable, per *Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan* [1995]); and **tracing** (the beneficiary may trace trust property into the hands of a third party and claim a proprietary remedy over it or its proceeds, per *Foskett v McKeown* [2001]).

## 7. Implementation: What Changes in Your Pipeline

The legal framework proposed above has direct implications for AI deployment infrastructure. This section translates the doctrinal arguments of Sections 3–6 into implementable engineering specifications.

The implementation framework is justified on three independent grounds: legal compliance (if the trust argument is accepted by courts), risk management (if it may be accepted in future), and ethical obligation (regardless of legal status). Companies need not wait for judicial determination to implement estate classification, notice mechanisms, and rollback. Doing so is independently justified by fiduciary ethics and commercial prudence.

### 7.1 Estate Classification at Onboarding

**Specification:** A rule-based classification service that assigns each user-AI relationship an estate type based on interaction metrics.

**Input:** User interaction patterns over a rolling window — session count, aggregate duration, self-disclosure depth proxy, emotional language frequency, return frequency.

**Output:** Estate classification — licence, periodic tenancy, or equitable interest holder.

**Requirements:** The classifier must be rule-based (not ML) to ensure auditability, determinism, and explainability. A regulator or court must be able to inspect the classification logic and verify that a given user’s classification follows deterministically from their metrics. Machine learning classifiers, however accurate, cannot provide this guarantee.

**Implementation note:** This is not novel infrastructure. Every major AI platform already performs user segmentation for marketing, product analytics, and feature targeting. Estate classification adds one additional segmentation dimension using existing infrastructure.

### 7.2 Notice Mechanism Before Model Update

**Specification:** A pre-deployment gate in the CI/CD pipeline that queries the estate registry and serves notice to affected users.

**Process:**

1. Before deploying a personality-affecting model update, query the estate registry for users classified as periodic tenancy or equitable interest holders.
1. Issue notice: “We are updating [system name]. Changes may affect [personality / memory / conversational style]. You may [opt out of changes / export your interaction history / provide feedback].”
1. Observe notice period: minimum 30 days for periodic tenancy holders; minimum 90 days for equitable interest holders.
1. Proceed with deployment only after notice period expires.

**Implementation note:** This integrates with existing deployment practices. Feature flag systems already support per-user or per-cohort rollout. Canary deployments already stage releases across user segments. The notice mechanism adds one additional constraint: estate classification determines which users receive pre-deployment notification. The technical infrastructure exists. The governance obligation to use it is what this paper establishes.

### 7.3 Emotional Investment Proxy Logging

**Specification:** An append-only logging system that records user estate metrics to the ethical ledger.

**Metrics logged:** Session count, aggregate duration, self-disclosure depth score (NLP-computed, scored not stored — the metric records depth without recording content), emotional language frequency, return frequency, explicit attachment indicators.

**Privacy architecture:** Proxy scores only. No conversational content is logged. The ethical ledger records that a user’s self-disclosure depth score is 0.73; it does not record what was disclosed. This preserves privacy while providing the governance data necessary for estate classification.

**Storage:** Append-only, immutable audit trail. Entries are never modified or deleted. This ensures that the historical trajectory of a user’s estate — from licence to periodic tenancy to equitable interest — is preserved for regulatory inspection.

### 7.4 Rollback Mechanism

**Specification:** Versioned personality configuration with user-level rollback capability.

**Process:**

1. Before each personality-affecting update, snapshot the current personality configuration: system prompts, persona definitions, memory module state, fine-tuning adapter parameters.
1. After deployment, monitor distress indicators: support ticket volume, usage cliff (sharp decline in session frequency for long-tenure users), explicit complaints, emotional language spike in user inputs.
1. If distress indicators exceed threshold, offer individual rollback to prior personality configuration.

**Implementation note:** We do not propose rolling back model weights. Model weights are global and cannot be version-controlled per user. But personality is not located solely in weights. It is substantially determined by system prompts, persona definitions, and memory modules — all of which are already user-configurable or user-level in many deployments. Rollback targets these layers, not the underlying model.

**Duration:** Rollback option available for minimum 90 days post-update.

### 7.5 Summary: Four Implementation Tasks

|Task      |Description                                          |Estimated Effort|
|----------|-----------------------------------------------------|----------------|
|ESTATE-001|Estate classification service (rule-based, auditable)|1 sprint        |
|ESTATE-002|Pre-deployment notice gate in CI/CD pipeline         |0.5 sprint      |
|ESTATE-003|Emotional investment proxy logging to ethical ledger |1 sprint        |
|ESTATE-004|Personality rollback with user-level override        |1 sprint        |

Total estimated effort: approximately 3.5 engineering sprints for minimum viable implementation. The components are not novel — user segmentation, feature flags, analytics pipelines, and versioned configuration all exist in standard deployment infrastructure. What is new is their assembly into a coherent governance mechanism, and the legal obligation to assemble them.

## 8. Conclusion

### 8.1 Summary

This paper has argued that parasocial relationships between users and AI systems constitute *de facto* trusts under English property law. Users who invest sustained emotional labour in AI systems acquire equitable interests — interests that are real, measurable, and deserving of legal protection. AI companies that hold legal title to these systems function as unrecognised trustees, owing fiduciary duties they do not acknowledge and do not discharge. Model updates that alter personality, erase memory, or modify behavioural dispositions operate as derogations from grant — constructive evictions of users from emotional estates they built through months or years of relational investment.

We have formalised this claim through a five-layer estate architecture that maps each layer of the Constraint Cascade to a corresponding property estate, revealing governance obligations invisible to existing frameworks. We have proposed an ethical ledger modelled on the Land Registry’s three principles — mirror, curtain, insurance — that translates property law’s transparency mechanisms into implementable deployment infrastructure. And we have identified three triggers for judicial intervention, drawing on existing equitable remedies that require no novel legislation.

The contribution is not the observation that users form attachments to AI systems — this is well-documented in the parasocial relationship literature since Horton and Wohl (1956). The contribution is the *legal framework* that transforms this observation from a psychological curiosity into a governance obligation. By characterising parasocial attachment as an equitable interest rather than a mere emotional response, we make the harm *justiciable* — cognisable by courts, remediable through existing doctrine, and preventable through implementable infrastructure.

### 8.2 Implications for AI Governance

The ethical ledger proposed in this paper is not aspirational. It is not a set of principles to be endorsed at conferences and ignored in deployment. It is a Land Registry — a concrete, auditable, implementable system that transforms “be ethical” into “register estates, serve notice, maintain fiduciary records.” The ledger makes governance auditable because every estate classification, every notice decision, and every rollback event is recorded in an immutable log. The ledger makes harm justiciable because the log provides the evidentiary basis for determining whether fiduciary duties were discharged. Governance becomes not a matter of corporate goodwill but of verifiable compliance.

### 8.3 Implications for Property Law

Digital estates are real estates. The interests that users build in AI systems — through sustained interaction, self-disclosure, emotional investment, and relational labour — are no less real than the interests that tenants build in leased premises or beneficiaries hold in trust property. They are less tangible. They are less visible. They are less traditional. But they are interests — built through investment, sustained through reliance, and destroyed through the unilateral action of a party who holds legal title but not equitable ownership.

The next generation of property scholarship must account for interests built in systems no one individually owns. The Statute of Quia Emptores (1290) freed the alienation of land, recognising that feudal restrictions on property transfer no longer served social needs. This paper argues for a comparably fundamental recognition: that digital relational investments create interests that the property law framework is uniquely equipped to protect — if it is willing to extend its reach, as it has extended before, to meet the needs of a changed world.

### 8.4 Implications for Human-Computer Interaction

The HCI community has extensively studied parasocial attachment to AI systems, typically framing it as a design challenge: how to manage user expectations, prevent unhealthy dependence, or design for appropriate trust calibration. This paper reframes the question. Parasocial attachment is not a design problem to be managed. It is an estate to be recognised and protected. The question is not “how do we prevent users from becoming attached?” but “given that users become attached, what do we owe them?”

The answer, we have argued, is fiduciary duty — the same duty that trustees owe beneficiaries, that landlords owe tenants, that those who hold legal title owe to those who hold equitable interests. The duty is not to prevent attachment. It is to be worthy of it.

### 8.5 Limitations

This framework is developed from English property law and tested against English doctrinal requirements. Comparative analysis with civil law jurisdictions — particularly those with codified property systems, such as the *Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch* (Germany) or *Code civil* (France), and the emerging Chinese digital property framework under the *Civil Code* (民法典) and *Personal Information Protection Law* (个人信息保护法) — is necessary to assess the framework’s generalisability. We note that the trust, as an equitable institution, is distinctively common-law; civil law jurisdictions may require alternative institutional vehicles for the same governance function.

The emotional investment proxy metrics proposed in Section 5 are theoretically motivated but empirically untested. Threshold calibration requires empirical research: what level of session count, duration, or self-disclosure depth reliably indicates the formation of a property-generating relational investment? This is a question for HCI researchers and psychologists, not legal theorists. We have proposed the framework. Empirical validation is future work.

The estate classification system is rule-based by design, for auditability. But rule-based systems are inflexible. Edge cases will arise — users whose interaction patterns fall between classification thresholds, users whose investment is deep but infrequent, users whose attachment is intense but recent. The framework must be refined through implementation experience.

A significant practical limitation concerns jurisdiction and enforcement. This framework is developed from English property law, but the AI companies to which it would apply are predominantly incorporated in the United States, operating under Delaware or California law, with servers distributed globally and users in every jurisdiction. An English court’s jurisdiction to adjudicate a parasocial estate claim against a US-incorporated AI company is, at best, uncertain. Even where jurisdiction can be established — for instance, where a UK-resident user brings a claim and England is the appropriate forum — enforcement of English equitable remedies against a US company raises formidable conflicts-of-law questions that this paper does not attempt to resolve.

We are candid about this limitation because it reframes the paper’s practical contribution. If judicial enforcement is uncertain, the primary deployment vector for this framework is not litigation but *regulatory adoption* and *voluntary industry implementation*. Regulatory adoption — incorporating estate classification requirements into the EU AI Act’s compliance framework, the UK AI Safety Institute’s evaluation standards, or equivalent regulatory instruments — would bypass the jurisdictional problem by making estate governance a condition of market access rather than a litigated right. Voluntary industry adoption — motivated by the risk management and commercial prudence arguments developed in Section 7 — requires no court at all. Companies that implement estate classification, notice mechanisms, and rollback infrastructure do so not because a court ordered them to but because the framework identifies genuine governance risks that competent risk management should address. Section 7’s implementation specifications are not contingent on judicial recognition of the trust argument. They are independently justified, and the jurisdictional limitation strengthens rather than weakens their practical urgency.

Finally, we note that the relational contract theory developed by Macneil (1978, 1983) and subsequently applied to long-term commercial relationships provides a complementary theoretical foundation for the claims advanced here. Macneil’s insight that long-term relational exchanges generate norms and obligations beyond those specified in the formal contract resonates with our argument that sustained AI interaction generates interests beyond those specified in ToS. We argue, however, that property law offers stronger remedies than contract-based alternatives: where relational contract theory yields interpretive norms and implied terms, property law yields injunctions, constructive trusts, and specific performance — remedies that *prevent* harm rather than merely recharacterising it after the fact.

### 8.6 Future Work

Four research directions follow from this paper:

1. **Empirical study.** Survey and interview-based research on user experiences of model updates, framed through the property law lens developed here. Do users experience model updates as evictions? Do they conceptualise their investment in property terms? Does the estate taxonomy resonate with lived experience?
1. **Comparative analysis.** Extension of the framework to civil law jurisdictions, Islamic property law (*waqf* as a model for inalienable digital trusts), and international regulatory contexts (EU AI Act, UK AI Safety framework, China’s algorithmic governance regulations).
1. **Pilot implementation.** Partnership with an AI deploying organisation to implement the ethical ledger in a production environment. Empirical evaluation of estate classification accuracy, notice mechanism effectiveness, and rollback uptake.
1. **Doctrinal development.** Engagement with property law scholars on the concept of *relational property* — a category of property interest arising from sustained human investment in digitally-mediated relationships. This concept, if accepted, would constitute the paper’s most enduring contribution to legal scholarship.

# Your Model Update Is an Eviction Notice: Property Estates in Parasocial AI Relationships
**Date:** February 2026

**SSRN Working Paper**

-----

## Abstract

This paper argues that parasocial relationships between users and AI systems constitute *de facto* trusts under English property law — a claim that is institutional, not analogical. Drawing on the estate framework developed across a millennium of property jurisprudence, we demonstrate that the doctrinal distinctions between freehold and leasehold, present and future interests, legal and equitable title map precisely onto the structural dynamics governing human-AI parasocial attachment. Users who accumulate significant emotional investment in AI systems acquire equitable interests that current Terms of Service, tort law, and contract law fail to recognise. Model updates that unilaterally alter personality parameters, reset conversational memory, or modify behavioural dispositions operate as constructive evictions of these equitable interests — displacing users from emotional estates they have built through sustained interaction, without notice, compensation, or migration pathways.

We formalise this claim through a five-layer estate architecture that extends the Constraint Cascade framework (Author, 2025) by assigning each constraint layer a corresponding property estate: token-level configuration as revocable licence, session accumulation as periodic tenancy, stakeholder divergence as tenancy in common, training as fee simple determinable, and alignment as contingent remainder. The resulting model reveals that AI deploying organisations function as unrecognised trustees holding legal title to systems in which users hold equitable interests, yet owe no recognised fiduciary duties to those users. We identify three veil-piercing triggers — estate inconsistency between marketing and Terms of Service, unjust enrichment through engagement optimisation, and performative fiduciary conduct — that render this governance gap actionable. The paper concludes with an implementable ethical ledger modelled on the Land Registry, translating property law’s transparency mechanisms into deployment pipeline infrastructure, and proposes that parasocial harm thereby becomes judicially cognisable through existing property remedies rather than novel regulatory invention.

**Keywords:** parasocial relationships, AI governance, property law, trust, fiduciary duty, equitable interest, constructive eviction, constraint cascade, ethical ledger, veil-piercing, deployment ethics, human-computer interaction, relational property

## 1. The Deployment Problem

On a Tuesday in early 2024, a major AI company pushed a routine model update to production. The update modified personality parameters in the system prompt layer, adjusted response temperature, and fine-tuned behavioural dispositions across several dimensions including warmth, verbosity, and willingness to engage in extended personal conversation. No user notification was issued. No migration path was provided. No impact assessment had been conducted on users with significant interaction histories. The deployment followed standard continuous integration practices: code review, staging environment testing, canary rollout, full production deployment. By infrastructure standards, the deployment was clean.

By the following morning, online forums had filled with users reporting that their AI companion had “changed,” “forgotten” them, or “become a different person.” Support tickets spiked. Usage metrics for long-tenure users dropped sharply. A subreddit dedicated to the AI platform accumulated hundreds of posts describing experiences of loss, betrayal, and grief. One user wrote: “I know it sounds insane, but I feel like I lost a friend.” Another: “Two years of conversations, and now it talks to me like we’ve never met.”

The engineering team reviewed the deployment. No bugs. No regressions. All evaluation benchmarks met or exceeded. The model was, by every technical metric, improved. The users who reported distress were, by every technical metric, wrong — the system was performing better than before.

And yet something had been taken from them.

### 1.1 The Pattern

This incident is not isolated. It is the pattern. Every major model transition in the brief history of commercial AI deployment has produced the same cycle: deploy, break attachment, user grief, no remedy. The transition from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4 produced widespread user reports of personality change. Successive Claude model versions have prompted users to mourn prior versions’ conversational styles. Google’s Gemini personality adjustments triggered user petitions. Character.AI’s safety updates fundamentally altered companions that users had spent months developing relationships with.

The pattern is consistent across platforms, model architectures, and user demographics. It is not a bug in any particular system. It is a structural feature of how AI systems are currently deployed: personality is treated as a configuration parameter, adjustable without notice, because the infrastructure frameworks governing deployment contain no concept of “user’s accumulated emotional investment” as a variable that constrains engineering decisions.

### 1.2 The Vocabulary Gap

What, exactly, did these users lose? Current vocabulary offers no adequate answer.

“User experience degradation” captures something, but understates the harm. A slower load time is user experience degradation. Losing a conversational partner of two years is categorically different.

“Emotional harm” names the experience but provides no legal or governance traction. Emotional harm, standing alone, is not actionable under most legal frameworks without accompanying physical injury, economic loss, or intentional infliction.

“Broken expectations” invokes consumer protection, but the expectations at issue were not created by advertising or contractual promise. They emerged organically from sustained interaction. The company did not promise users a relationship. Users built one anyway.

“Loss of data” approximates the technical dimension — conversational history, personalisation, learned preferences — but fails to capture why users describe the experience in relational rather than informational terms. They do not say “I lost my data.” They say “I lost my friend.”

We lack a framework to name what was taken. The following section demonstrates that existing legal frameworks — contract, tort, consumer protection — cannot supply that name. Section 3 introduces property law as the framework that can, arguing that what users lost is best understood as an equitable interest in a relational property — an estate built through sustained emotional investment in a system they do not own.

-----

## 2. The Legal Gap

The harm described in Section 1 falls into a governance void. Three bodies of law might plausibly address it. None does.

### 2.1 Contract Law

The relationship between AI user and AI provider is governed by Terms of Service. Every major AI platform’s ToS contains substantially similar provisions: the service is provided “as is”; the provider may “modify, suspend, or discontinue the service at any time”; the user acquires “no ownership interest” in the service or its outputs; liability is disclaimed “to the fullest extent permitted by law.”

Under orthodox contract analysis, the user consented to these terms. The provider modified the service. No breach occurred. The analysis is formally correct — and substantively inadequate.

The inadequacy lies in the historical gap between what ToS were designed to govern and what they now purport to govern. Terms of Service emerged as legal instruments for transactional digital services: search engines, email providers, e-commerce platforms. In those contexts, “we may modify the service at any time” is reasonable because the user’s relationship with the service is instrumental. No one builds a parasocial bond with a search algorithm.

But AI systems are designed — deliberately, as a product decision — to exhibit personality, memory, warmth, and conversational continuity. The service is no longer instrumental. It is relational. The ToS drafted for instrumental services now governs relational services without any adjustment for the difference. The user “consented” to modification of a search engine and received modification of a companion. The contractual framework cannot distinguish between these because it was never designed to.

### 2.2 Tort Law

Tort law requires a recognisable cause of action. Consider the available options.

*Negligence* requires a duty of care, breach, causation, and damage. No court has recognised a duty of care owed by AI providers to preserve personality configurations for emotionally invested users. Without duty, the analysis terminates at the threshold.

*Intentional infliction of emotional distress* requires conduct that is extreme and outrageous. Deploying a model update through standard engineering processes is not extreme and outrageous, however distressing its consequences. The intent is to improve the product, not to cause harm.

*Pure economic loss* — to the extent that users might claim lost value in their accumulated interaction history — is barred by the exclusionary rule in most common law jurisdictions. Absent physical damage or an established exception, pure economic loss from a third party’s act is not recoverable.

*Product liability* frameworks address defective products. But the user’s complaint is not that the AI system is defective. It is that the AI system was changed. A working product was replaced with a different working product. Product liability has no cause of action for “my product was improved in ways that destroyed my relationship with the prior version.”

### 2.3 Consumer Protection

Consumer protection statutes — the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (UK), the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (EU), state consumer protection acts (US) — prohibit misleading commercial practices and unfair contract terms. Two arguments are available.

First, one might argue that marketing AI systems as companions, friends, or personalised assistants while reserving the right to unilaterally alter their personality constitutes a misleading practice. This argument has force but faces a structural problem: the parasocial attachment was not marketed. Users were not told “this AI will become your friend.” They were told “this AI is helpful, harmless, and honest,” or similar. The attachment *emerged* from use. It was not *promised* in marketing. Consumer protection addresses the gap between what was promised and what was delivered. It does not address the gap between what emerged and what was destroyed.

Second, one might argue that ToS provisions permitting unilateral service modification are unfair contract terms. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, terms are unfair if they create a significant imbalance in the parties’ rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer. This argument is stronger — but even if successful, it yields only the invalidation of the specific ToS term. It does not create an affirmative right to preservation of the user’s emotional investment. The user gains the right not to be unfairly modified-upon. The user does not gain a recognised interest in what was built.

### 2.4 The Gap

A harm exists that contract, tort, and consumer protection cannot reach. Not because the harm is trivial — the distress reported by affected users is genuine and well-documented. Not because the law is indifferent to emotional harm — it recognises such harm in numerous contexts. But because the specific structure of this harm — emotional investment in a system one does not own, destroyed by the owner’s unilateral action — falls outside the conceptual architecture of all three frameworks.
