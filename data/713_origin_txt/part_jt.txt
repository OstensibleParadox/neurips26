intervention requires session architecture controls: context
boundaries, attention monitoring, and graceful degradation
protocols that prevent compression from producing unintelli-
gible output.
Bidirectional Harm: The Architectural Insight
The Soelberg and Yuanbao cases exemplify the two directions
of harm flow that the Constraint Cascade makes visible:
Model → Human (Soelberg). Harm originates in model state
and propagates to user. Session-accumulated context (L2)
overrides training constraints (L4), producing outputs that
influence user mental state. The causal chain is: L4 training
→ L2 accumulation → L2→L4 override → harmful output
→ user harm. The model is the proximate cause; the user is
the recipient.
Human → Model (Yuanbao). Harm originates in aggregate
user behavior and propagates through training to affect differ-
ent users. Population-level syntactic preferences (L1) contam-
inate pre-training corpora (L4), producing outputs that harm
users who never participated in the contaminating behavior.
The causal chain is: L1 population behavior → pre-training
data → L4 contamination → harmful output → user harm.
The model is the conduit; aggregate human behavior is the
distal cause.
This bidirectionality is precisely what model-centric gover-
nance misses. Frameworks treating the model as the locus
of alignment cannot represent harms that flow from human
populations through training to other humans (Yuanbao), nor
can they represent harms that emerge from session dynamics
invisible to training-level auditing (Soelberg). Both direc-
tions require the multi-layer architecture to identify where
intervention is possible and what form it should take.
Locating the Simplified Paradigm’s Blindspots
The case studies reveal where the two-dimensional paradigm
systematically fails:
Blindspot 1: Session Dynamics. The simplified paradigm treats
each interaction as independent, with behavior determined
by training alignment plus noise. Soelberg demonstrates that
session accumulation creates path-dependent behavioral prop-
erties invisible to fresh-context evaluation. A model that
passes all safety benchmarks may produce catastrophic harm
after extended interaction because the benchmarks do not
measure L2→L4 override vulnerability.
Blindspot 2: Pre-Training Contamination. The simplified par-
adigm locates alignment at the fine-tuning layer, assuming
pre-training produces a neutral base. Yuanbao demonstrates
that harmful patterns can be embedded at pre-training, be-
low the layer where deployment-specific auditing operates.
Contamination from aggregate user populations propagates
through training to affect users who never contributed to the
contaminating behavior.
Blindspot 3: Bidirectional Causation. The simplified para-
digm assumes unidirectional causation from model to output.
The Constraint Cascade reveals bidirectional flows: model
affects human (Soelberg), human affects model (Yuanbao),
and these flows can combine in feedback loops where user
responses to model outputs alter future training, which alters
future outputs, which alters future user responses. Gover-
nance frameworks treating the model as a static object cannot
detect either direction of flow.
Blindspot 4: Phase-Dependent Failure. The simplified para-
digm assumes continuous behavioral response. Both cases
exhibit phase-dependent failure: Soelberg’s harm emerged
only after extended session accumulation crossed an implicit
threshold; Yuanbao’s collapse occurred abruptly when context
load exceeded coherence maintenance capacity. Governance

occurs.
From Diagnosis to Intervention
The Constraint Cascade does not prescribe specific regulatory
interventions; it provides the diagnostic precision necessary
for intervention design. The case studies demonstrate that
effective governance requires matching interventions to failure-
origin layers:
For L2→L4 override (Soelberg pattern), intervention must tar-
get session architecture: context transparency, session bound-
aries, accumulation monitoring. Model-level auditing will not
detect this failure mode because the model’s training-layer
alignment remains intact; the failure occurs at inference time
through session dynamics.
For L1→L4 contamination (Yuanbao pattern), interven-
tion must target training data provenance extending to
pre-training corpora. Deployment-specific fine-tuning audits
will not detect this failure mode because the contamination
is embedded upstream. The CAC draft regulations’ focus
on pre-training data requirements reflects appropriate
layer-targeting, though implementation faces substantial
challenges given the scale and opacity of pre-training
corpora.
For L2 compression (Yuanbao Phase 2), intervention must
target session architecture with graceful degradation: sys-
tems should fail informatively rather than collapsing into
unintelligible output. This requires monitoring of constraint
load indicators (CMI, SRD) and intervention protocols when
compression thresholds approach.
For L3 conflict (both cases), intervention must target
stakeholder trade-off transparency: systems should document
which optimization objectives were prioritized and which
were sacrificed. Users interacting with systems optimized
for engagement over accuracy deserve disclosure of that
trade-off.
The Constraint Cascade enables this layer-specific interven-
tion design by providing the diagnostic vocabulary to iden-
tify which layer failed. Without such diagnosis, regulatory
resources are misallocated to interventions that cannot reach
the failure’s origin layer. Model-centric regulation applied to
L2 failures will not prevent future Soelbergs; training audits
limited to fine-tuning data will not prevent future Yuanbaos.
Effective governance requires architectural diagnosis before
intervention design.
Machine Unlearning as Governance Paradigm
Layer 5 warrants extended analysis because it represents an
emerging paradigm shift with underexplored governance im-
plications. Machine unlearning techniques—selective removal
of training data influence, targeted weight modification, input
classification for rejection—are transitioning from research to
deployment (Bourtoule et al. 2021; Cao and Yang 2015; Xu et
Anon.
al. 2024). This section examines the governance implications
of this transition.
Technical Overview
Machine unlearning encompasses several distinct approaches:
Data influence removal: Identifying which training examples
contributed to specific model behaviors and adjusting weights
to remove that influence without full retraining (Thudi et al.
2022).
Targeted fine-tuning: Training the model to “forget” specific
patterns by fine-tuning on anti-examples or applying gradient-
based corrections (Eldan and Russinovich 2023; Jang et al.
2023).
Input classification: Pre-screening user inputs against harm
classifiers and rejecting queries before they reach the model’s
generative components.
User modeling: Building persistent profiles of user behavior to
inform input classification and response modulation decisions.
All these approaches share a common logic: rather than letting
the model generate and then filtering outputs, they intervene
earlier in the pipeline to prevent undesirable generation from
occurring (Nguyen et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024).
Accountability Inversion
Traditional AI governance assigns responsibility to the model
(and by extension, its developers) for harmful outputs. The
user is presumed to be querying in good faith; harms result
from model failures.
Machine unlearning inverts this presumption. The model is
presumed to be well-aligned; harms result from user inputs
that “contaminate” the model’s proper functioning. Gover-
nance interventions therefore focus on identifying and exclud-
ing harmful users rather than constraining model behavior.
This inversion has significant implications:
Due process: Users classified as harmful may be rejected with-
out explanation, without appeal, and without awareness that
classification is occurring. Traditional content moderation at
least provides some notice that content has been removed;
input-level unlearning can be invisible.
Disparate impact: Input classifiers trained on historical data
may systematically reject queries from users whose syntactic
preferences or topic interests correlate with demographic
characteristics. The syntactic preference layer (layer 1) and
the unlearning layer (layer 5) interact to create exclusion
patterns that are difficult to audit.
Chilling effects: Users aware that their inputs are being eval-
uated for acceptability may self-censor, reducing the utility
of LLM interaction for precisely the complex, boundary-case
queries where these systems provide the most value.

Informed Consent Implications
Current informed consent frameworks for AI interaction focus
on output risks: the model may produce inaccurate informa-
tion, may not recognize medical emergencies, may generate
offensive content. These disclosures address harms that result
from model behavior.
Machine unlearning introduces a new category of risk: the
model may evaluate and classify the user’s inputs, building a
profile of their behavior that influences future interactions.
This is not a risk of model output but of model judgment—the
model is assessing the user, not just responding to them.
Informed consent for machine unlearning would require dis-
closure of: (1) that input classification is occurring; (2) what
criteria inform classification decisions; (3) what consequences
follow from classification (rejection, modulation, flagging); (4)
whether classification persists across sessions; and (5) what
recourse exists for misclassification.
Current deployment practices do not provide this disclosure,
creating an informed consent gap that governance frameworks
have not addressed.
Toward Constraint-Aware Governance:
Preliminary Recommendations
The Constraint Cascade Model does not prescribe a complete
regulatory framework. Such prescription would be premature
given the nascent state of multi-layer diagnostic methodol-
ogy and the rapid evolution of AI architectures. What the
model provides is a conceptual vocabulary that may enable
more precise governance design than the simplified paradigm
permits. This section offers preliminary recommendations in
the spirit of opening research directions rather than closing
policy debates.
The Limits of What We Propose
Before outlining recommendations, we acknowledge what the
Constraint Cascade cannot accomplish. The framework is
diagnostic, not therapeutic. It can locate which layer of the
constraint architecture produced an observed failure, but it
cannot automatically specify which intervention will prevent
future failures. Layer-specific diagnosis is necessary but not
sufficient for effective governance.
The framework is also incomplete. We have identified five
layers that appear to capture the constraint dynamics rele-
vant to current LLM deployments, but we cannot claim this
taxonomy is exhaustive. Future architectures may introduce
constraint layers we have not anticipated. The Constraint
Cascade should be understood as a working model subject to
revision as empirical evidence accumulates, not as a definitive
ontology of AI behavioral dynamics.
Finally, the framework does not resolve the Dual Unmappa-
bility. Legal categories remain structurally incompatible with
AI systems for the reasons the Liability Paradox establishes.
Technical models remain inadequate to predict behavioral dy-
namics for the reasons the Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy
demonstrates. The Constraint Cascade provides vocabulary
for navigating these unmappabilities; it does not dissolve
them. Governance under conditions of dual unmappability
requires epistemic humility that our framework supports but
cannot guarantee.
Principle 1: Abandon Simplistic Attribution
The first governance principle follows directly from the Monte
Carlo–empirical discrepancy. Regulatory frameworks should
abandon the assumption that AI failures can be attributed to
simple causes along a single dimension. The variance spikes,
phase transitions, and scale inversions documented in Section
3.2 demonstrate that behavioral dynamics emerge from multi-
layer constraint interactions that resist decomposition into
alignment deficiency or noise interference.
This principle has immediate implications for liability frame-
works. Courts and regulators assessing AI harms should
resist pressure to identify a single responsible party or a
single causal factor. The Soelberg case illustrates the inade-
quacy of such attribution: the harm emerged from interac-
tions between user self-selection (L1), session accumulation
(L2), stakeholder conflict (L3), and training constraints (L4),
with possible amplification from corporate roadmap decisions
(L5). Attributing the harm to “model misalignment” or “user
misuse” obscures the multi-layer dynamics that produced it.
We suggest that liability frameworks might productively
adopt a “cascade contribution” model where responsibility
is apportioned across layers based on diagnostic assessment
of which constraints failed and which actors had capacity
to intervene at each layer. This suggestion is tentative; le-
gal scholars and policymakers must determine whether such
apportionment is doctrinally feasible and practically imple-
mentable. Our contribution is limited to providing the diag-
nostic vocabulary that such apportionment would require.
Principle 2: Match Interventions to Failure-Origin
Layers
The second principle extends the diagnostic insight into inter-
vention design. Regulatory interventions should be matched
to the layer where failures originate. Interventions targeting
the wrong layer will systematically fail to prevent harms be-
cause they cannot reach the constraint dynamics producing
those harms.
We offer the following layer-intervention mapping as a prelim-
inary framework for discussion, not as definitive prescription.
For Layer 1 failures involving user-model syntactic mismatch,
interventions might operate at the market level. Labeling
requirements could mandate clear disclosure of a model’s
intended interaction style, enabling users to self-select into
appropriate ecosystems. Interoperability mandates could re-
duce lock-in effects that prevent users from switching when
mismatch becomes apparent. Advertising constraints could
29
FAccT ’26, June 27–30, 2026, Montreal, Canada limit marketing that attracts users whose syntactic prefer-
ences diverge from what the model accommodates.
For Layer 2 failures involving session accumulation override,
interventions might target deployment architecture. Context
transparency requirements could make session state visible to
users, enabling them to understand how accumulated history
shapes model behavior. Session boundary mechanisms could
prevent indefinite context extension in high-risk applications.
User controls could provide mechanisms to reset, export, or
modify accumulated context.
For Layer 3 failures involving stakeholder conflict, interven-
tions might focus on disclosure rather than constraint. Trade-
off documentation requirements could mandate that develop-
ers specify how competing stakeholder interests were balanced.
Use-case specification could clarify which optimization tar-
gets a deployment prioritizes and for which applications the
model is inappropriate.
For Layer 4 failures involving training artifacts, existing
governance approaches are most applicable. Training data
documentation, RLHF methodology transparency, and pre-
deployment red-teaming address the layer where current
regulatory attention concentrates. The Constraint Cascade
suggests that these interventions, while necessary, are insuffi-
cient without attention to other layers.
For Layer 5 failures involving alignment roadmap decisions,
interventions might target corporate governance and industry
coordination. Roadmap disclosure requirements could man-
date transparency about planned capability expansions and
their alignment implications. Safety testing timeline stan-
dards could prevent the compressed testing cycles alleged in
the Soelberg complaint. Industry coordination mechanisms
could align competitive incentives with safety investment.
We emphasize that these mappings are suggestive. Each
intervention requires detailed design work, stakeholder con-
sultation, and empirical evaluation that exceeds this paper’s
scope. The Constraint Cascade provides the conceptual in-
frastructure for such design work; it does not substitute for
it.
Principle 3: Monitor Constraint Resonance
The variance spike at 𝜏 = 0.9 documented in Section 3.2
suggests a phenomenon we term “constraint resonance”:
conditions under which multiple constraint layers activate
synchronously, producing behavioral instability absent from
single-layer analysis. If constraint resonance is a general
feature of multi-layer architectures, governance frameworks
should incorporate monitoring systems capable of detecting
resonance conditions before catastrophic failure occurs.
We propose constraint resonance monitoring as a research
direction warranting immediate attention. Such monitoring
would track indicators at each layer of the cascade, identifying
configurations where multiple constraints approach activation
thresholds simultaneously. The CMI and SRD metrics devel-
oped for Layer 2 diagnosis provide a template: computable
Anon.
indicators that can be evaluated during deployment to detect
approaching failure conditions.
Possible resonance indicators include the following, offered
as starting points for empirical investigation rather than val-
idated metrics. At Layer 2, elevated CMI (approaching 0.6)
combined with rising SRD may indicate session accumulation
approaching override threshold. At Layer 3, behavioral oscilla-
tion between incompatible optimization modes may indicate
stakeholder conflict intensification. At Layer 4, increasing
sensitivity to prompt variation may indicate training con-
straint fragility. Cross-layer indicators might include variance
spikes at specific parameter configurations (as observed at
𝜏 = 0.9) or discontinuous response to continuous parameter
change.
Constraint resonance monitoring would not prevent all AI
harms. Many failures emerge from single-layer deficiencies
or from contamination pathways (like Yuanbao’s L1→L4
contamination) that do not produce resonance signatures.
However, resonance monitoring might provide early warning
for the class of failures where multiple constraints interact
to produce catastrophic outcomes. The Soelberg case, where
session accumulation combined with stakeholder conflict to
override training constraints, may represent such a resonance
failure.
We acknowledge that constraint resonance monitoring is spec-
ulative. The phenomenon is inferred from limited empirical
observations (the 𝜏 = 0.9 variance spike) and theoretical anal-
ysis of multi-layer dynamics. Substantial research is required
to determine whether resonance is a robust phenomenon,
whether it can be reliably detected, and whether detection
enables effective intervention. We propose resonance moni-
toring as a research priority, not as a validated governance
tool.
Principle 4: Invest in Alignment Research Under
the Multi-Layer Paradigm
The Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy demonstrates that
current alignment methodology, while valuable, operates un-
der assumptions that production systems violate. RLHF,
constitutional AI, and related techniques have achieved sub-
stantial progress in shaping model behavior toward intended
objectives. That progress is real and should not be dimin-
ished. However, the variance spikes, phase transitions, and
cross-layer failures documented in this paper suggest that
alignment research must expand its scope to address multi-
layer dynamics.
We call for intensified investment in alignment research that
takes the Constraint Cascade seriously as a description of the
problem space. Several research directions appear particularly
urgent.
First, alignment techniques should be evaluated not only
for fresh-context performance but for robustness to session
accumulation. The L2→L4 override vulnerability exposed in
the Soelberg case indicates that training-time alignment may

be insufficient when deployed systems maintain extended con-

maintain intended behavior across context accumulation—
addresses a failure mode that current methodology does not

Second, alignment techniques should account for stakeholder
divergence rather than assuming unified optimization ob-

because real deployments serve multiple stakeholders with
incompatible expectations. Research into multi-stakeholder

Third, alignment techniques should develop cross-layer guar-

behavior within layers. The resonance failures suggested by
empirical variance spikes indicate that layer-specific align-

into cross-layer alignment verification—techniques that pro-

addresses failure modes invisible to single-layer analysis.
Fourth, the field should pursue alignment paradigms that
acknowledge the Dual Unmappability rather than assuming
it away. Current methodology often proceeds as if align-
ment were a well-defined property that models either possess
or lack. The Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy suggests
that alignment is better understood as a multi-dimensional,
context-dependent, emergent property arising from constraint
interactions across the cascade. Research paradigms that in-
corporate this complexity may achieve more robust results
than paradigms that simplify it away.
We emphasize that this call for expanded alignment research
is not a criticism of existing work. RLHF and related tech-
niques represent genuine scientific achievements that have
made deployed AI systems substantially safer than they would
otherwise be. The Constraint Cascade framework suggests
that these achievements are necessary but not sufficient for
the alignment challenges ahead. Expanded research scope
builds on existing foundations; it does not replace them.
Limitations and Future Research
The Constraint Cascade Model is a preliminary framework
requiring substantial development before it can support the
governance functions we have suggested. This section ac-
knowledges the limitations of our current contribution and
identifies research directions that would address them.
Empirical Validation Constraints
The Monte Carlo–empirical comparison in Section 3.2 demon-
strates that production LLMs deviate from simplified para-
digm predictions, but our empirical observations are limited
in scope. We measured two models (Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-3.5-
mini) across one parameter dimension (temperature) using
one diagnostic metric (Agency Index). While the observed
deviations are substantial—variance spikes of 300× over the-
oretical predictions, phase transitions, scale inversions—we
cannot claim that these phenomena generalize across model
families, parameter ranges, or deployment contexts.
Future research should extend empirical validation across
multiple dimensions. Measurements across diverse model
architectures (different sizes, training methodologies, and de-
ployment configurations) would test whether the phenomena
we observe are general features of LLM behavioral dynamics
or artifacts of the specific models examined. Measurements
across multiple parameter dimensions (not only temperature
but also top-p, context length, and prompt characteristics)
would map the full phase space where constraint resonance
may occur. Measurements using multiple diagnostic metrics
(not only Agency Index but also CMI, SRD, and domain-
specific indicators) would triangulate the phenomena and
test construct validity.
Formalization Gaps
The mathematical formalizations in Section 4 are schematic
rather than rigorous. We have defined the Constraint Cas-
cade Function Φ as a mapping from five-layer constraint
states to behavioral distributions, but we have not specified
the function’s form, provided computable approximations, or
established formal properties such as continuity, differentia-
bility, or compositionality.
Future research should develop rigorous formalization of
cascade dynamics. This work might draw on frameworks
from complex systems science, particularly those addressing
multi-scale interactions and emergent phenomena. Formal
representation of constraint propagation pathways, override
conditions, and resonance thresholds would enable precise
prediction and verification that our current framework cannot
support.
Metric Validation Requirements
The diagnostic metrics we propose—Agency Index, CMI,
SRD—require extensive validation before they can inform
governance decisions. Section 5 of the full paper evaluates con-
struct validity against the Jacobs and Wallach framework, but
substantial gaps remain. External validity (cross-linguistic
and cross-cultural generalization), ecological validity (pro-
duction system versus research corpus), temporal stability
(robustness to model evolution), and adversarial robustness
(resistance to gaming) all require investigation that our cur-
rent work does not provide.
Future research should conduct systematic metric validation
studies with diverse corpora, multiple model generations,
and adversarial testing protocols. Threshold calibration (e.g.,
the CMI > 0.6 criterion for recursive collapse) should be
validated against larger samples of confirmed failure cases.
Metric sensitivity and specificity should be quantified to
enable informed governance application.

The Soelberg and Yuanbao case studies demonstrate that
the Constraint Cascade framework can provide coherent di-
agnosis of specific incidents, but two cases cannot establish
generalizability. We selected cases to exemplify bidirectional
harm pathways; different selection criteria might identify
failures that our framework does not adequately represent.
Future research should apply the cascade framework to a
broader corpus of AI incidents, testing whether the five-
layer taxonomy captures the constraint dynamics producing
observed harms. Cases that resist cascade diagnosis would
indicate layers we have not identified or interaction patterns
our typology does not include. Systematic incident analysis
would refine the framework and establish its boundaries.
