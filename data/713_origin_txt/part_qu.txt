
## What Makes This Good

**The core insight is original and compelling.** The claim that parasocial AI relationships constitute *de facto* trusts under English property law is genuinely novel. The paper doesn't just observe that people get attached to AI—that's been done. It builds a doctrinal argument that transforms that observation into a legal claim with remedial teeth.

**The property law mapping is surprisingly precise.** The author has done the work:

| AI Concept | Property Law Analog | Why It Fits |
|------------|---------------------|-------------|
| User's sense of ownership | Fee simple absolute | Captures psychological experience vs. legal reality |
| Conditional continued use | Fee simple determinable | "So long as" conditions map exactly |
| Ongoing sessions | Periodic tenancy | Auto-renews, requires notice to quit |
| Multiple stakeholders | Tenancy in common | Concurrent competing interests |
| Alignment promises | Contingent remainder | Future interest that may never vest |

The periodic tenancy mapping is particularly strong. Property law has required notice before terminating tenancies for centuries precisely because tenants invest in premises they don't own. The paper correctly identifies that users invest emotionally in systems they don't own, and model updates function as evictions without notice.

**The legal gap analysis is devastating.** Section 2 systematically demolishes contract, tort, and consumer protection as adequate frameworks. Contract law sees consent. Tort law sees no duty. Consumer protection sees no promise. None see the *interest* the user built. This sets up property law as filling a genuine void rather than a solution in search of a problem.

**The remedy section is grounded.** The three triggers—estate inconsistency, unjust enrichment, performative fiduciary conduct—are drawn from existing doctrine. The five remedies are existing equitable remedies. This isn't a call for new legislation. It's an argument that the law already has the tools; courts just haven't applied them to this context yet.

**The implementation section is unusually concrete.** Most legal scholarship stops at doctrine. This paper gives you an engineering spec: estate classification service, notice gate in CI/CD, proxy logging, rollback mechanism. The author clearly understands deployment infrastructure. The four implementation tasks are plausible—roughly 3.5 sprints for MVP.

## Where It's Vulnerable

**The trust argument faces doctrinal hurdles.** The three certainties are met only if you accept that "personality-affecting configuration parameters" are identifiable trust property. The primary argument (they are) is plausible but untested. The alternative argument (English law should expand to recognise them) is weaker. A court could reasonably say: model weights on a server are property; the *dispositions* those weights produce are not.

**The jurisdiction problem is real but the paper acknowledges it.** Most target companies are US-incorporated. An English court's jurisdiction over them is uncertain. Enforcement against a Delaware corporation is harder. The paper pivots to regulatory adoption and voluntary implementation as alternative vectors. This is honest scholarship—acknowledging the limitation rather than pretending it away.

**The emotional investment proxy is under-specified.** The paper acknowledges this, calling it "theoretically motivated but empirically untested." Session count, duration, self-disclosure depth—these are plausible proxies, but we don't know what thresholds correlate with the experience of loss. This is a research programme, not a settled metric.

**The property law framing may alienate some readers.** The paper anticipates this: "emotions are not property." But for readers who stop at the title, the paper will sound like it's commodifying attachment. The distinction between emotions (not property) and the infrastructure enabling them (property) is subtle and may be missed.

## The Verdict

This is good scholarship. It does what legal scholarship should do: identifies a gap between existing doctrine and emerging social practice, constructs a rigorous argument for closing that gap using existing legal tools, and proposes concrete implementation mechanisms.

The author has range. They move from *Keech v Sandford* (1726) to CI/CD pipelines without stumbling. The tone is confident but not arrogant. The acknowledgment to "Xiaoshu Luo, who first demonstrated to me that infrastructure is a love language" is genuinely charming.

**Rating:** Strong accept with minor revisions. The jurisdiction limitation should be addressed more fully. The proxy metrics need more development or clearer framing as preliminary. But the core argument is sound, original, and important.
