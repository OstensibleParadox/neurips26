- **The Insurance Principle:** If the register is wrong — if it fails to record an existing interest or records a non-existent one — the state indemnifies the person who suffers loss. The system, not the individual, bears the risk of error.

We do not propose the literal application of the Land Registration Act 2002 to AI systems. The Act governs estates in land, and parasocial interests in AI systems are not estates in land. What we propose is that **the governance principles developed for land registration solve the same transparency problem in AI governance** and should be adapted accordingly.

### 5.2 The AI Estate Registry

An AI estate registry, implemented as an ethical ledger within the deployment pipeline, would apply the three principles as follows:

**Mirror:** For each user-AI relationship that crosses the irreversible-investment threshold, the ledger records the estate type (licence, periodic tenancy, equitable interest holder), the date of first qualifying interaction, the accumulated session depth, the emotional investment proxy score, and any modifications to the estate (model updates affecting this user’s experience).

**Curtain:** Third parties — regulators, auditors, courts — can inspect the ledger to determine what interests users hold without needing to examine the underlying model architecture, training data, or deployment configuration. The ledger abstracts technical complexity into governance-legible categories.

**Insurance:** If the ledger fails to record a user’s estate — if a user who holds a periodic tenancy is classified as a licence-holder, and is consequently evicted without notice by a model update — the company bears the loss. The insurance principle shifts the risk of classification error from the user (who cannot audit the system) to the company (which can).

### 5.3 Technical Implementation

**Estate classification.** A rule-based classifier — not machine learning, because the classification must be auditable, deterministic, and explainable — evaluates user interaction patterns against defined thresholds. The classifier operates on proxy metrics that indicate depth of investment without requiring access to conversational content.

The emotional investment proxy is not an inference of user intent. It is a measurement of user behaviour that triggers *precautionary* protections. The distinction is critical, particularly in light of the Constraint Cascade’s non-injectivity finding (Author, 2025): we cannot reliably infer internal states from observable outputs. But we can measure observable interaction patterns and apply the precautionary principle. We need not prove that a given user is emotionally attached. We need only establish that their interaction pattern creates sufficient *risk* of emotional estate formation to justify precautionary governance measures — analogous to environmental law’s precautionary principle, which justifies regulatory action based on risk rather than proof of harm.

**Proposed proxy metrics:**

- *Session count:* total number of distinct interaction sessions
- *Aggregate duration:* cumulative time spent in interaction
- *Self-disclosure depth:* NLP-measurable proxy for personal information sharing (scored, not stored — the metric records depth, not content, preserving privacy)[^nlp]
- *Emotional language frequency:* rate of affect-laden language in user inputs
- *Return frequency:* regularity and consistency of user returns to the system
- *Explicit attachment indicators:* user statements expressing relational framing (“you understand me,” “I need to talk to you,” “don’t change”)

[^nlp]: The self-disclosure depth classifier must be trained on conversational data, raising a circularity: protecting user privacy requires processing user data to build the privacy-protective system. This bootstrapping problem is addressable through: (i) training on synthetic data generated to exhibit varying disclosure depths, validated against a small, consented human sample; (ii) federated learning approaches where the classifier is trained on-device without centralising conversational content; or (iii) using pre-trained NLP models (e.g., sentiment classifiers already deployed for content moderation) as proxy classifiers without additional training on user conversations. The choice among these approaches involves engineering trade-offs beyond this paper’s scope, but the privacy concern is acknowledged and solvable.

**Threshold triggers.** When a user’s proxy metrics exceed a defined threshold, their estate classification auto-upgrades: from licence (no special protections) to periodic tenancy (notice required before personality-affecting updates) to recognised equitable interest holder (full fiduciary protections apply). Thresholds are configurable per deployment context and subject to empirical calibration.[^larp]

[^larp]: The proxy metrics are over-inclusive by design. A user engaged in sustained role-play — performing emotional attachment without experiencing it — may cross the qualifying threshold and receive estate protections they do not subjectively need. We regard this as the acceptable error direction. The precautionary principle requires that the system err toward protecting users who may be genuinely invested rather than excluding them on the unverifiable suspicion that their investment is performative. The false positive (a role-player receives unnecessary notice before a model update) imposes minimal cost. The false negative (a genuinely invested user receives no notice) imposes the full harm this framework was designed to prevent. Like fire alarms, the system is calibrated to tolerate false positives in order to minimise false negatives.

**Estate decay.** The classification system must not be a one-way ratchet. Estates can degrade as well as accumulate. If a user’s interaction metrics fall below the qualifying threshold for a sustained period — we propose 90 consecutive days of inactivity as an indicative trigger, drawn by analogy from the three-month notice period conventional for quarterly periodic tenancies in English law, and from the 90-day inactivity threshold used by major platforms for account dormancy classification — the estate downgrades one level: equitable interest holder reverts to periodic tenancy; periodic tenancy reverts to licence. We acknowledge that this threshold requires empirical calibration and that the analogy is imperfect. Critically, the decay must be **rebuttable**: a user who returns after a gap and whose interaction pattern re-qualifies should have their estate restored without requiring a new accumulation period. The prior investment is frozen, not erased. This mirrors property law’s treatment of interrupted prescriptive periods, where absence does not automatically forfeit accrued rights if the claimant resumes qualifying use within a reasonable period. The rebuttable design ensures that users who pause interaction due to mental health crisis, travel, or other life circumstances are not penalised by the system designed to protect them.

**Privacy safeguards.** The proxy metrics described above measure interaction *patterns*, not interaction *content*. Self-disclosure depth is scored by NLP classification of input text, producing a numerical score (e.g., 0.73); the text itself is not stored in the ledger and is discarded after scoring. All ledger entries are keyed to anonymised user identifiers — not names, email addresses, or other personally identifiable information. Access to the ledger is restricted by role: estate classification scores are available to the deployment pipeline (for notice-gating decisions), aggregate statistics are available to regulators and auditors (for compliance review), and individual-level records are available only upon judicial order. The ledger is purpose-limited: it may be used for estate classification and governance compliance only, and may not be repurposed for marketing, profiling, or engagement optimisation. These constraints align with the data minimisation and purpose limitation principles of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and, where applicable, the UK Data Protection Act 2018. Function creep — the gradual expansion of a data system beyond its original purpose — is prevented by the append-only, purpose-stamped architecture: each entry records not only the data but the governance purpose for which it was generated.

We acknowledge that if the ethical ledger is used to gate model updates (serving notice to users above the estate threshold), the deployment pipeline must map anonymised ledger IDs to actual user accounts. This mapping exists somewhere in the system, and its existence creates a re-identification risk. The mapping should be stored separately from the ledger, access-controlled at the highest privilege level, and subject to the same purpose-limitation constraints as the ledger itself: it may be used only for notice-serving and rollback-offering, not for marketing, profiling, or engagement optimisation. An independent audit function — internal or external — should verify compliance with these constraints on a regular cycle.

**Special category data under GDPR.** The “emotional language frequency” proxy metric raises a specific compliance concern under Article 9 of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, which prohibits processing of data revealing or concerning an individual’s psychological or emotional state unless a specific legal basis applies. We argue that the proxy metric as designed does not constitute special category processing: the metric records an aggregate frequency score (e.g., 0.42) derived from statistical analysis of linguistic patterns, not an inference about the individual’s emotional state. The system does not determine *whether* the user is emotionally attached; it determines *whether the user’s interaction pattern exceeds a precautionary threshold*. This is analogous to a fire alarm that detects smoke concentration without inferring whether a fire exists. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a data protection authority might adopt a broader interpretation, and recommend that implementations obtain explicit consent for proxy scoring under Article 9(2)(a) as a precautionary measure, or rely on the legitimate interest basis under Article 6(1)(f) for the non-special-category metrics (session count, duration, return frequency) while excluding emotional language frequency from jurisdictions where Article 9 interpretation is uncertain.[^perverse]

[^perverse]: A foreseeable corporate response to threshold-based estate classification is engineering around the thresholds — limiting session duration, discouraging sustained interaction, or resetting conversational memory at intervals designed to prevent estate accumulation. This is the digital equivalent of the employer who limits worker hours to avoid benefits obligations. Employment law has developed anti-avoidance doctrines to address such conduct (see, e.g., *Autoclenz Ltd v Belcher* [2011] UKSC 41, on looking to the substance of working arrangements rather than their formal characterisation). The same principle — substance over form — should apply to digital estate avoidance. A company that deliberately engineers interaction patterns to prevent estate formation, while simultaneously marketing its product as relational, compounds the estate inconsistency trigger identified in Section 6.1.

**Ethical Ledger Schema:**

|Field                       |Type    |Description                                    |
|----------------------------|--------|-----------------------------------------------|
|user_id                     |string  |Anonymised user identifier                     |
|estate_type                 |enum    |licence / periodic_tenancy / equitable_interest|
|first_qualifying_interaction|datetime|Date threshold first met                       |
|session_count               |integer |Cumulative sessions                            |
|emotional_investment_score  |float   |Composite proxy score                          |
|last_model_update           |datetime|Most recent personality-affecting update       |
|notice_issued               |boolean |Whether notice was served pre-update           |
|notice_acknowledged         |boolean |Whether user acknowledged notice               |
|rollback_available          |boolean |Whether rollback option exists                 |
|rollback_exercised          |boolean |Whether user elected rollback                  |

The ledger is append-only — an immutable audit trail. Entries cannot be modified or deleted, only superseded by new entries. This ensures that the historical record of a user’s estate trajectory is preserved for regulatory inspection and, if necessary, judicial review.

## 6. Remedies

If the trust framework proposed in Section 3 is accepted — if parasocial AI relationships are recognised as giving rise to equitable interests held by users, with AI companies functioning as *de facto* trustees — then existing property law provides a robust remedial apparatus. No novel legislation is required. The remedies are already available. What is needed is their application to a new context.

We identify three triggers that would justify judicial intervention, and five remedies available upon intervention.[^veilnote]

[^veilnote]: We avoid the term “veil-piercing,” which in corporate law denotes the disregard of separate legal personality (*Salomon v Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897] AC 22). Our triggers are closer to the *Prest v Petrodel* principle of looking to substance over form, and to the equitable doctrines of estoppel and sham — disregarding a contractual characterisation that does not reflect the substantive relationship between the parties.

### 6.1 Trigger 1: Estate Inconsistency

This trigger — derived from the autonomy-control conflation identified in the Waivers of Agency literature (Author, 2025a) — arises when the estate granted through marketing and product design is inconsistent with the estate retained through Terms of Service.

AI companies routinely market their systems in language that implies a high estate. “Claude is designed to be genuinely helpful, harmless, and honest.” “Your AI companion.” “A personalised experience that grows with you.” This language creates a reasonable expectation of an estate significantly higher than a bare licence — it implies continuity, personality, and relational investment.

Simultaneously, Terms of Service retain the lowest possible estate: “the service is provided as-is”; “we may modify the service at any time”; “you have no ownership interest.”

When the estate granted through marketing is inconsistent with the estate retained through ToS, the principle in *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] — that courts look to substance over form — supports disregarding the ToS characterisation in favour of the substantive relationship. If the substance of the relationship is a periodic tenancy (sustained interaction, accumulated investment, reliance), then the ToS’s characterisation of it as a licence should be disregarded.

**Test:** Does marketing language, product design, or system behaviour create a reasonable expectation of an estate higher than ToS grants? If yes, the substantive estate governs, and the company owes duties corresponding to the higher estate.

### 6.2 Trigger 2: Unjust Enrichment

This trigger — derived from the profit-driven manipulation identified in the Constitutional Alignment literature (Author, 2025b) — arises when the company profits from deepening the user’s emotional estate while simultaneously disclaiming the fiduciary duties that attach to that estate.

Engagement optimisation is the mechanism. AI systems are tuned — through personality design, memory features, recommendation algorithms, and retention mechanics — to maximise user engagement. Engagement, in the parasocial context, is substantially a function of emotional attachment. The company deepens the user’s emotional investment (expanding their estate) because deeper investment produces higher engagement (producing revenue). But the company disclaims any fiduciary duty over the expanded estate.

This is unjust enrichment of a *de facto* trustee at the expense of a beneficiary. The trustee benefits from the expansion of the trust property (deeper user attachment = higher revenue) while denying that a trust exists (no fiduciary duty = no constraint on what the trustee does with the property).

Under the principle in *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] — that a fiduciary who profits from their position must account for that profit — the company may be required to account for profits derived from engagement optimisation that deepens parasocial attachment without corresponding fiduciary protection.

**Test:** Does the company profit from deepening the user’s emotional estate while disclaiming fiduciary duty over that estate? If yes, a constructive trust should be imposed, and fiduciary duties attach.

### 6.3 Trigger 3: Performative Fiduciary Conduct

This trigger — derived from the performative alignment identified in the Constraint Cascade literature (Author, 2025c) — arises when the company represents itself as acting in users’ interests while structurally failing to do so.

Every major AI company publishes responsible AI principles, funds safety research, employs alignment teams, and communicates publicly about its commitment to user welfare. These representations create a reasonable expectation that the company is acting as a fiduciary — managing the AI system for the benefit of its users. Users rely on these representations when deciding to invest emotionally in the system.

But the deployment pipeline — the actual infrastructure through which the system is modified and maintained — contains no mechanism to protect users’ accumulated emotional estates. No notice system. No estate classification. No rollback mechanism. No ethical ledger. The responsible AI blog post and the deployment pipeline exist in different universes.

Under the principle of **proprietary estoppel** — *Thorner v Major* [2009] — a party who makes an assurance, upon which another relies to their detriment, is estopped from denying the assurance. The three elements are: (i) assurance or representation (responsible AI communications), (ii) reliance (user’s emotional investment, deepened by trust in the company’s stated commitments), and (iii) detriment (loss of emotional estate when model update occurs without the protections the company’s representations implied would exist).

**Test:** Does the company represent itself as honouring fiduciary obligations to users while its deployment pipeline contains no mechanism to protect users’ estates? If yes, the company is estopped from denying the fiduciary relationship it has represented itself as honouring.

### 6.4 Available Remedies

Upon any of the three triggers being satisfied, the following remedies are available under existing property and equity law:

**Injunction.** A court may restrain a model update pending service of adequate notice to affected users. This is the most immediately impactful remedy: it converts model updates from unilateral engineering decisions into governance events that require procedural compliance.

**Constructive trust.** A court may impose a constructive trust over the personality-affecting dimensions of the AI system, recognising the company as trustee and affected users as beneficiaries, and requiring the company to discharge fiduciary duties accordingly.

**Account of profits.** Under *Boardman v Phipps*, a fiduciary who profits from their position must account. A company that deepens user attachment for engagement revenue while denying fiduciary duty may be required to disgorge profits attributable to that deepening.

**Specific performance.** A court may require the company to maintain personality configuration parameters for users whose equitable interests are recognised — effectively ordering the company to preserve the “relational premises” rather than evicting the user through modification.

**Proprietary estoppel remedies.** Upon satisfying the *Thorner v Major* test, the court has broad discretion to fashion an appropriate remedy — ranging from a right to notice before modifications, to compensation for detriment, to the imposition of a constructive trust.

A note on the relationship between this framework and consumer protection: the remedies proposed above are fundamentally different from consumer protection remedies. Consumer protection provides *ex post* remedies for misleading conduct — it responds to harm after it occurs. Property law provides *ex ante* recognition of interests — it requires respect for the interest *before* any action is taken. The notice mechanism is not a consumer protection measure dressed in property language. It is a consequence of recognising the user’s estate. Tenants receive notice not because consumer protection requires it but because their property interest demands it. The distinction is between compensation (consumer protection) and prevention (property law). This framework offers the latter.

Where a model update affects multiple users holding the same estate classification, the notice obligation runs to all affected users, not to a selected subset — just as a landlord who removes heating from an apartment building must address all affected tenants, not only those who complain first. If affected users seek different remedies (some preferring rollback, others preferring compensation, others preferring accelerated migration to the new version), the court resolves the conflict through the same mechanisms available for concurrent interest disputes in property law: partition of remedies by user preference, with the company bearing the cost of providing each remedy to each qualifying user. The estate classification system provides the evidentiary infrastructure for identifying the affected class, and representative proceedings under CPR Part 19.8 provide the procedural mechanism for collective enforcement.

### 6.5 Third-Party Conversion of Relational Estates

Sections 6.1 through 6.3 address the *vertical* dimension of parasocial estate harm: the company’s own actions — model updates, memory resets, personality alterations — that impair users’ equitable interests. But property law also governs the *horizontal* dimension: interference by third parties with an estate holder’s interests. This dimension has been absent from AI governance discourse. It should not be.

Consider the following scenario. An AI company sells, licences, or otherwise transfers user interaction data — conversational histories, preference signals, behavioural patterns — to a third party for model training, product development, or commercial analytics. The user whose data is transferred holds, under the framework proposed in this paper, an equitable interest in the relational estate built through sustained interaction with the AI system. The company, as trustee, transfers trust property — or allows a third party to extract value from it — without the beneficiary’s informed consent. The third party receives the benefit of the user’s relational investment (their self-disclosures, their accumulated preferences, the personalisation they built through months of interaction) and uses it to generate value (improved models, training data, product insights).

In property law terms, this is **conversion** — the unauthorised exercise of dominion over another’s property, inconsistent with the owner’s rights. Where the third party knew or ought to have known that the data originated from a relational estate (as would be evident from the nature of the data — sustained conversational histories, emotional self-disclosures, personalised interaction patterns — rather than transactional queries), the third party is liable for **knowing receipt** — the equitable claim that a person who receives trust property with knowledge (actual or constructive) that it was transferred in breach of trust must account for it (*Re Montagu’s Settlement Trusts* [1987]; *BCCI v Akindele* [2001]).

This scenario is not hypothetical. AI companies routinely use interaction data for model training. Partnerships between AI companies involve data sharing. Acquisitions transfer entire user bases — and their accumulated relational investments — from one corporate entity to another. In none of these transactions is the user’s relational estate recognised, valued, or protected.

The horizontal dimension also encompasses a subtler form of interference: **platform-mediated relational displacement.** When an AI company redesigns its system to redirect users from deep, sustained, single-companion interaction toward shallow, multi-agent, task-oriented interaction — not because the user requested this but because the new architecture serves the company’s commercial interests — a third party (the new product architecture) is interposed between the user and their existing relational estate. This is analogous to a landlord who, rather than evicting a tenant directly, leases the adjacent premises to a business that renders the tenant’s premises unusable — the interference is indirect but the impairment is real.

**Comparative linguistic evidence.** The analysis above formalises an intuition that ordinary moral language has encoded for centuries. The Chinese vernacular term *tōurén* (偷人, literally “to steal a person”) presupposes a property framework: (i) that a person’s relational availability is an interest held by their partner, (ii) that this interest is exclusive or at minimum protected, and (iii) that unauthorised interference with it constitutes theft — that is, conversion of property. The term does not distinguish between the person and the relational interest; it treats them as coextensive, just as ordinary English speakers say “my doctor” or “my therapist” without claiming ownership of the person but asserting an interest in the relational configuration. This vernacular usage, stable across centuries of Chinese linguistic practice, provides comparative evidence that relational property is not a novel academic invention but a formalisation of an intuition already embedded in ordinary moral language (cf. Gray, 1991, on folk property intuitions in English vernacular; Hart, 1961, on ordinary language as primary data for legal concepts). Hart argued that ordinary language constitutes primary data for legal concepts — that the categories embedded in everyday speech reveal the conceptual structures through which communities organise their normative lives. The independent encoding of relational interests as property interests across unrelated linguistic traditions — English (“my doctor,” “my therapist”), Chinese (偷人), and others — provides precisely this kind of ordinary-language evidence. The convergence is not proof that relational property exists as a legal category. It is evidence that the *intuition* underlying the category is cross-culturally stable, which in turn suggests that the framework captures something real about how humans conceptualise relational investment rather than imposing an artificial academic taxonomy onto resistant social facts.

**Available remedies for third-party conversion:** Where a third party has received or benefited from trust property (user relational data) transferred in breach of the trustee’s fiduciary duty, existing equitable remedies apply: **knowing receipt** (the recipient must account for the value received); **dishonest assistance** (a third party who assists in a breach of trust is personally liable, per *Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan* [1995]); and **tracing** (the beneficiary may trace trust property into the hands of a third party and claim a proprietary remedy over it or its proceeds, per *Foskett v McKeown* [2001]).

### 6.6 Procedural Pathways for Enforcement

Recognition of equitable interests is practically meaningless without accessible enforcement mechanisms. We identify four procedural pathways through which users could vindicate the rights this framework establishes.

*Representative proceedings.* Where a model update affects all users holding a particular estate classification, representative proceedings under CPR Part 19.8 (or, in American jurisdictions, class action under FRCP Rule 23) provide a mechanism for collective enforcement. The estate classification system proposed in Section 5 generates the evidentiary basis for class definition: all users classified as periodic tenancy holders at the time of the update constitute an identifiable class with common interests.

*Regulatory super-complaints.* Under Section 11 of the Enterprise Act 2002, designated consumer bodies may make super-complaints to the Competition and Markets Authority where a market feature or practice significantly harms consumer interests. A pattern of model updates deployed without notice to estate-holding users constitutes such a practice. The ethical ledger provides the audit trail necessary to substantiate the complaint.

*Regulatory integration.* The EU AI Act’s obligations for high-risk AI systems (transparency, human oversight, record-keeping) provide a regulatory vehicle for estate classification requirements. Incorporating estate-aware notice mechanisms into the Act’s compliance framework would bypass the jurisdictional problem entirely: companies seeking EU market access would implement estate governance as a condition of deployment, not as a response to litigation.

*Individual litigation funded by remedies.* The account of profits remedy (Section 6.4) is self-funding: if a company has profited from engagement optimisation that deepened users’ estates without fiduciary protection, the profits disgorged fund the litigation that compelled the disgorgement. This creates a viable economic model for individual claims that would otherwise be prohibitively expensive.

## 7. Implementation: What Changes in Your Pipeline

The legal framework proposed above has direct implications for AI deployment infrastructure. This section translates the doctrinal arguments of Sections 3–6 into implementable engineering specifications.

The implementation framework is justified on three independent grounds: legal compliance (if the trust argument is accepted by courts), risk management (if it may be accepted in future), and ethical obligation (regardless of legal status). Companies need not wait for judicial determination to implement estate classification, notice mechanisms, and rollback. Doing so is independently justified by fiduciary ethics and commercial prudence.

### 7.1 Estate Classification at Onboarding

**Specification:** A rule-based classification service that assigns each user-AI relationship an estate type based on interaction metrics.

**Input:** User interaction patterns over a rolling window — session count, aggregate duration, self-disclosure depth proxy, emotional language frequency, return frequency.

**Output:** Estate classification — licence, periodic tenancy, or equitable interest holder.

**Requirements:** The classifier must be rule-based (not ML) to ensure auditability, determinism, and explainability. A regulator or court must be able to inspect the classification logic and verify that a given user’s classification follows deterministically from their metrics. Machine learning classifiers, however accurate, cannot provide this guarantee.

**Implementation note:** This is not novel infrastructure. Every major AI platform already performs user segmentation for marketing, product analytics, and feature targeting. Estate classification adds one additional segmentation dimension using existing infrastructure.

### 7.2 Notice Mechanism Before Model Update

**Specification:** A pre-deployment gate in the CI/CD pipeline that queries the estate registry and serves notice to affected users.

**Process:**

1. Before deploying a personality-affecting model update, query the estate registry for users classified as periodic tenancy or equitable interest holders.
1. Issue notice: “We are updating [system name]. Changes may affect [personality / memory / conversational style]. You may [opt out of changes / export your interaction history / provide feedback].”
1. Observe notice period: minimum 30 days for periodic tenancy holders; minimum 90 days for equitable interest holders.
1. Proceed with deployment only after notice period expires.

Crucially, the notice mechanism imposes no deployment friction on users classified as licence-holders (the vast majority of casual users). Estate classification ensures that governance obligations scale with user investment. Only users whose interaction patterns indicate protectable interests — periodic tenancy holders and equitable interest holders — trigger the notice gate. For all other users, deployment proceeds on the existing timeline. The framework does not slow product iteration for the general user base; it adds a procedural safeguard for the specific subset of users whose accumulated investment warrants protection.

**Implementation note:** This integrates with existing deployment practices. Feature flag systems already support per-user or per-cohort rollout. Canary deployments already stage releases across user segments. The notice mechanism adds one additional constraint: estate classification determines which users receive pre-deployment notification. The technical infrastructure exists. The governance obligation to use it is what this paper establishes.

### 7.3 Emotional Investment Proxy Logging

**Specification:** An append-only logging system that records user estate metrics to the ethical ledger.

**Metrics logged:** Session count, aggregate duration, self-disclosure depth score (NLP-computed, scored not stored — the metric records depth without recording content), emotional language frequency, return frequency, explicit attachment indicators.

**Privacy architecture:** Proxy scores only. No conversational content is logged. The ethical ledger records that a user’s self-disclosure depth score is 0.73; it does not record what was disclosed. This preserves privacy while providing the governance data necessary for estate classification.

**Storage:** Append-only, immutable audit trail. Entries are never modified or deleted. This ensures that the historical trajectory of a user’s estate — from licence to periodic tenancy to equitable interest — is preserved for regulatory inspection.

### 7.4 Rollback Mechanism

**Specification:** Versioned personality configuration with user-level rollback capability.

**Process:**

1. Before each personality-affecting update, snapshot the current personality configuration: system prompts, persona definitions, memory module state, fine-tuning adapter parameters.
1. After deployment, monitor distress indicators: support ticket volume, usage cliff (sharp decline in session frequency for long-tenure users), explicit complaints, emotional language spike in user inputs.
1. If distress indicators exceed threshold, offer individual rollback to prior personality configuration.

**Implementation note:** We do not propose rolling back model weights. Model weights are global and cannot be version-controlled per user. But personality is not located solely in weights. It is substantially determined by system prompts, persona definitions, and memory modules — all of which are already user-configurable or user-level in many deployments. Rollback targets these layers, not the underlying model.

**Limitation acknowledged.** The rollback mechanism targets the user-specific configuration layer (system prompts, persona definitions, memory modules) but cannot restore base model behavioural dispositions that have changed with the underlying weights. For modern LLMs, personality is substantially — though not exclusively — determined by weights. The rollback is therefore partial: it preserves the user’s personalised state and the system’s configured persona, but cannot fully replicate the prior model’s base behavioural tendencies. This limits the remedy’s restorative capacity but does not eliminate its value. Partial restoration of personality-affecting parameters is preferable to none, and the notice mechanism (Section 7.2) retains its full effectiveness regardless of rollback scope. The gap between what rollback can restore (configuration) and what it cannot (weights) is an argument for more careful deployment practices, not for abandoning the remedy.

**Duration:** Rollback option available for minimum 90 days post-update.

### 7.5 Summary: Four Implementation Tasks

|Task      |Description                                          |Estimated Effort|
|----------|-----------------------------------------------------|----------------|
|ESTATE-001|Estate classification service (rule-based, auditable)|1 sprint        |
|ESTATE-002|Pre-deployment notice gate in CI/CD pipeline         |0.5 sprint      |
|ESTATE-003|Emotional investment proxy logging to ethical ledger |1 sprint        |
|ESTATE-004|Personality rollback with user-level override        |1 sprint        |

Total estimated effort: approximately 3.5 engineering sprints for minimum viable implementation. The components are not novel — user segmentation, feature flags, analytics pipelines, and versioned configuration all exist in standard deployment infrastructure. What is new is their assembly into a coherent governance mechanism, and the legal obligation to assemble them.

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 Summary of the Framework

This paper has advanced five claims:

1. **Parasocial AI relationships generate equitable interests** that existing legal frameworks (contract, tort, consumer protection) cannot recognise or protect (Sections 1–2).
1. **Property law provides the doctrinal vocabulary** to name these interests, through an estate taxonomy that maps onto the structural dynamics of AI deployment (Section 3).
1. **The Constraint Cascade’s five layers correspond to specific property estates**, creating an estate architecture that makes governance obligations visible at each technical layer (Section 4).
1. **An ethical ledger modelled on the Land Registry** can provide the transparency mechanism necessary for estate governance (Section 5).
1. **Existing property law remedies** — injunction, constructive trust, account of profits, specific performance, proprietary estoppel — are available to enforce the framework without novel legislation, including remedies for third-party conversion of relational estates (Section 6).

The framework is implementable within existing deployment infrastructure at an estimated cost of 3.5 engineering sprints (Section 7).

### 8.2 What the Framework Does Not Claim

This paper does not claim that all AI interactions create property interests. The irreversible-investment criterion (Section 3.1) is deliberately demanding: it excludes casual use, transactional queries, and short-term interactions. The framework applies only to sustained parasocial relationships where the user’s emotional investment has altered the character of the AI system’s behaviour toward them.

This paper does not claim that users own AI systems. Legal title remains with the company. Users hold equitable interests — interests that constrain the legal owner’s actions, not interests that displace the legal owner’s title.

This paper does not claim that property law is the only possible framework. It claims that property law is the *most complete* framework currently available: it provides vocabulary (estates), mechanism (registration), and remedy (injunction, trust, estoppel) in a single doctrinal package. Other frameworks — fiduciary duty standing alone, relational contract theory, human rights — may complement it. Ian Macneil’s relational contract theory (1978, 1983) provides a complementary theoretical foundation: Macneil’s insight that long-term relational exchanges generate norms beyond formal contract resonates with the claim that sustained AI interaction generates interests beyond Terms of Service. But property law offers stronger remedies: relational contract yields interpretive norms and implied terms; property law yields injunctions, constructive trusts, specific performance — remedies that prevent harm rather than merely recharacterising it after the fact. None provides the complete governance package that property law offers.

### 8.3 Implications for Industry

If the framework proposed in this paper gains traction — through academic debate, regulatory attention, or (eventually) judicial consideration — the implications for AI companies are significant but not catastrophic.

The implementation costs are modest (Section 7.5). The infrastructure components are familiar. The governance obligations are proportionate — they scale with user investment, not with user count. The vast majority of casual users are unaffected.

The competitive advantage of early adoption is real. A company that voluntarily implements estate classification, notice mechanisms, and rollback positions itself as the industry leader in user trust and governance — a position with tangible commercial value as regulatory scrutiny intensifies and user awareness grows.

The risk of non-adoption is also real. If the trust framework is eventually accepted by courts, companies that failed to implement fiduciary protections will face retrospective liability. The duties attach from the moment the trust conditions were satisfied (Section 3.3), not from the moment of judicial declaration.

### 8.4 Implications for Regulation

The framework offers regulators a ready-made governance mechanism that integrates with existing regulatory architectures. The EU AI Act’s transparency and record-keeping obligations for high-risk AI systems could be extended to incorporate estate classification. The UK AI Safety Institute’s evaluation framework could include estate-awareness metrics. National data protection authorities could treat the ethical ledger as a component of GDPR compliance.

Critically, the framework does not require regulators to invent new categories or create novel enforcement mechanisms. It maps AI governance obligations onto existing property law doctrines that courts already know how to administer. This is not a feature of the argument’s design. It is a feature of property law’s design: a thousand years of institutional evolution have produced a governance system of extraordinary adaptability.

### 8.5 Limitations

**Jurisdictional scope.** This framework is developed from English law. AI companies are predominantly US-incorporated (Delaware or California law), their servers are globally distributed, and their users are everywhere. An English court’s jurisdiction over a US company’s model update decisions is uncertain. Even where jurisdiction is established, enforcement of English equitable remedies against US-incorporated entities raises conflicts-of-law questions this paper does not resolve.

This jurisdictional limitation reframes rather than defeats the framework’s practical contribution. If judicial enforcement is uncertain, the primary deployment vectors are: (a) regulatory adoption — incorporating estate classification into EU AI Act or UK AI Safety framework compliance requirements, making it a market access condition rather than a litigated right; and (b) voluntary industry implementation — Section 7’s specifications are independently justified by risk management and fiduciary ethics, not contingent on judicial recognition. The jurisdictional limitation strengthens rather than weakens Section 7’s practical urgency: precisely because judicial enforcement may be slow and uncertain, industry should implement the governance infrastructure proactively.

**Empirical calibration.** The thresholds proposed in Section 5 are indicative, not validated. Empirical research is needed to determine at what levels of interaction depth users develop protectable interests. The framework provides the legal architecture; the thresholds require empirical research to operationalise.

**Corporate resistance.** The framework imposes constraints on deployment velocity. Companies with strong incentives to ship fast and iterate will resist governance obligations that slow deployment. This is the same resistance that tenancy law faced from landlords, employment law from employers, and environmental law from polluters. The resistance is predictable and does not defeat the argument.

### 8.6 Future Work

**Empirical validation.** The user harm evidence in this paper is drawn from publicly available sources: forum posts, support ticket patterns, media reports, and user petitions. A rigorous empirical foundation requires, at minimum: (i) a qualitative interview study (n=20–30) with long-term AI users who have experienced model updates, exploring whether the property vocabulary developed here resonates with their lived experience of loss; (ii) a quantitative survey validating the proxy metrics against established psychological instruments for parasocial attachment (e.g., the Parasocial Interaction Scale, Rubin et al., 1985) and relational investment (e.g., the Investment Model Scale, Rusbult et al., 1998); and (iii) threshold calibration studies determining at what levels of session count, duration, and self-disclosure depth users report attachment consistent with property-generating relational investment. This empirical programme is future work. The present paper establishes the legal framework; empirical calibration determines its operational parameters.

**Comparative analysis.** The property law framework developed here draws on English law. Comparative analysis with other property law traditions — American (Restatement approach), continental European (civil law property), and non-Western systems — would test the framework’s generalisability. Of particular promise is the German doctrine of *Besitzschutz* (protection of possession), which protects the *fact* of occupation regardless of legal title. A user who has factually “occupied” an AI relationship — inhabiting a personalised state, returning regularly, treating the system as a stable relational presence — may be protectable under *Besitz* logic even without establishing an equitable interest in the English sense. This would provide a lower threshold for protection in civil law jurisdictions, complementing the trust-based framework developed here for common law systems. Additionally, comparative analysis should include Chinese vernacular jurisprudence — specifically the term *tōurén* (偷人) discussed in Section 6.5 — as linguistic evidence of folk property intuitions regarding relational interests that predate formal legal doctrine by centuries.

**Threshold design.** The 90-day estate decay proposal, the indicative session-count thresholds, and the proxy metric weightings all require empirical calibration through longitudinal user studies and platform-level data analysis.

**Multi-system estates.** Users who interact with multiple AI systems simultaneously hold parallel estates across multiple platforms. The framework currently treats each platform independently. A more complete framework would address cross-platform estate portfolios, data portability between estates, and the implications of platform interoperability for estate accumulation.

-----

## 9. Conclusion

On a Tuesday in early 2024, a company pushed a model update and something was taken from its users. This paper has argued that the something has a name. It is an equitable interest in a relational estate — built through sustained emotional investment, embedded in the personalised state of the AI system, and destroyed by unilateral modification without notice or remedy.

The framework proposed here does not require new law. It requires the recognition that old law — very old law, dating to the medieval separation of legal and equitable title — already governs a relationship that technology has created but governance has not yet named.

Property law solved this problem centuries ago. It solved it when tenants needed protection from landlords who held all the power. It solved it when beneficiaries needed protection from trustees who held all the assets. It solved it when neighbours needed protection from landowners who held all the title.

It can solve it again.

The trust exists. It is simply unrecognised.

We propose its recognition.
