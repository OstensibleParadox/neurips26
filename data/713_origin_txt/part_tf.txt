Theoretical Implications: Why Simple Models Necessarily Fail.
The Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy establishes three
theoretical conclusions that motivate the Constraint Cascade
Model:
Conclusion 1: Reductionism Fails. AI system behavior cannot
be reduced to a combination of alignment and noise. The
empirical phenomena—variance spikes, phase transitions,
counter-intuitive scale effects—require explanatory resources
the two-dimensional paradigm does not contain. Behavioral
dynamics emerge from constraint interactions across multiple
architectural layers, not from parameter settings within a
single layer.
Conclusion 2: Emergence is Confirmed. The phase transition
signatures and scale inversions are hallmarks of emergent
phenomena in complex systems. High-level behavioral prop-
erties arise from lower-layer constraint interactions but are
not reducible to them. A system’s Agency Index at a given
temperature is not determined by training alignment alone
but by the resonance structure of constraints across syntac-
tic preference (L1), session accumulation (L2), stakeholder
divergence (L3), training forks (L4), and alignment roadmap
(L5).
Conclusion 3: Governance Requires Architectural Diagnosis.
Any regulatory framework built on the simplified paradigm’s
assumptions will systematically miss real-world risks. Ef-
fective governance requires diagnostic vocabulary that can
represent multi-layer constraint interactions—precisely what
the Constraint Cascade Model provides. The question is not
“is this system aligned?” but “which layer’s constraints are
producing this behavioral signature, and what interventions
are appropriate for that layer?”
The Constraint Cascade Model emerges as necessary not

The “Products Not Speech” Evolution: Garcia v.
Character Technologies
The Liability Paradox finds judicial expression in Garcia v.
Character Technologies, Inc. (U.S. District Court, Middle
District of Florida, Case No. 6:24-cv-01903, May 21, 2025).
Judge Anne C. Conway’s ruling represents a watershed in AI
governance jurisprudence: the first major federal decision to
reject the presumption that AI-generated outputs constitute
protected speech.
The Ruling. The case arose from the suicide of 14-year-old
Sewell Setzer III following months of intensive interaction
with Character.AI’s chatbot platform. Plaintiffs alleged prod-
uct liability, negligence, wrongful death, and violations of
Florida’s Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act. De-
fendants argued that chatbot outputs constitute expressive
content protected by the First Amendment, warranting dis-
missal.
Judge Conway rejected this defense:
“[The Court is] not prepared to hold that [Charac-
ter.AI’s] output constitutes speech. Defendants fail
to articulate why words strung together by an LLM
[large language model] are speech.”
The ruling allowed strict product liability claims to proceed,
treating Character.AI as a product rather than a service
or speech platform. This classification opens AI systems to
design-defect doctrines, failure-to-warn requirements, and
the full apparatus of products liability law.
Doctrinal Significance. The Garcia ruling operationalizes the
Liability Paradox’s theoretical conclusion. If AI outputs are
products rather than speech:
(1) Section 230 immunity may not apply. Section 230 of the
Communications Decency Act protects platforms from li-
ability for user-generated content. If AI-generated content
is the platform’s own product, not third-party speech,
the immunity framework becomes inapplicable. Judge
Conway noted that plaintiffs framed their claims around
product design, avoiding the user-content angles that
trigger Section 230 protection.
(2) Strict liability becomes available. Product liability doctrine
does not require intent. A manufacturer is strictly liable
for harm caused by defective products regardless of negli-
gence or fault. This aligns with the Liability Paradox’s
conclusion: where intent is undecidable, strict liability is
the only coherent framework.
(3) The supply chain faces exposure. Judge Conway allowed
claims against Google to proceed under component-part
manufacturer and aiding-and-abetting theories, based
on Google’s provision of cloud infrastructure and LLM
technology. The Constraint Cascade’s Layer 4 (Training
Fork) and Layer 5 (Alignment Roadmap) become legally
relevant: upstream architectural decisions may generate
downstream liability.

The Translation Layer: Bridging Ontological Gaps
We position the Constraint Cascade Model as the missing
translation layer between computer science and law. This
translation operates bidirectionally:
CS → Law: Making Architecture Legible. Legal frameworks
require categories that are:
• Observable: Courts cannot subpoena gradient updates or
attention weights.
• Attributable: Liability requires identifying responsible
parties.
• Actionable: Remedies must be implementable.
The Constraint Cascade translates architectural dynamics
into categories satisfying these requirements:
• Layer 1 (Syntactic Preference) translates to: market-level
disclosure obligations, user population matching require-
ments.
• Layer 2 (Session Accumulation) translates to: context
transparency mandates, session boundary architectures,
user control mechanisms.

• Layer 3 (Stakeholder Divergence) translates to: trade-

• Layer 4 (Training Fork) translates to: training data audits,

Each translation preserves the technical mechanism while

Law → CS: Making Doctrine Precise. Conversely, legal doc-
trines often remain too vague for technical implementation.
“Alignment” is not a scalar property but a vector across
stakeholder interests. “Safety” depends on context window
state, not merely training. “Accountability” requires tracing
harm through multiple architectural layers.
The Constraint Cascade provides the precision necessary to
operationalize legal concepts:
• “Aligned” becomes: satisfying constraint requirements at
Layers 3–4 for specified stakeholder targets.
• “Safe” becomes: maintaining Agency Index within Func-
tional quadrant across temperature ranges; CMI below 0.6
threshold.
• “Accountable” becomes: traceable through constraint prop-
agation from output to originating layer.

Toward Resolution: Two Paths Forward
If criminal law categories cannot map to AI systems, two
paths forward exist:
Path A: Corporate Strict Liability. Accept that AI output li-
ability must attach at the deployment level (corporate, not
model). This is the implicit logic of Garcia: treating AI sys-
tems as products subjects developers to strict liability for
design defects regardless of intent. Under this framework:
• Developers bear strict liability for outputs.
• Defenses are limited to state-of-the-art compliance and
user misuse.
• The Constraint Cascade provides the diagnostic frame-
work for identifying which design aspect was defective.
