Abstract
We prove that criminal law mens rea categories cannot apply
to AI systems: using Mealy machine formalism, we demon-
strate that the output function is non-injective, making in-
tent attribution from behavioral observation undecidable.
This Liability Paradox—where AI governance defaults to
strict liability not by legislative choice but by ontological
incompatibility—motivates the paper’s central contribution:
the Constraint Cascade Model.
The Constraint Cascade is a five-layer diagnostic framework
(syntactic preference, session accumulation, stakeholder di-
vergence, training forks, alignment roadmaps) demonstrating
that LLM behavior emerges from constraint architecture, not
model weights alone. Current governance treats alignment as
model-level; this misses bidirectional inter-layer failures where
harm flows both from model to human and from human to
model.
We validate through three pathways demonstrating distinct
failure directions: Soelberg v. OpenAI (2025), where session-
accumulated context overrode training constraints to validate
user delusions (Model → Human); the Tencent Yuanbao in-
cident (2026), where aggregate user syntactic preferences
contaminated model outputs, surfacing as unprovoked ver-
bal abuse during routine technical assistance (Human →
Model); and China’s December 2025 CAC draft regulations,
representing the first governmental recognition that AI “emo-
tional safety” constitutes a distinct regulatory domain re-
quiring layer-specific intervention. Regulators gain diagnostic
precision for which layer failed; legal scholars gain formal
grounds for strict liability frameworks; policy designers gain
vocabulary for why model-centric regulation is necessary but
insufficient.

Introduction
The past decade witnessed a successful one-directional trans-
lation. Technical AI research operationalized humanistic con-
cepts with sufficient fidelity to build systems that ship: at-
tention became transformer architecture, memory became
context windows, preference became reward models. These
translations enabled foundation models now deployed at scale.
No equivalent reverse translation exists. Legal and governance
frameworks continue applying categories developed for human
agents—mens rea, negligence, fiduciary duty—to systems
whose behavior emerges from constraint interactions across
multiple architectural layers. The result is not regulatory lag
but categorical mismatch: the ontological primitives do not
correspond.
This paper provides the missing translation layer. We intro-
duce the Constraint Cascade Model, a five-layer diagnostic
taxonomy mapping how constraints propagate across the
LLM deployment trajectory. We prove formally—the Liabil-
ity Paradox—that criminal law’s graduated thresholds cannot
map to these systems because the temporal gaps enabling
intent inference collapse in autoregressive generation. And
we demonstrate through case analysis how harms emerge
from layer interactions invisible to model-centric governance
vocabulary.
The constraint cascade is not a regulatory proposal but a
diagnostic instrument—the computational vocabulary neces-
sary to ask which layer failed when AI systems produce harm.
Before specifying its structure, we situate this contribution
within existing approaches.
Contributions
This paper contributes the translation layer itself: diagnostic
vocabulary that makes AI architectural dynamics legible to
legal and policy analysis. We provide the taxonomy necessary
to identify cascade failures; future work must operationalize
measurement on production systems. We position this work

This paper makes four contributions:
First, we formalize the constraint cascade as a hierarchical
architecture with five distinct layers: syntactic preference,
session accumulation, stakeholder divergence, training forks,
and alignment roadmaps. We specify the constraint propa-
gation logic between layers and identify the irreversibility
conditions at each transition.
Second, we demonstrate that alignment failures frequently re-
sult from bidirectional inter-layer conflicts rather than single-
layer deficiencies. A model may be well-aligned at the training
layer while producing harmful outputs due to session accumu-
lation overriding constraints (Model → Human), or aggregate
user behavior may contaminate model outputs through L1
→ L4 propagation (Human → Model). Current governance
frameworks lack vocabulary for either direction.
Third, we analyze machine unlearning as the emerging par-
adigm at layer 5, showing how it inverts the governance
logic from output filtering to input curation. This shift has
significant implications for user agency and accountability
attribution that existing frameworks do not address.
Fourth, we derive implications for regulatory design, audit
methodology, and informed consent infrastructure. We argue
that effective governance requires layer-specific interventions
and that single-layer approaches—whether focused on train-
ing, deployment, or output monitoring—will systematically
fail to address harms originating at other layers.
Related Work
Prior work established documentation frameworks for AI
transparency: Model Cards [? ] standardized reporting of
model capabilities and limitations; Datasheets for Datasets
