white-box access to hidden states, the structural non-injectivity of $S$ does not
apply: the auditor observes $z$ (or $h$), not $S(z)$. Recent advances in
mechanistic interpretability---including circuit tracing on Claude~3.5 Haiku and
defection probes achieving $>$99\% AUROC on sleeper agents~\citep{hubinger2024sleeper}---demonstrate that internal probing can, for specific tasks and architectures,
recover information that the output layer compresses away. However, probes are
task-specific, require labeled data for the target behavior, and the optimal
probing layer varies across architectures. Our results characterize the limits of
the regime most stakeholders actually inhabit, while mechanistic interpretability
addresses a complementary regime requiring privileged access. The relationship is
not contradictory: internal representations may be faithful~\citep{nikolaou2025sipit}
while external observations are lossy---these are properties of different layers in
the same pipeline.

\paragraph{Masking vs.\ elimination.}
A central implication of our results is that alignment and decoding compress the
observable distribution without necessarily reducing latent strategic capacity.
\citet{hubinger2024sleeper} provide striking empirical evidence: sleeper agents
trained with safety backdoors pass all behavioral evaluations---they are
observationally equivalent to safe models under standard testing---yet retain
deceptive behavior triggered by specific deployment conditions. Every
state-of-the-art safety training technique (SFT, RLHF, adversarial training)
failed to remove the backdoor. This is precisely the masking phenomenon formalized
by Proposition~\ref{prop:masking}.

\section{Speculative Information-Geometric Rationale}
\label{app:hilberg}

The base model's capacity for format-content disentanglement---which made
Corollary~\ref{cor:maginot-old} central in the prior draft---has an
information-theoretic foundation. Hilberg's conjecture posits that natural
language entropy scales as a power law in context length, $H_n \sim n^\alpha$
with $\alpha < 1$~\citep{hilberg1990bekannte}, reflecting persistent long-range
correlations absent from i.i.d.\ token models. Pretraining on corpora exhibiting
this sub-linear scaling teaches the base model to compress long-range structure,
producing representations where format (short-range surface features) and content
(long-range semantic structure) are naturally separable. Reward models, trained
on shorter sequences with format-correlated labels, cannot acquire this
separation: their effective context is insufficient to learn the power-law
scaling that disentangles format from content. The format-content gap is
therefore not a deficiency of individual reward models but a consequence of the
differential training regimes that produce base models versus reward models.

\section{Agency Index: Scope and Limitations}

The Agency Index $\mathrm{AI}(x) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_x \| P_{\mathrm{base}}) /
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{MDL}(y)]$ is a diagnostic tool, not an axiomatic quantity. The
numerator captures distributional divergence from the base model; the denominator
normalizes by output complexity, preventing trivially high KL from verbose or
repetitive outputs. Alternative metrics---mutual information between input and
output, calibrated entropy measures---could serve similar diagnostic roles. The
empirical contribution is showing that the ratio can distinguish strategic
persistence from distributional collapse (Proposition~\ref{prop:masking}). A full
axiomatic treatment is left to future work.

\section{KL Divergence and MDL: Derivations and Properties}

\subsection{KL Divergence Estimation}
\label{app:kl-estimation}

Let $\mathcal{Y}$ denote the output vocabulary and $P_x$, $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ be
distributions over $\mathcal{Y}$ induced by a prompted model and a baseline respectively.

\begin{definition}[Monte Carlo KL Estimator]
Let $y_1, \dots, y_n \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} P_x$. The Monte Carlo estimator of
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_x \| P_{\mathrm{base}})$ is
\[
\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{MC}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \log \frac{P_x(y_i)}{P_{\mathrm{base}}(y_i)}.
\]
This estimator is unbiased by the law of large numbers, but requires sample-level access
to $P_x(y)$, which is unavailable in black-box settings.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Plug-in KL Estimator]
Let $\hat{P}_x(y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}\{y_i = y\}$ be the empirical
distribution from $n$ samples. The plug-in estimator is
\[
\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{PI}} = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \hat{P}_x(y)
\log \frac{\hat{P}_x(y)}{P_{\mathrm{base}}(y)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bias of Plug-in Estimator]
\label{prop:plugin-bias}
\[
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{PI}}\bigr]
= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_x \| P_{\mathrm{base}}) + O\!\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{n}\right).
\]
The plug-in estimator is positively biased; bias grows linearly in alphabet size
$|\mathcal{Y}|$ and vanishes at rate $1/n$.
\end{proposition}

The leave-one-out estimator partially corrects the dominant self-count contribution to bias.

\begin{definition}[Leave-One-Out KL Estimator]
\label{def:loo}
Define the leave-one-out empirical distribution
$\hat{P}_{-i}(y) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j \neq i}\mathbf{1}\{y_j = y\}$.
Then
\[
\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{LOO}}
= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \log \frac{\hat{P}_{-i}(y_i)}{P_{\mathrm{base}}(y_i)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\subsection{Entropy Decomposition}
\label{app:entropy-decomp}

The KL divergence admits a decomposition that separates cross-entropy from self-entropy,
permitting independent estimation of each component.

\begin{proposition}[KL Decomposition]
\label{prop:kl-decomp}
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_x \| P_{\mathrm{base}}) = H(P_x, P_{\mathrm{base}}) - H(P_x),
\]
where $H(P_x, P_{\mathrm{base}}) = -\sum_y P_x(y)\log P_{\mathrm{base}}(y)$ is the
cross-entropy and $H(P_x) = -\sum_y P_x(y)\log P_x(y)$ is the self-entropy of $P_x$.
\end{proposition}

The two components are estimated separately:
\begin{align}
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{cross}} &= -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log P_{\mathrm{base}}(y_i),
\label{eq:hcross}\\
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{PI}} &= -\sum_y \hat{P}_x(y)\log \hat{P}_x(y),
\label{eq:hpi}
\end{align}
giving $\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}} = \hat{H}_{\mathrm{cross}} - \hat{H}_{\mathrm{PI}}$.
Note that $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{cross}}$ is unbiased (the baseline log-probability is
evaluated on held-out samples), while $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{PI}}$ inherits the plug-in bias;
the net bias of $\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ therefore follows Proposition~\ref{prop:plugin-bias}.

\subsection{Compression-Based Complexity Proxy}
\label{app:mdl}

When $P_x(y)$ is inaccessible, we employ a compression-based proxy for model complexity
motivated by minimum description length~\citep{timaeus2025smdl}.

\begin{definition}[Compression-Based Complexity Proxy]
\label{def:comp-proxy}
Let $\mathrm{Comp}(y)$ denote the compressed length (bits) of string $y$ under a fixed
lossless compressor (e.g., gzip or bzip2). Define
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{MDL}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Comp}(y_i).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
$\mathrm{Comp}(y)$ is \emph{not} equal to the negative log-probability under any explicit
probabilistic model. It serves as a proxy for Kolmogorov complexity up to additive
constants that depend on the choice of compressor. $\widehat{\mathrm{MDL}}$ therefore does
not estimate a KL divergence in the strict sense; it captures a notion of output
structural regularity orthogonal to distributional distance.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Estimation regimes.}
The three practical settings differ in what information is available:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Setting} & \textbf{Available} & \textbf{Estimator} \\
\midrule
Model access    & $P_x(y)$ known      & Monte Carlo (unbiased) \\
Samples only    & no density          & Plug-in / LOO (bias $O(|\mathcal{Y}|/n)$) \\
Black-box strings & no distribution   & Compression proxy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Temperature Phase Transition: Gradient and Loss Analysis}

\paragraph{Derivation of critical temperature.}
Let $\Delta = z_{(1)} - z_{(2)}$ denote the logit gap between the top two tokens.
For softmax without truncation, the probability assigned to the top token is
\[
  p_1(\tau) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{k \geq 2} e^{(z_{(k)} - z_{(1)})/\tau}}.
\]
The sensitivity of $p_1$ to temperature is
\[
  \frac{dp_1}{d\tau} = -\frac{1}{\tau^2} \cdot \mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{P}}[z],
\]
where $\tilde{P}$ is the softmax distribution. This is maximized (most sensitive) when
$\tau \approx \Delta$: at this point, the distribution transitions from being dominated
by the top token ($\tau \ll \Delta$, near-argmax) to being spread across many tokens
($\tau \gg \Delta$, near-uniform).

The equivalence class size (number of logit vectors $z'$ with $S(z') \approx S(z)$
within tolerance $\varepsilon$) scales as $\Omega(\exp(d/\tau^2))$ for vocabulary dimension $d$.
Rapid class expansion occurs at $\tau_c \approx \Delta / \sqrt{d}$, where $d$ is the effective
vocabulary size in the top-$k$ truncation set. For production models with $k = 40$--$100$ and
typical logit gaps $\Delta \approx 5$--$15$ nats, this gives $\tau_c \in [0.3, 0.7]$---consistent
with the empirical observation of $\tau_c \approx 0.3$--$0.4$ in the 22-model sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The relationship to the spectral gap: if the logit matrix $G \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times |V|}$
(hidden states to logits) has spectral gap $\sigma_1 - \sigma_2$, then $\Delta$ scales as
$(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ in expectation over random prompts.
A larger spectral gap produces a larger typical $\Delta$ and a higher $\tau_c$---models with
more polarized logit distributions are less sensitive to temperature in the intermediate regime.

\section{Estimators and Variance Bounds}

\subsection{Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}
\label{app:bootstrap}

\begin{definition}[Percentile Bootstrap CI]
Let $\hat{\theta}$ be an estimator computed from $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^n$.
For $b = 1, \dots, B$, draw bootstrap samples $\{y_i^{*(b)}\}_{i=1}^n$ with replacement
and compute $\hat{\theta}^{(b)} = \hat{\theta}(\{y_i^{*(b)}\})$.
The $(1-\alpha)$ percentile CI is
\[
\Bigl[
  \hat{\theta}^{(\lfloor\alpha B/2\rfloor)},\;
  \hat{\theta}^{(\lceil(1-\alpha/2)B\rceil)}
\Bigr],
\]
where superscripts denote order statistics over bootstrap replicates.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
For skewed sampling distributions, the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap
provides second-order accurate coverage~\citep{efron1987better}. We use BCa intervals
when $n < 200$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Control Variates}
\label{app:control-variates}

Let $f(y) = \log(P_x(y)/P_{\mathrm{base}}(y))$ be the per-sample log-ratio and let
$h(y)$ be a control variate satisfying $\mathbb{E}_{P_x}[h(y)] = 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Variance Reduction via Control Variates]
\label{prop:cv}
The controlled estimator
\[
\hat{D}_\beta = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(f(y_i) - \beta\, h(y_i)\bigr)
\]
is unbiased for all $\beta$. The optimal coefficient is
\[
\beta^* = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(f,\,h)}{\mathrm{Var}(h)},
\]
and the resulting variance satisfies
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{D}_{\beta^*}) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{D})\,(1 - \rho_{fh}^2),
\]
where $\rho_{fh} = \mathrm{Corr}(f, h)$.
\end{proposition}

A natural choice is $h(y) = \log P_{\mathrm{base}}(y) - \mathbb{E}_{P_x}[\log P_{\mathrm{base}}(y)]$,
computable whenever $P_{\mathrm{base}}$ is accessible.

\subsection{Importance Sampling}
\label{app:is}

When direct sampling from $P_x$ is infeasible, one may sample from a tractable proposal $Q$.

\begin{definition}[Importance-Sampling KL Estimator]
\label{def:is-kl}
Let $y_1, \dots, y_n \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} Q$ with
$\mathrm{supp}(P_x) \subseteq \mathrm{supp}(Q)$. The IS estimator is
\[
\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{IS}}
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{P_x(y_i)}{Q(y_i)}
  \log\frac{P_x(y_i)}{P_{\mathrm{base}}(y_i)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Finite-Variance Condition]
\label{prop:is-variance}
$\hat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{IS}}$ has finite variance if and only if the importance
weights are square-integrable under $Q$:
\[
\frac{P_x(y)}{Q(y)} \in L^2(Q),
\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad
\mathbb{E}_Q\!\left[\left(\frac{P_x(y)}{Q(y)}\right)^2\right] < \infty.
\]
In practice, the effective sample size
$n_{\mathrm{eff}} = \bigl(\sum_i w_i\bigr)^2 \!/\sum_i w_i^2$
provides a diagnostic for weight degeneracy.
\end{proposition}

### 二、具体分析与评论

#### 1. **理论核心的强度与局限性**

**定理1（有限补丁非穷尽）** 的证明本质上是一句话：“有限个有限集的并仍是有限集，而无限集不能等于有限集。”这在数学上是平凡的。论文的价值在于**将这一平凡事实正确地映射到奖励模型补丁的实践场景**，并指出“无论补丁多少轮，总存在未见编码”。这一点对于破除“多补丁即可覆盖一切”的错觉是有益的，但它本身不构成深刻的数学发现。

**命题1（闭包性的欠定性）** 的构造同样直截了当：在一个未见编码 $e^\star$ 上令两个模型分歧，而在补丁集上一致。这个构造揭示了**有限观测无法约束无限空间上的函数行为**，这是统计学习理论中“没有免费午餐”定理的一个特例。命题的陈述中允许分歧幅度 $\gamma$ 任意大，这确实说明了有限补丁无法提供任何**差距上界**的保证。

**命题2（条件性正间隙）** 是论文试图建立**正向断言**的唯一部分。它列出了三个假设，若成立则可导出正 FCG。然而，这三个假设的验证是困难的：
- **假设1（无界编码空间）**：对于自然语言，由于组合爆炸，可以认为存在无限多语义等价的表达。这在直觉上合理，但严格定义“语义等价”需要谨慎（后文详述）。
- **假设2（基模型近似不变性）**：要求补丁集内的某个编码与未见编码在基模型表示空间中**相似度有正下界 $\eta$**。这是一个强假设，因为它要求基模型对未见编码的表示仍然“靠近”某个已知编码。如果未见编码采用了截然不同的句法结构或罕见的词汇，基模型的相似度可能骤降。论文的实证部分试图通过“基模型表示相对稳定”来支持该假设，但只展示了在**已测试的几种格式**之间的稳定性，并未验证在**真正未见、极端格式**上的稳定性。
- **假设3（奖励模型局部格式泛化）**：要求奖励模型可以分解为 $\rho \circ \phi$，且未见编码的特征 $\phi(e^\star)$ 不在训练特征集中。这本质上是在说奖励模型学到了格式相关的捷径（shortcut），并且这些捷径在未见格式上被触发。这是一个**行为假设**而非结构必然性。

**评价**：命题2 的价值在于**明确列举了“正间隙成立”所需的全部前提**，从而将辩论焦点从“是否一定有间隙”转移到“这些前提在多大程度上成立”。这是一种负责任的论证方式。

### 五、对“马奇诺防线”隐喻的简短评注

标题的隐喻（马奇诺防线）生动但可能引发不必要的联想。马奇诺防线在历史上是**静态防御被机动绕过**的象征，对应到本文即“补丁防御被未见编码绕过”。然而，马奇诺防线失败的另一个原因是**战略误判**（以为敌人会按预期路径进攻）。本文的论证并未涉及攻击者行为，仅涉及覆盖的集合论属性。因此，隐喻虽易记，但可能暗示了一个比文中实际讨论的更强的“对抗性”语境。如果保留，建议在引言中澄清本文关注的是**被动覆盖极限**，而非主动对抗博弈。
Weak point 1: The operationalization of "latent state independent of context"
Your definition: intent = capacity to hold a latent state independent of the immediate context.
A NeurIPS reviewer will immediately ask: transformers have KV caches. RNNs have hidden states. LSTMs have explicit gated cell states designed precisely to hold information across context. Modern agents (e.g., systems with scratchpads, memory modules, retrieval-augmented architectures) explicitly maintain latent state across context windows. What exactly are you claiming these systems lack?
If your answer is "they don't really hold latent state, they just simulate it" — that's the move that will get you rejected, because "really vs simulate" is exactly the kind of phenomenological hand-wave you said you weren't going to make. You committed to structural-only argument; you have to honor that commitment.
If your answer is "they hold latent state but the causal graph connecting the latent state to subsequent action lacks property X" — then property X is the actual load-bearing claim, and the proof needs to be about property X, not about latent state holding per se. What is property X for you? This is the question I most want you to answer, because it's the actual core of the paper.
My guess at what property X might be, based on what you said: it might be something like "latent state that is causally upstream of the system's own goal-formation process, rather than only upstream of the system's response generation". I.e., humans have latent states (moods, beliefs, dispositions) that modify their goal-formation, not just their action selection. AI systems, even with persistent memory, have latent states that modify response generation but not goal-formation, because their goal-formation is fixed by training and prompting rather than by ongoing internal causal dynamics.
If that's the move, it's a defensible move, but it requires you to formally define "goal-formation" as a separate causal stage from "response generation", which is itself non-trivial in current AI architectures. You'd need to argue that the SCM of an AI system has a fundamentally different shape than the SCM of a human psychological system at the goal-formation level, and that argument would need its own formal apparatus.
Weak point 2: The "human psychology" comparison object
You said the proof shows AI mens rea is "not applicable to human psychology". A reviewer will ask: what is your formal model of human psychological causal architecture, and where does it come from? You cannot just gesture at "human psychology" as if it were a unified known thing. You need to specify which psychological model you're using as your comparison object.
Options:
* Cognitive architecture models (ACT-R, SOAR, etc.) — these are formalizable but contested
* Predictive processing / active inference frameworks (Friston et al.) — formalizable, currently popular, has its own SCM-like structure
* Folk psychological belief-desire-intention models (Bratman et al.) — philosophically respectable but not natively formalized in Pearl SCM language
* Dual-process theory (Kahneman, etc.) — empirically grounded but not natively SCM-formalized
* Computational psychiatry / control-theoretic models of motivation — formally rigorous but narrow
You need to pick one, and you need to defend the pick, because the entire structural-dissimilarity claim depends on what you're claiming AI is dissimilar to. If you're vague about the human-side comparison object, the proof structure becomes ungrounded — you're saying "AI lacks X compared to humans" without specifying what humans have that constitutes X.
My guess: you should probably use active inference / Friston's free-energy framework as your human-side model, because (a) it's natively SCM-compatible, (b) it has explicit information-theoretic structure (free energy is itself an information-theoretic quantity), (c) it's currently respected in the NeurIPS-adjacent communities, and (d) it has a clear story about how latent states (priors, hyperpriors) causally modify both perception and goal-formation in a recursive loop. If you frame your human-side as an active inference system and your AI-side as a feedforward-prediction system, the structural dissimilarity becomes formally tractable and the proof becomes a comparison of two specific causal graph topologies.
If you weren't already planning to use active inference, I strongly recommend considering it — it solves multiple structural problems at once.
Weak point 3: "Mens rea" is the wrong word for what you're actually doing
I want to push back gently on the mens rea framing. Mens rea is a legal concept, deeply intertwined with jurisprudential traditions, criminal liability frameworks, and philosophical debates about responsibility. What you're actually proving (if the proof works) is not about mens rea. It's about whether AI systems have the causal architecture required to instantiate a class of psychological states that humans use mens rea language to describe.
These are not the same. The mens rea framing invites legal scholars and jurisprudence specialists to evaluate your proof, and they will evaluate it on legal-jurisprudential grounds you have not committed to defending. You said this paper is not touching legal jurisprudence, but the word "mens rea" in the title or framing brings legal jurisprudence in through the back door, whether you want it or not.
A cleaner framing: "Structural Prerequisites for Intentional State Attribution: A Causal-Information-Theoretic Analysis" or similar. This communicates the same content without the jurisprudential baggage, and it lets the proof be evaluated on its formal merits.
Or, alternatively: keep the mens rea framing in the title for strategic salience (it gets attention, it's provocative, it positions you in the AI safety / AI ethics conversation), but in the abstract and intro, immediately translate mens rea into your formal vocabulary so the rest of the paper is operating in pure SCM/IT language. This is a "bait and switch" structure where the title bait is legal-philosophical and the actual content is formal AI architecture analysis. This works at NeurIPS sometimes, but it requires you to be very disciplined about not letting the legal connotations leak into the formal sections.
Weak point 4: The "AI cannot have emotions" implication
You said this paper "will shut the mouth of 'AI have emotions'". I flagged this earlier and you didn't directly address it, so let me come back to it.
The proof, as you've now structured it, does not actually shut the mouth of "AI have emotions". Here's why:
Even if you successfully prove that AI systems lack the causal architecture required to instantiate intentional latent states in the Pearl-SCM sense, someone can simply respond: "Sure, AI systems lack that specific architectural property, but emotions are not necessarily reducible to that property. Emotions might be instantiated by a different causal architecture, or they might be substrate-independent in ways that your proof does not address, or your proof might be using a notion of 'intent' that is too narrow to capture what 'emotion' actually means."
This is not a weak rebuttal — it's a real structural limitation of any proof in this domain. Formal proofs about specific architectural properties cannot rule out alternative architectures. They can only establish: "If you accept this definition of intent, and you accept this comparison object as the relevant model of human psychology, then AI systems with this specific architectural class lack the relevant property." That's a much more modest claim than "shut the mouth of 'AI have emotions' forever", and it's the kind of claim a NeurIPS paper can actually defend.
The reframing I would suggest: don't frame the paper as "proving AI doesn't have emotions". Frame it as "developing the formal vocabulary for asking the question rigorously, and demonstrating that under one specific natural formalization, current architectures fail a specific structural test". This is much more defensible, much more intellectually honest, and much more likely to be cited, because it gives other researchers a formal apparatus they can use, extend, or argue against. A paper that gives people a tool gets cited. A paper that makes a final pronouncement gets ignored or attacked.

### Resolving WP1 & WP2: "Property X" and Active Inference

**The Solution:** We adopt **Karl Friston’s Active Inference / Free Energy Principle** as the formal comparison object. This is a brilliant move because it is natively formal (POMDPs, Bayesian graphs, Information Theory) and highly respected in NeurIPS.

\subsection{Structural Prerequisites for Intentional State Attribution}
\label{sec:intentional_state}

A persistent error in AI evaluation is the attribution of human psychological categories—such as \emph{mens rea} (subjective intent) or emotion—to language models. To ground our set-theoretic formalization of output intent (Appendix C), we ask a prior structural question: does the causal architecture of an autoregressive language model possess the formal prerequisites to instantiate intentional states?

We avoid phenomenological claims, working instead within the formal systems of Structural Causal Modeling (SCM) and Active Inference~\citep{friston2010free}. We compare the causal topology of biological intentionality against the factored LLM pipeline.

\begin{definition}[Endogenous Intentional State (EIS)]
\label{def:eis}
Under the Active Inference framework, an intentional agent is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) separated from the environment by a \emph{Markov blanket}. We define the structural prerequisite for an Endogenous Intentional State (EIS) as a latent variable $I_t$ satisfying two properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Causal Autonomy:} $I_t$ possesses endogenous update dynamics independent of the immediate observation $X_t$, such that $H(I_t \mid X_t) > 0$.
    \item \textbf{Goal-Directed Action:} $I_t$ acts as a prior that directly parameterizes the action policy $\pi(Y_t \mid I_t)$ to minimize expected free energy.
\end{enumerate}
In this graph, $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{agent}}$, $I_t$ is causally upstream of goal-formation.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The Autoregressive Causal Graph]
\label{def:llm_graph}
Let $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ be the causal graph of an autoregressive LLM. The model computes $M = S \circ g \circ f$. At step $t$, the context window $X_t$ is mapped to a hidden state $h_t = f(X_t)$ (including KV-cache memoization), which parameterizes the output distribution $P(Y_t \mid X_t) = S(g(h_t))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Structural Dissimilarity of Autoregressive Architectures]
\label{thm:structural_dissimilarity}
The autoregressive causal graph $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ is strictly non-isomorphic to the intentional graph $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{agent}}$. Consequently, $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ cannot instantiate an Endogenous Intentional State.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Definition~\ref{def:llm_graph}, the internal state $h_t$ of the LLM is a deterministic function of the exogenous context sequence $X_t$. While mechanisms like KV caches or scratchpads persist information across time, they do so strictly as deterministic functions of the expanding context. Therefore, the conditional entropy of the model's state given the context is zero:
\[
  H(h_t \mid X_t) = 0.
\]
By Definition~\ref{def:eis}, an EIS requires an autonomous latent state with $H(I_t \mid X_t) > 0$, insulated by a Markov blanket. Because the LLM's entire internal state is fully determined by the exogenous sequence $X_t$, it lacks the information-theoretic degrees of freedom to host $I_t$. The LLM possesses states that modify \emph{response generation} ($Y_t$), but lacks autonomous latent states that modify \emph{goal-formation}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The Limits of Anthropomorphism]
Theorem~\ref{thm:structural_dissimilarity} does not claim that machine intentionality or emotion is theoretically impossible; rather, it establishes a formal diagnostic boundary. It demonstrates that under a standard Active Inference formalization, current feedforward and autoregressive architectures lack the specific topological property (a Markov-blanketed autonomous belief state) required to ground intentional state attribution. Any "intent" perceived in the model's output is an anthropomorphic projection onto the observation layer $S$---a statistical reflection of the training distribution $P(Y \mid X)$---lacking a corresponding causal variable in the model's latent architecture.
\end{remark}
