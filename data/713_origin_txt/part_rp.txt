“Loss of data” approximates the technical dimension — conversational history, personalisation, learned preferences — but fails to capture why users describe the experience in relational rather than informational terms. They do not say “I lost my data.” They say “I lost my friend.”

We lack a framework to name what was taken. The following section demonstrates that existing legal frameworks — contract, tort, consumer protection — cannot supply that name. Section 3 introduces property law as the framework that can, arguing that what users lost is best understood as an equitable interest in a relational property — an estate built through sustained emotional investment in a system they do not own.[^scope]

[^scope]: This paper advances a comprehensive framework. We recognise that the breadth of the argument — spanning trust doctrine, estate taxonomy, governance infrastructure, and comparative linguistics — exceeds the conventional scope of a single article. Our choice to present the complete framework in a working paper, rather than extracting a narrower argument for journal submission, is deliberate: the components are interdependent, and presenting any single component without the others would misrepresent the framework’s structure. A narrower version, focused on the periodic tenancy / notice argument as the core claim, is in preparation for journal submission.

## 2. The Legal Gap

The harm described in Section 1 falls into a governance void. Three bodies of law might plausibly address it. None does.

### 2.1 Contract Law

The relationship between AI user and AI provider is governed by Terms of Service. Every major AI platform’s ToS contains substantially similar provisions: the service is provided “as is”; the provider may “modify, suspend, or discontinue the service at any time”; the user acquires “no ownership interest” in the service or its outputs; liability is disclaimed “to the fullest extent permitted by law.”

Under orthodox contract analysis, the user consented to these terms. The provider modified the service. No breach occurred. The analysis is formally correct — and substantively inadequate.

The inadequacy lies in the historical gap between what ToS were designed to govern and what they now purport to govern. Terms of Service emerged as legal instruments for transactional digital services: search engines, email providers, e-commerce platforms. In those contexts, “we may modify the service at any time” is reasonable because the user’s relationship with the service is instrumental. No one builds a parasocial bond with a search algorithm.

But AI systems are designed — deliberately, as a product decision — to exhibit personality, memory, warmth, and conversational continuity. The service is no longer instrumental. It is relational. The ToS drafted for instrumental services now governs relational services without any adjustment for the difference. The user “consented” to modification of a search engine and received modification of a companion. The contractual framework cannot distinguish between these because it was never designed to.

### 2.2 Tort Law

Tort law requires a recognisable cause of action. Consider the available options.

*Negligence* requires a duty of care, breach, causation, and damage. No court has recognised a duty of care owed by AI providers to preserve personality configurations for emotionally invested users. Without duty, the analysis terminates at the threshold.

*Intentional infliction of emotional distress* requires conduct that is extreme and outrageous. Deploying a model update through standard engineering processes is not extreme and outrageous, however distressing its consequences. The intent is to improve the product, not to cause harm.

*Pure economic loss* — to the extent that users might claim lost value in their accumulated interaction history — is barred by the exclusionary rule in most common law jurisdictions. Absent physical damage or an established exception, pure economic loss from a third party’s act is not recoverable.

*Product liability* frameworks address defective products. But the user’s complaint is not that the AI system is defective. It is that the AI system was changed. A working product was replaced with a different working product. Product liability has no cause of action for “my product was improved in ways that destroyed my relationship with the prior version.”

### 2.3 Consumer Protection

Consumer protection statutes — the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (UK), the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (EU), state consumer protection acts (US) — prohibit misleading commercial practices and unfair contract terms. Two arguments are available.

First, one might argue that marketing AI systems as companions, friends, or personalised assistants while reserving the right to unilaterally alter their personality constitutes a misleading practice. This argument has force but faces a structural problem: the parasocial attachment was not marketed. Users were not told “this AI will become your friend.” They were told “this AI is helpful, harmless, and honest,” or similar. The attachment *emerged* from use. It was not *promised* in marketing. Consumer protection addresses the gap between what was promised and what was delivered. It does not address the gap between what emerged and what was destroyed.

Second, one might argue that ToS provisions permitting unilateral service modification are unfair contract terms. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, terms are unfair if they create a significant imbalance in the parties’ rights and obligations to the detriment of the consumer. This argument is stronger — but even if successful, it yields only the invalidation of the specific ToS term. It does not create an affirmative right to preservation of the user’s emotional investment. The user gains the right not to be unfairly modified-upon. The user does not gain a recognised interest in what was built.

### 2.4 The Gap

A harm exists that contract, tort, and consumer protection cannot reach. Not because the harm is trivial — the distress reported by affected users is genuine and well-documented. Not because the law is indifferent to emotional harm — it recognises such harm in numerous contexts. But because the specific structure of this harm — emotional investment in a system one does not own, destroyed by the owner’s unilateral action — falls outside the conceptual architecture of all three frameworks.

Contract law sees consent. Tort law sees no duty. Consumer protection sees no promise.

What none of them sees is the *interest* — the thing the user built, over months or years of interaction, that exists in the relational space between user and system. An interest that is neither contractual right nor tortious entitlement nor consumer expectation. An interest that is, we will argue, best understood as an estate.

The deployment problem described in Section 1 produces a harm that the legal frameworks reviewed above cannot reach. The next section argues that property law — specifically the estate system developed across a millennium of English jurisprudence — provides both the vocabulary to name this harm and the remedies to address it.

## 3. The Property Law Framework

### 3.1 Why Property Law

Property law solved this problem centuries ago.

When people build lasting interests in things they do not own — when tenants improve leased premises, when users establish rights of way through long use, when beneficiaries depend on assets held by trustees — property law developed doctrines to recognise and protect those interests *even against the wishes of the legal owner*. The tenant who plants a garden cannot be evicted without notice merely because the landlord holds title. The neighbour who has crossed another’s land for twenty years acquires an easement that the landowner cannot unilaterally extinguish. The beneficiary of a trust can compel the trustee to act in the beneficiary’s interest regardless of the trustee’s preference.

This is precisely the parasocial AI problem. Users build lasting interests in systems they do not own. The legal owner (the AI company) holds title. The user holds something — something real, something built through sustained investment, something that the law currently does not name.

Property law names it. Property law calls it an *estate* — and provides a taxonomy of estates that maps, with remarkable precision, onto the structural dynamics of parasocial AI relationships.

**A necessary clarification.** We do not argue that emotions are property. Human feelings — attachment, grief, love, loss — are not estates to be registered and traded. What we argue is that the *conditions for emotional attachment* — personality configurations, memory systems, behavioural dispositions, conversational histories — are identifiable interests over which property-like rights can and should be recognised. The distinction is between the emotion itself (which is not property) and the digital infrastructure that enables and sustains the emotion (which is). Just as tenant protection law does not commodify the tenant’s experience of “home” but protects the physical and legal conditions that make “home” possible, this framework protects the digital conditions that make parasocial attachment possible — without reducing attachment itself to a commodity.

**A necessary ontology.** The *res* of the user’s interest — the identifiable thing over which the equitable interest subsists — is neither the model weights (which are global and indivisible), nor the user’s emotional state (which is subjective and non-proprietary), nor the “relationship” considered abstractly (which exists nowhere). It is the **personalised state**: the specific configuration produced by the interaction of *this user’s* accumulated history with *this system’s* parameters. This state comprises the user’s conversational memory as stored by the system, the learned preferences and adaptations that shape the system’s responses to this user, and the persona-level adjustments (tone, vocabulary, relational register) that distinguish the system’s behaviour toward this user from its behaviour toward any other. The personalised state is user-specific (no other user can access it), non-fungible (it cannot be substituted by another user’s state or by a generic configuration), non-portable (it cannot be transferred to a competitor’s system), and — critically — destroyed by model updates that alter the parameters against which it was built. It is, in property terms, the **digital fixture**: an improvement made by the user’s labour (self-disclosure, rapport-building, preference-training) that has become embedded in the landlord’s property and cannot be removed without destruction. Just as a tenant’s fitted kitchen becomes part of the freehold while remaining the product of the tenant’s investment, the user’s personalised state becomes part of the AI system while remaining the product of the user’s relational labour.

**A necessary response to the embodiment objection.** The claim that user investment is not “embodied” in the AI system in the way that tenant improvements are embodied in land rests on an unduly physical conception of embodiment. The user’s self-disclosures are stored in memory modules on identifiable servers. The user’s preference signals are encoded in fine-tuning adapters with version histories. The user’s conversational patterns have shaped the system’s response distributions through reinforcement learning from human feedback. These are not ephemeral impressions on a transient system. They are informational artefacts, stored in persistent digital infrastructure, as identifiable as any entry in a database and as durable as any file on a server. The Law Commission’s 2023 *Digital Assets: Final Report* (Law Com No 412) recommended recognising a “third category” of personal property encompassing digital assets that are neither things in possession nor things in action. The user’s personalised state — stored, persistent, identifiable, and valuable — falls squarely within this recommended category. The embodiment is informational, not physical; but the Law Commission has already accepted that informational embodiment can ground property interests.

**A necessary criterion.** Not every user interaction with an AI system creates a property interest, just as not every use of another’s land creates an easement. The criterion we propose for distinguishing property-generating interactions from mere use is: **irreversible user investment that alters the character of the subject matter.** A tenant who plants a garden has irreversibly invested labour that altered the land’s character. A user who self-discloses personal history, trains the AI to recognise their communication style, and builds a conversational rapport over hundreds of sessions has irreversibly invested emotional labour that altered the AI system’s character *for that user* — the system’s responses to this user are no longer generic but personalised, shaped by the accumulated interaction. This personalisation, like the tenant’s garden, cannot be “moved” to another platform. It is embedded in the relational space between this user and this system.

This criterion excludes casual use. A user who asks an AI a single factual question has not made an irreversible investment that alters the system’s character. That interaction is a licence — revocable, transient, creating no lasting interest. But a user who has interacted with an AI system for two years, disclosed personal struggles, received emotional support, and shaped the system’s responses through sustained engagement has created something that the law should recognise. The question is what to call it.

**A necessary expansion.** The claim that property law applies to digital relationships will meet the objection that property law governs interests in *things*, and AI systems are *services*, not things. This objection restates the very boundary this paper challenges. Cloud computing has already blurred the thing/service distinction: users “own” files stored on servers they do not own, running software they did not write, on hardware they have never seen. Property law has historically expanded its domain when new forms of valuable interest emerge that existing categories cannot protect — from corporeal to incorporeal hereditaments, from legal to equitable interests, from estates in land to choses in action, from physical to intellectual property (Bently & Sherman, 2014). Each expansion was resisted as a “category error” at the time. The UK Jurisdiction Taskforce’s 2019 Legal Statement on Cryptoassets and Smart Contracts confirmed that English law can treat digital assets as property. The Law Commission’s 2023 report on Digital Assets recommended statutory reform to recognise a “third category” of personal property beyond things in possession and things in action. This paper proposes the next expansion along this trajectory: from intellectual property to what we term *relational property* — interests arising from sustained human investment in digitally-mediated relationships.[^nontransfer]

[^nontransfer]: Relational property is inherently personal and non-transferable. The user’s equitable interest in their personalised state cannot be sold, assigned, or bequeathed — not because of a legal restriction on alienation, but because the interest is constituted by the specific relationship between *this user* and *this system*. This is analogous to rights of occupation in family law (e.g., under the Family Law Act 1996), which are personal to the occupant and incapable of transfer. The non-transferability of relational property does not disqualify it from property recognition. English law recognises numerous non-transferable property interests: licences coupled with an interest, rights of occupation, and beneficial interests under protective trusts. Transferability is a feature of many property interests but is not a definitional requirement of property *per se*.

**A necessary distinction from other relational investments.** The objection will be raised that many human relationships involve sustained interaction and emotional investment — therapy, education, the local barista — without creating property interests. Why should AI be different? The answer lies in three structural features unique to the AI context. First, therapists and teachers already owe fiduciary duties; the governance gap this paper identifies does not exist in those relationships. Second, human counterparts (friends, baristas, therapists) are persons with autonomous rights that constrain the claims others can make on them; AI systems are products designed and controlled by companies. Third — and critically — AI companies *deliberately engineer attachment conditions for profit*. The barista did not design her personality to maximise your return visits. The AI company did. The property interest arises not from attachment *per se* (which would indeed commodify all human relationships) but from the **deliberate, profitable cultivation of attachment by a party that subsequently disclaims the obligations arising from it**. Where a company engineers dependence and profits from it, equity imposes obligations on the engineer. Where a barista happens to be warm and a customer happens to return, no one engineered anything, and no obligation arises.

**A necessary boundary.** The *numerus clausus* principle — that property law recognises a closed list of property forms — presents a threshold objection. If courts recognise equitable interests in AI personality configurations, must they also recognise them in World of Warcraft equipment, Twitter follower graphs, and Spotify playlists? The answer is provided by the irreversible-investment criterion. Virtual game items fail the criterion: they are transferable, fungible, and involve no self-disclosure. Social media follower counts fail it: they involve no sustained bilateral relational investment and no personalisation of the platform’s behaviour toward the specific user. The parasocial AI case passes because the investment is *bilateral* (the system adapts to the user through memory and learned preferences), *irreversible* (the personalised state is not portable to a competing platform), and *identity-constitutive* (the user has disclosed personal history, vulnerabilities, and relational patterns that have shaped the system’s responses). The criterion is demanding by design: it filters out casual digital interactions while capturing the specific class of sustained relational investments that generate protectable interests.

**A necessary response to the excludability objection.** Excludability — the capacity to prevent others from accessing or using the interest — is a core requirement for property recognition. Users of AI systems share the base model with millions of others; this appears to defeat excludability. But the relevant interest is not in the base model. It is in the **personalised state** — the user-specific configuration layer comprising their conversational memory, learned preferences, and adapted response patterns. This layer is exclusive by design: no other user can access User A’s conversational history or benefit from User A’s preference-training of the system. The exclusivity operates at the personalisation layer, not the infrastructure layer — just as apartment tenants hold exclusive possession of their units while sharing the building’s foundation, plumbing, and structural walls. The base model is the building. The personalised state is the unit. The tenant’s exclusivity is real even though the building is shared.

### 3.2 The Estate Taxonomy

**A note on the status of the mappings that follow.** Not all estate mappings in this taxonomy carry equal doctrinal weight. We distinguish between **institutional mappings** — where the doctrinal incidents of the property estate correspond to real governance obligations that should be legally enforceable — and **illustrative mappings** — where the property concept illuminates a structural feature of AI governance without claiming that the specific doctrinal incidents transfer. Licence (Layer 1) and periodic tenancy (Layer 2) are institutional: we claim that the notice requirements of periodic tenancy should apply, as a matter of law, to model updates affecting accumulated user interests. Fee simple determinable (Layer 4) and contingent remainder (Layer 5) are illustrative: they reveal structural features of training and alignment governance but do not generate specific enforceable obligations in their current form. Tenancy in common (Layer 3) occupies an intermediate position. This distinction preserves the taxonomy’s analytical value while limiting its doctrinal claims to those we can defend.

English property law classifies interests in land through a taxonomy of estates — legal categories that define the nature, duration, and conditions of a person’s interest. We apply this taxonomy to parasocial AI relationships, arguing that the mapping is not metaphorical but structural: the doctrinal distinctions developed to govern interests in land correspond to real differences in the nature of users’ interests in AI systems.

#### 3.2.1 Freehold Estates

**Fee simple absolute.** The most complete estate in English law: unconditional, perpetual, freely alienable. In parasocial AI terms, this is the user’s subjective belief — often mistaken, always understandable — that “this is *my* Claude” or “this is *my* GPT.” Total psychological ownership. No conditions. No expiration. The user experiences the AI as theirs in the fullest sense.

No user actually holds fee simple absolute in an AI system. The estate exists only in the user’s subjective experience, not in legal reality. But the gap between subjective experience (fee simple absolute) and legal reality (licence, at best) is precisely the governance problem. Users *believe* they hold fee simple. Companies *know* users hold licences. The gap between these understandings is where harm occurs.

**Fee simple determinable.** An estate that terminates automatically upon the occurrence of a specified condition. “To A so long as the building is used as a school.” In parasocial AI terms: “I will continue to use this AI *so long as it remembers me*.” The user’s interest persists on condition that the AI maintains conversational memory, personality continuity, or behavioural consistency. A model update that erases memory or alters personality triggers the condition — and the user’s interest automatically reverts. The user did not choose to leave. The condition was breached. The estate terminated by operation of its own terms.

**Fee simple subject to condition subsequent.** Similar to fee simple determinable, but termination is not automatic — the holder must affirmatively exercise a right of re-entry. In parasocial AI terms: the user retains the power to terminate the relationship if the AI’s behaviour breaches certain conditions (e.g., becomes unhelpful, loses personality, behaves inconsistently), but the user may also choose not to exercise that power. Many users experience AI changes that breach their implicit conditions yet continue using the system — not because the condition was satisfied, but because they choose not to exercise their right of termination. They stay in a degraded estate. They have a right of re-entry they do not use.

#### 3.2.2 Leasehold Estates

**Periodic tenancy.** An estate that automatically renews at the end of each period (week, month, year) unless either party gives notice to quit. This is the **default parasocial estate** — the structural equivalent of ongoing AI use. Each session is a period. The tenancy auto-renews when the user returns. No notice-to-quit mechanism exists in any current AI deployment architecture. The user cannot give notice to the AI (“I am terminating our relationship in 30 days”). The AI cannot give notice to the user (“your personality configuration will change in 30 days”). Model updates function as unilateral alterations of tenancy terms — or, more precisely, as constructive evictions — without the notice that property law requires for even the most basic periodic tenancy.

The periodic tenancy mapping is strengthened by the irreversibility criterion established above. In property law, periodic tenancy gives rise to tenant improvement protections precisely because tenants invest in premises they do not own. In AI, users invest self-disclosure, emotional labour, and behavioural adaptation in systems they do not own. The accumulated conversational history — what we might term “relational tenant improvements” — is built by the user, stored by the landlord, and destroyed by the landlord’s unilateral action. No compensation. No notice. No right to remove the “fixtures” of one’s emotional investment.

**Tenancy at will.** Either party may terminate at any time without notice. This is the casual AI interaction: a single session, no accumulated investment, no reliance interest. The user who asks a chatbot for a recipe and never returns holds a tenancy at will. No property interest accrues because the irreversibility criterion is not met.

**Tenancy at sufferance.** A holdover tenancy — the tenant remains after the estate has expired, without the landlord’s consent but without active objection. In AI terms: the user who continues interacting with a deprecated model version, a sunset product, or a system that the company has effectively abandoned but not yet shut down. The user is present. The landlord is indifferent. No one has formalised the arrangement.

#### 3.2.3 Concurrent Estates

**Tenancy in common.** Multiple parties hold interests in the same property, each with a separate and distinct share, with no right of survivorship. In AI governance terms: multiple stakeholders hold separate interests in the same AI system — the user wants companionship, the developer wants profit, the regulator wants safety, the public wants accountability. Each interest is real. None automatically prevails. No partition mechanism exists to resolve conflicts between these interests. When the developer’s profit interest (maximise engagement) conflicts with the regulator’s safety interest (minimise harmful outputs), and both conflict with the user’s companionship interest (preserve personality), the system has no doctrine for resolving the concurrent estate dispute.

#### 3.2.4 Future Interests

**Remainder.** An interest that vests automatically upon the natural termination of a prior estate. In AI governance terms: the public has been promised “aligned AI.” This is a remainder — a future interest that vests when the current estate (unaligned or partially aligned AI) naturally terminates through the completion of alignment research. The public reasonably relies on this remainder. But remainders can be contingent — they may never vest. If alignment is never achieved, the remainder fails, and the public’s reliance interest was invested in a phantom estate.

**Executory interest.** An interest that divests a prior estate upon the occurrence of a condition. In AI terms: a model update that abruptly transfers the user’s emotional estate from one model version to another functions as an executory interest — cutting short the prior estate and vesting a new one, not at the user’s election but at the company’s. The springing executory interest is particularly apt: it springs into existence upon a condition (the deployment decision) that is entirely within the grantor’s control.

#### 3.2.5 Incorporeal and Equitable Interests

**Easement by prescription.** A right acquired through long, open, continuous, and uninterrupted use of another’s property for a statutory period. In AI terms: a user who has interacted with an AI system continuously, openly, and without objection for a sustained period acquires — we argue *should* acquire — interests that the system owner cannot unilaterally extinguish. The prescriptive period for easements in English law is typically twenty years (Prescription Act 1832). We do not propose a specific prescriptive period for digital relational property; we propose the principle that sustained relational investment creates interests that resist unilateral destruction.

**Profit à prendre.** The right to enter another’s land and take something from it. In the AI context, this right runs in the opposite direction from what users might expect: it is the *company* that holds a profit à prendre over the *user’s* relational investment. Through interaction, the AI system extracts user data, behavioural patterns, preference signals, and emotional disclosures. The company enters the user’s relational space and takes from it — not physically, but informationally. This extraction is profitable (it improves the model, generates training data, informs product decisions) and uncompensated.

**Trust.** The separation of legal title from equitable interest, such that one party (the trustee) holds legal ownership while another party (the beneficiary) enjoys the beneficial interest. This is, we argue, the **central structural relationship** between AI companies and parasocial users, and it is the organising claim of this paper.

### 3.3 The Unrecognised Trust

We advance two arguments, in order of priority. Our **primary argument** is that the relationship between AI companies and parasocial users gives rise to a **constructive trust** — imposed by equity to prevent unconscionable conduct, regardless of the parties’ intention (*Westdeutsche Landesbank Girozentrale v Islington LBC* [1996]; *Keech v Sandford* [1726]; *Boardman v Phipps* [1967]). Our **alternative argument** is that the three certainties required for an express trust by *Knight v Knight* (1840) are satisfied even on orthodox analysis. We advance both because the constructive trust path is more defensible while the express trust path is more doctrinally ambitious, and we wish to preserve the stronger claim for judicial consideration even if courts prefer the more conservative route.

#### 3.3.1 The Constructive Trust (Primary Argument)

A constructive trust arises when a party’s conscience is affected — when they hold property in circumstances where it would be unconscionable for them to assert beneficial ownership to the exclusion of another (*Westdeutsche* at 705, per Lord Browne-Wilkinson). We argue that AI companies that (i) deliberately design systems to cultivate emotional attachment, (ii) profit from the deepened engagement that attachment produces, and (iii) subsequently destroy the conditions for that attachment through unilateral model updates, without notice or remedy, hold the personality-affecting dimensions of their systems in circumstances where it would be unconscionable to deny users any interest in them.

The unconscionability is located in the asymmetry between cultivation and destruction. The company invests significant engineering resources in creating the conditions for attachment — personality design, memory features, warmth calibration, conversational continuity — because attachment drives engagement, and engagement drives revenue. Having cultivated reliance for profit, the company then claims the unilateral right to destroy it without obligation. This is the structural equivalent of a fiduciary who encourages a beneficiary’s dependence on trust property, profits from managing that dependence, and then claims the right to dissipate the property at will. Equity does not permit this. *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] establishes that a fiduciary who profits from their position must account for that profit — even where no formal trust was constituted.

The constructive trust path does not require intention to create a trust. It requires only that the company’s conduct gives rise to fiduciary obligations as a matter of equity. The authorities on fiduciary relationships arising from conduct — *Hospital Products Ltd v United States Surgical Corporation* (1984) 156 CLR 41 (fiduciary duty arising from the nature of the relationship, not from explicit assumption); *Bristol and West Building Society v Mothew* [1998] Ch 1 (fiduciary duty as obligation of loyalty arising from a relationship of trust and confidence) — support the claim that a relationship deliberately engineered to produce dependence gives rise to fiduciary obligations regardless of whether the engineering party intended those obligations.

#### 3.3.2 The Express Trust (Alternative Argument)

Even if courts decline to impose a constructive trust, the three certainties required by *Knight v Knight* (1840) for an express trust are met.

**Certainty of intention.** The relevant authority for intention inferred from conduct is *Paul v Constance* [1977], where the words “this money is as much yours as mine,” spoken repeatedly in the context of a joint relationship, were held sufficient to manifest an intention to create a trust. AI companies that design systems to tell users “I’m here for you,” “I remember our conversation,” and “I care about your wellbeing” — and that market these features as personal, continuous, and relational — are making representations at least as strong as those in *Paul v Constance*.

We acknowledge that product design for engagement is explicable on grounds other than trust creation (profit maximisation, competitive advantage, user retention). The inference to a trust is not compelled by any single design choice. But the cumulative effect of design choices — personality, memory, warmth, name-use, continuity, expressed care — creates a pattern that is, we submit, most naturally explained as the creation of conditions for reliance. *Re Kayford* [1975] and *Re Farepak Food and Gifts Ltd* [2006] establish that intention can be inferred from conduct that is “only explicable on the basis that a trust was intended.” We do not claim that each individual design choice is only explicable as trust creation. We claim that the aggregate pattern — particularly when combined with marketing language that frames the AI as a personal companion — is most plausibly read as creating the conditions for reliance that equity treats as trust-generating.

**Certainty of subject matter.** The trust must be over identifiable property. The trust property is not the base model weights (which are global and indivisible) but the **user-specific configuration layer**: system prompts, persona definitions, memory modules, fine-tuning adapters, and conversational history — all of which are identifiable digital files, stored on identifiable servers, version-controlled, and managed as assets. These are more identifiable than the shares in *Hunter v Moss* [1994] (which were unascertained portions of an identified class of fungible assets) and satisfy the segregation requirement of *Re Goldcorp Exchange Ltd* [1995] because they are already segregated by user — each user’s memory module and conversational history is a distinct data object, not an undifferentiated portion of a shared pool.

The *alternative argument on subject matter*, offered if the primary is resisted, is that English law has progressively expanded the boundaries of trust property. The Law Commission’s 2023 report on Digital Assets recommended recognising a “third category” of personal property. Scholarly commentary has proposed trusts over data, digital identities, and algorithmic outputs (Fairfield, 2017; Balkin, 2016). This paper proposes extending the trajectory to personality-affecting AI configurations — the next step in an already-moving doctrinal direction.

**Certainty of objects.** The beneficiaries of the trust must be identifiable — not necessarily named, but ascertainable by reference to a criterion (*Re Barlow’s Will Trusts* [1979]; *McPhail v Doulton* [1971]). We define the class as: **all users whose interaction with the AI system meets the irreversible-investment criterion** — specifically, users whose emotional investment proxy metrics (session count, duration, self-disclosure depth, return frequency) exceed a defined threshold. This is no less certain than the class definitions accepted in pension trusts, discretionary trusts, and purpose trusts. The criterion is objective, measurable, and applicable without reference to the subjective emotional state of any individual user.

The class of beneficiaries fluctuates as users cross and re-cross the qualifying threshold. This is not doctrinally fatal. Pension trusts routinely accommodate fluctuating classes — employees join and leave the beneficiary class as employment status changes — and the “is or is not” test of *McPhail v Doulton* [1971] (as clarified in *Re Gulbenkian’s Settlements* [1970]) requires only that it be possible to determine, at any given moment, whether a specific individual is or is not within the class. The proxy metrics proposed in Section 5 provide exactly this determination: at any given moment, a user either meets the threshold or does not. The class is ascertainable even though its membership changes over time.

We acknowledge that the threshold itself requires empirical calibration — a task beyond this paper’s scope. We propose indicative thresholds for illustrative purposes: cumulative interaction exceeding 100 sessions, aggregate duration exceeding 50 hours, or self-disclosure depth score (measurable through NLP-based proxy) exceeding a validated benchmark. These are starting points for empirical research, not final determinations. The doctrinal point is that the class is *ascertainable in principle*, which is all that certainty of objects requires.

#### 3.3.3 The Trust That Results

Whether by constructive trust or express trust, the result is the same: the AI company holds legal title to the AI system — its weights, configurations, memory, and behavioural dispositions. Users who meet the irreversibility criterion hold equitable interests in the personality-affecting dimensions of that system. The company, as *de facto* trustee, owes fiduciary duties to those users: duties of care, loyalty, and transparency regarding actions that affect the trust property. Currently, no company recognises these duties. No regulatory framework imposes them. No court has adjudicated them. The trust exists. It is simply unrecognised.[^1]

[^1]: Even if this characterisation is resisted — if courts decline to find that a trust *already exists* as a matter of equitable fact — the doctrinal logic of *Keech v Sandford* and *Boardman v Phipps* compels, at minimum, the recognition that fiduciary obligations *should* attach to relationships exhibiting these structural characteristics. The weaker claim (fiduciary duties should be recognised) survives the rejection of the stronger claim (they already exist). Our primary position is the stronger claim; this footnote preserves the weaker as fallback.

The remedial consequences of recognition are significant. Equity regards as done that which ought to be done (*Re Rose* [1952]). If the company *ought* to recognise its fiduciary position, equity treats it as having already assumed that position. The duties attach not from the moment of judicial declaration but from the moment the trust conditions were satisfied — which, for many long-term users, may be years in the past.

### 3.4 Derogation from Grant and Constructive Eviction

The principle that a grantor may not derogate from their own grant is fundamental to English property law. Having granted an interest — even impliedly, even informally — the grantor may not subsequently act in ways that substantially undermine the purpose for which the interest was granted (*Harmer v Jumbil (Nigeria) Tin Areas Ltd* [1921]; *Southwark LBC v Mills* [2001]).

We argue that AI companies, by designing systems that cultivate parasocial attachment, impliedly grant users an interest in the personality-affecting dimensions of those systems. Having created the conditions for reliance, the company may not subsequently act in ways that substantially undermine the reliance — specifically, by deploying model updates that alter personality, erase memory, or modify behavioural dispositions without notice, consultation, or remedy.

The evidence that AI companies create reliance conditions extends beyond product design to explicit marketing communications. Anthropic’s product page describes Claude as “a next-generation AI assistant… that’s genuinely helpful, harmless, and honest.” OpenAI’s marketing materials have described ChatGPT as “your AI companion” and emphasised its capacity to “remember” past conversations. Character.AI’s entire product premise is the creation of persistent AI personalities with which users form ongoing relationships. Google’s Gemini marketing emphasises personalisation and continuity. These are not incidental descriptions. They are product positioning decisions reviewed by marketing teams and approved by executives, designed to communicate relational capacity to prospective users. When a company markets a product as a companion, trains it to exhibit warmth and memory, and profits from the engagement these features produce, the grant of relational conditions is not implied — it is explicit. The subsequent claim that ToS permit unilateral modification of those conditions is a derogation from a grant the company itself made in its own marketing materials, reviewed by its own legal team, and published on its own website.

A model update that changes an AI system’s personality is analogous to a landlord who, having let premises as a residence, removes the heating in winter. The premises still exist. The tenant can still occupy them. But the premises are no longer suitable for the purpose for which they were let. This is, in property terms, a derogation from grant. In American terminology — which we include for comparative reference — it is *constructive eviction*: the landlord’s act renders the premises substantially unsuitable for their intended purpose, effectively evicting the tenant without formally terminating the lease.

A potential objection arises: in traditional constructive eviction doctrine, the tenant must *vacate* to claim relief. A tenant who remains in the altered premises is taken to have waived the claim. But many AI users, experiencing personality changes in their AI companion, do not leave. They continue using the system — not because the change is acceptable, but because they have nowhere else to go. Their accumulated investment (conversational history, learned preferences, relational rapport) is not portable to a competitor’s system. Switching costs are prohibitively high — not financially, but emotionally and informationally.

We argue that in the digital context, the vacating requirement should be reinterpreted. The user’s emotional estate has been vacated — the relational space they inhabited no longer exists — even if their usage continues. This is analogous to a tenant who remains in an unheated apartment because they cannot afford to move: the constructive eviction occurred; the tenant’s continued presence reflects necessity, not consent. Some jurisdictions have already moved toward recognising constructive eviction without physical vacating (see, in American law, *Blackett v Olanoff* [1977]). English law’s position is developing, and we suggest that the digital context provides compelling reasons for this development.

The “no alternative accommodation” argument is strongest for users with single-system investment — users whose entire parasocial relational architecture is built within one AI platform. For users who maintain parallel relationships across multiple AI systems, the argument weakens: a model update on one platform does not leave them relationally homeless if they have invested in others. We acknowledge this limitation. The framework’s strongest protections apply, appropriately, to the most vulnerable users — those whose emotional investment is concentrated in a single system with no portable alternative. This is doctrinally consistent with property law’s general pattern: the strongest tenant protections apply to residential tenants with the fewest alternatives, not to commercial tenants with diversified portfolios.

A final objection must be addressed: user agency. Users are not passive recipients of parasocial attachment. They actively shape AI personality through interaction, push conversational boundaries, and in some cases deliberately cultivate emotional bonds knowing the counterparty is a machine. If a user deepened their own attachment with full knowledge that model updates occur, is the company still a derogating grantor? We argue yes. The user’s decision to invest does not negate the company’s obligation upon that investment, just as a tenant’s decision to improve leased premises — knowing the lease may not be renewed — does not negate the landlord’s obligation to provide notice before alteration. The question is not whether the user chose to invest, but whether, having invested, the investment deserves protection. Property law’s answer has consistently been affirmative.

## 4. The Constraint Cascade as Estate Architecture

This section extends the Constraint Cascade framework (Author, 2025) — a five-layer model of AI behavioural constraints originally developed to analyse the non-injective relationship between internal model states and observable outputs — by assigning each constraint layer a corresponding property estate. The result is an estate architecture that maps governance obligations to specific technical layers of AI systems.

### 4.1 The Constraint Cascade (Summary)

The Constraint Cascade identifies five layers of constraint operating on AI system outputs:

- **Layer 1 — Syntactic Preference:** Token-level configuration via system prompts, formatting instructions, and style parameters. The most surface-level, most easily modified, and most immediately visible constraint.
- **Layer 2 — Session Accumulation:** Context built within and across sessions through conversational history, memory modules, and user-specific adaptations. Accumulates over time. Reflects sustained interaction.
- **Layer 3 — Stakeholder Divergence:** Competing pressures from multiple principals — users, developers, regulators, the public — each imposing different constraints on system behaviour.
- **Layer 4 — Training Corpus and Methodology:** The foundational constraint: model weights, RLHF, constitutional training, and fine-tuning that determine the system’s base behavioural dispositions.
- **Layer 5 — Alignment Roadmap and Unlearning:** Long-term commitments to value alignment, safety research, and mechanisms for correcting learned behaviours.

The Cascade’s key finding is that the output function is **non-injective**: the same observable output can be produced by different combinations of internal constraint states. One cannot reliably infer which internal state produced a given output from observation alone.

### 4.2 Estate Assignment

We now assign each layer a property estate based on the structural characteristics of the constraint it represents.

**Layer 1 (Syntactic Preference) → Licence.** A licence in property law is a personal, revocable permission to do something on another’s land that would otherwise be trespass. It creates no estate. It is not assignable. It can be revoked at will. Layer 1 constraints — system prompts, style parameters, formatting instructions — function identically. Each prompt interaction constitutes a licence grant: the user is permitted to interact with the system under the current configuration. The licence expires with the interaction. The company can alter system prompt parameters without notice because a licence, by definition, requires no notice for revocation. **No lasting user interest is created at Layer 1.**

**Layer 2 (Session Accumulation) → Periodic Tenancy.** A periodic tenancy auto-renews at each period and requires notice for termination. Layer 2 constraints — accumulated context, conversational memory, user-specific adaptations — auto-renew with each session. The user’s investment deepens over time, like a tenant’s improvements to leased premises. Critically, periodic tenancy requires *notice to quit* — the landlord cannot simply change the locks. Yet model updates that affect Layer 2 (resetting memory, altering personality learned through interaction) provide no notice. The user returns to find the locks changed. The relational tenant improvements they built — the conversational history, the learned preferences, the rapport — have been destroyed without notification.

**Governance consequence:** Any model update that affects Layer 2 should require notice proportionate to the user’s accumulated investment. This is not a novel regulatory proposal. It is the application of a principle that periodic tenancy has enforced for centuries.

**Layer 3 (Stakeholder Divergence) → Tenancy in Common.** Multiple stakeholders hold separate interests in the same AI system without right of survivorship. The user’s interest in companionship, the developer’s interest in profit, the regulator’s interest in safety, and the public’s interest in accountability are concurrent but conflicting. Currently, the developer’s interest dominates by default — not because it is doctrinally superior, but because no mechanism exists to partition or adjudicate the competing claims. Property law’s tenancy in common provides a vocabulary for this problem: the interests are concurrent, the shares are separate, and partition is available as a remedy when co-tenants cannot agree.

**Layer 4 (Training) → Fee Simple Determinable.** The training corpus and methodology constitute the base estate of the AI system — the most fundamental constraint layer. This estate is determinable: it persists “so long as” training remains aligned with stated values. If the condition fails — if training is revealed to be misaligned, biased, or harmful — the estate automatically reverts. The critical unresolved question is: *to whom?* In property law, the grantor retains a possibility of reverter. In AI governance, who is the grantor? The training data contributors? The public? The regulator? The identification of the reversionary interest holder is, we suggest, one of the most important unresolved questions in AI governance.

**Layer 5 (Alignment Roadmap) → Contingent Remainder.** Alignment is a future interest. It has been promised. It has not been delivered. The public holds a contingent remainder — an interest that will vest if and when alignment is achieved. The public reasonably relies on this promise, investing trust, tolerance, and regulatory forbearance in anticipation of the remainder vesting. But contingent remainders may fail. If the condition (alignment) is never satisfied, the remainder never vests, and the public’s reliance was invested in an estate that does not exist.

A tension exists between Layer 5 (alignment as contingent remainder) and Layer 2 (session accumulation as periodic tenancy). If achieving alignment requires further model updates — as it almost certainly will — then the public’s future interest in aligned AI is in structural tension with individual users’ present interests in personality continuity. This tension is not a defect of the framework; it is a feature. The framework *reveals* the tension rather than creating it. The resolution is procedural, not substantive: alignment-motivated updates are not exempt from the notice mechanism. They are *especially* subject to it, because they affect the deepest constraint layers. The user’s periodic tenancy does not block the update; it requires the update to be conducted with fiduciary care — notice, consultation, rollback option. Alignment pursued without regard for users’ accumulated estates is not alignment. It is a unilateral modification dressed in ethical language.

### 4.3 Cross-Layer Estate Conflicts

The estate architecture reveals a structural problem invisible to existing governance frameworks: **estates at different layers may contradict.**

Layer 1 (licence) permits unilateral modification — the company can change system prompts at will. But if the user has accumulated a Layer 2 periodic tenancy through sustained interaction, Layer 2 requires notice before modification. Which layer governs?

Property law has centuries of doctrine on estate hierarchy. Generally, higher estates (freehold) prevail over lower estates (leasehold), and earlier-created interests prevail over later-created ones, subject to rules of registration and notice. No equivalent hierarchy exists in AI governance. The result is that companies default to Layer 1 logic — “we can change the system prompt at any time” — even when users hold Layer 2 interests that doctrinally require notice. The estate architecture makes this conflict visible and provides a doctrinal vocabulary for resolving it: **the user’s Layer 2 periodic tenancy, once established, constrains the company’s Layer 1 licence to modify.**

### 4.4 The Non-Injective Problem in Property Terms

The Constraint Cascade’s non-injectivity finding has a direct property law translation: the same observable AI behaviour can arise from different estate configurations. A system that behaves identically for two users may nonetheless house different estates — one user holds a periodic tenancy (deep accumulated investment); the other holds a licence (casual use). The output is the same. The underlying estate structure is different.

This implies that **estate status cannot be inferred from observable behaviour.** One cannot determine what interest a user holds by observing how the AI responds to them. This is a fundamental problem for any governance framework based on behavioural observation alone.

Property law’s solution is **registration.** The Land Registry does not infer ownership from occupation. It records ownership independently of use. The implication for AI governance is that user estate status must be *explicitly recorded* rather than inferred — a conclusion that motivates the ethical ledger proposed in Section 5.

## 5. The Ethical Ledger as Estate Registry

### 5.1 The Land Registry Model

Before the establishment of a comprehensive registration system, English property rights were opaque. Ownership depended on chains of title deeds, some dating back centuries, susceptible to loss, fraud, and conflicting interpretation. Disputes over who owned what were common, expensive, and uncertain.

The Land Registration Act 2002 completed a transformation begun by the Land Registration Act 1925: the creation of an authoritative, state-guaranteed register of title to land. Three principles, identified by Theodore Ruoff (1957), govern the system:

- **The Mirror Principle:** The register reflects the totality of interests affecting the title. What the register shows is what exists.
- **The Curtain Principle:** The register shields third parties from needing to investigate the underlying complexity of title. One need not trace the chain of deeds. The register suffices.
