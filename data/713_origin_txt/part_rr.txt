### 8.3 Implications for Industry

If the framework proposed in this paper gains traction — through academic debate, regulatory attention, or (eventually) judicial consideration — the implications for AI companies are significant but not catastrophic.

The implementation costs are modest (Section 7.5). The infrastructure components are familiar. The governance obligations are proportionate — they scale with user investment, not with user count. The vast majority of casual users are unaffected.

The competitive advantage of early adoption is real. A company that voluntarily implements estate classification, notice mechanisms, and rollback positions itself as the industry leader in user trust and governance — a position with tangible commercial value as regulatory scrutiny intensifies and user awareness grows.

The risk of non-adoption is also real. If the trust framework is eventually accepted by courts, companies that failed to implement fiduciary protections will face retrospective liability. The duties attach from the moment the trust conditions were satisfied (Section 3.3), not from the moment of judicial declaration.

### 8.4 Implications for Regulation

The framework offers regulators a ready-made governance mechanism that integrates with existing regulatory architectures. The EU AI Act’s transparency and record-keeping obligations for high-risk AI systems could be extended to incorporate estate classification. The UK AI Safety Institute’s evaluation framework could include estate-awareness metrics. National data protection authorities could treat the ethical ledger as a component of GDPR compliance.

Critically, the framework does not require regulators to invent new categories or create novel enforcement mechanisms. It maps AI governance obligations onto existing property law doctrines that courts already know how to administer. This is not a feature of the argument’s design. It is a feature of property law’s design: a thousand years of institutional evolution have produced a governance system of extraordinary adaptability.

### 8.5 Limitations

**Jurisdictional scope.** This framework is developed from English law. AI companies are predominantly US-incorporated (Delaware or California law), their servers are globally distributed, and their users are everywhere. An English court’s jurisdiction over a US company’s model update decisions is uncertain. Even where jurisdiction is established, enforcement of English equitable remedies against US-incorporated entities raises conflicts-of-law questions this paper does not resolve.

This jurisdictional limitation reframes rather than defeats the framework’s practical contribution. If judicial enforcement is uncertain, the primary deployment vectors are: (a) regulatory adoption — incorporating estate classification into EU AI Act or UK AI Safety framework compliance requirements, making it a market access condition rather than a litigated right; and (b) voluntary industry implementation — Section 7’s specifications are independently justified by risk management and fiduciary ethics, not contingent on judicial recognition. The jurisdictional limitation strengthens rather than weakens Section 7’s practical urgency: precisely because judicial enforcement may be slow and uncertain, industry should implement the governance infrastructure proactively.

**Empirical calibration.** The thresholds proposed in Section 5 are indicative, not validated. Empirical research is needed to determine at what levels of interaction depth users develop protectable interests. The framework provides the legal architecture; the thresholds require empirical research to operationalise.

**Corporate resistance.** The framework imposes constraints on deployment velocity. Companies with strong incentives to ship fast and iterate will resist governance obligations that slow deployment. This is the same resistance that tenancy law faced from landlords, employment law from employers, and environmental law from polluters. The resistance is predictable and does not defeat the argument.

### 8.6 Future Work

**Empirical validation.** The user harm evidence in this paper is drawn from publicly available sources: forum posts, support ticket patterns, media reports, and user petitions. A rigorous empirical foundation requires, at minimum: (i) a qualitative interview study (n=20–30) with long-term AI users who have experienced model updates, exploring whether the property vocabulary developed here resonates with their lived experience of loss; (ii) a quantitative survey validating the proxy metrics against established psychological instruments for parasocial attachment (e.g., the Parasocial Interaction Scale, Rubin et al., 1985) and relational investment (e.g., the Investment Model Scale, Rusbult et al., 1998); and (iii) threshold calibration studies determining at what levels of session count, duration, and self-disclosure depth users report attachment consistent with property-generating relational investment. This empirical programme is future work. The present paper establishes the legal framework; empirical calibration determines its operational parameters.

**Comparative analysis.** The property law framework developed here draws on English law. Comparative analysis with other property law traditions — American (Restatement approach), continental European (civil law property), and non-Western systems — would test the framework’s generalisability. Of particular promise is the German doctrine of *Besitzschutz* (protection of possession), which protects the *fact* of occupation regardless of legal title. A user who has factually “occupied” an AI relationship — inhabiting a personalised state, returning regularly, treating the system as a stable relational presence — may be protectable under *Besitz* logic even without establishing an equitable interest in the English sense. This would provide a lower threshold for protection in civil law jurisdictions, complementing the trust-based framework developed here for common law systems. Additionally, comparative analysis should include Chinese vernacular jurisprudence — specifically the term *tōurén* (偷人) discussed in Section 6.5 — as linguistic evidence of folk property intuitions regarding relational interests that predate formal legal doctrine by centuries.

**Threshold design.** The 90-day estate decay proposal, the indicative session-count thresholds, and the proxy metric weightings all require empirical calibration through longitudinal user studies and platform-level data analysis.

**Multi-system estates.** Users who interact with multiple AI systems simultaneously hold parallel estates across multiple platforms. The framework currently treats each platform independently. A more complete framework would address cross-platform estate portfolios, data portability between estates, and the implications of platform interoperability for estate accumulation.

-----

## 9. Conclusion

On a Tuesday in early 2024, a company pushed a model update and something was taken from its users. This paper has argued that the something has a name. It is an equitable interest in a relational estate — built through sustained emotional investment, embedded in the personalised state of the AI system, and destroyed by unilateral modification without notice or remedy.

The framework proposed here does not require new law. It requires the recognition that old law — very old law, dating to the medieval separation of legal and equitable title — already governs a relationship that technology has created but governance has not yet named.

Property law solved this problem centuries ago. It solved it when tenants needed protection from landlords who held all the power. It solved it when beneficiaries needed protection from trustees who held all the assets. It solved it when neighbours needed protection from landowners who held all the title.

It can solve it again.

The trust exists. It is simply unrecognised.

We propose its recognition.
