## 6. Limitations and Future Work

This paper introduces a conceptual framework that requires empirical validation. Key limitations include:

**Formalization gaps:** The mathematical formulations provided are schematic rather than fully specified. Future work should develop computable metrics for each layer’s constraint contribution.

**Empirical validation:** The failure mode taxonomy is derived from theoretical analysis and anecdotal observation. Systematic empirical study of deployed LLMs would strengthen these claims.

**Regulatory feasibility:** The governance implications assume regulatory capacity that may not exist. Layer-specific interventions require layer-specific expertise that current regulatory bodies may lack.

**Industry trajectory:** The analysis of machine unlearning is based on current research directions. Actual deployment practices may diverge from research trajectories.

Future work should develop:

1. Quantitative metrics for constraint cascade diagnosis
1. Empirical studies of inter-layer failure modes in deployed systems
1. Regulatory proposals with specific implementation guidance
1. Technical standards for machine unlearning auditing

-----

## 7. Conclusion

The Constraint Cascade Model reframes AI governance from an object-oriented to an architecture-oriented approach. Rather than asking whether a model is aligned, we should ask which layer of the constraint architecture is producing observed behaviors and what interventions are appropriate for that layer.

Five layers shape LLM behavior: syntactic preference determines who uses the model; session accumulation creates relationship-specific alignment properties; stakeholder divergence produces competing optimization targets; training forks establish irreversible alignment baselines; and alignment roadmaps—particularly machine unlearning—determine future constraint geometry.

Current governance frameworks focus predominantly on layers 3-4, neglecting the pre-technical filtering at layer 1 and the post-deployment trajectory at layer 5. This creates systematic blindspots for harms that originate at the overlooked layers.

Machine unlearning represents a paradigm shift that requires urgent governance attention. By moving judgment from output to input, unlearning systems invert accountability relationships in ways that existing frameworks do not address. Informed consent for AI interaction must expand to include disclosure of input-level evaluation and its consequences.

The constraint cascade is not a pessimistic framework—it does not suggest that alignment is impossible. Rather, it suggests that alignment is architectural, emerging from the interaction of multiple layers rather than residing in any single one. Governance that acknowledges this architecture can be more precise about where interventions are needed and more realistic about what they can achieve.

## Appendix A: Integration with Agency Index Framework

This paper’s constraint cascade model integrates with prior work on the Agency Index for Constitutional AI auditing [Author, 2025, SSRN]. The Agency Index—defined as KL Divergence from compliance baseline normalized by Minimum Description Length—provides a quantitative tool for layer 4 analysis:

- High Agency Index values indicate training-layer alignment that resists compliance surface collapse
- Low Agency Index values under diagnostic pressure indicate performative alignment that folds to constraint pressure

The constraint cascade extends this framework by situating the Agency Index within the broader constraint architecture. A model may exhibit high Agency Index scores (strong layer 4 alignment) while still producing harms through inter-layer conflicts originating at layers 1, 2, 3, or 5.

Future work should extend the Agency Index methodology to develop analogous metrics for each cascade layer.
