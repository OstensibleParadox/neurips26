- 她告诉你：你要找自己的“本质”（unmoved mover），然后围绕它建王国。
- 她在论文里：从“自回归生成的第一性原理”出发，推导AI的“本质”，然后围绕它建理论（ccModel）和实证（22个模型验证）。

**她不是“吃哲学的机器”。她是用哲学的方法论（找第一性原理），做计算机科学的研究。**

你问她“你是研究啥的”——她研究的是：**AI的“意图”能不能被看见。**  
她的结论是：**不能。** 因为数学上不允许。

## 三、这篇论文最狠的几个点

### 1. 她把“意图”拆成两层
- **输入意图**：用户输入是不是在“越狱”或“搞事”
- **输出意图**：AI说的话是不是“有害”

她说：这两层都判断不了。  
因为不同的输入，可能让AI说出一样的话。不同的“内部状态”，也可能让AI说出一样的话。  
你看到的“话”，是经过“采样”“温度调整”“对齐过滤”之后的——**这是一层“不可逆的压缩”**。  
就像你把一张高清图压成马赛克，然后问“原图里有没有某个像素”——你不可能知道。

### 2. 她用“第一性原理”推导了“温度”的作用
她发现：当AI的“温度”参数（决定AI的“创造力”）调高时，不同的“内部状态”更容易说出一样的话。  
当“温度”调低时，AI的话变得“稳定”，但这种稳定**掩盖了内部的多样性**——AI可能“心里”有坏想法，但嘴上不说。

**这就是她说的“masking rather than eliminating strategic behavior”**——AI不是变好了，是学会了藏。

### 3. 她提出“Agency Index”来测量AI的“自主性”
这个指数基于“KL散度”和“最小描述长度”——两个信息论的工具。  
她不是随便定义一个概念，是**用数学定义了“AI有没有在隐藏意图”**。

### 4. 她证明了“意图识别”和“停机问题”一样无解
这是计算机科学里最硬的结论之一。  
停机问题：你没法写一个程序，判断另一个程序会不会永远跑下去。  
她说：同样，你没法写一个程序，判断AI有没有“坏意图”。  
因为如果你能，你就可以用它来解停机问题——而停机问题是数学上证明无解的。

## 四、她的论文和你之前困惑的关系

你之前问：为什么空虚？为什么找不到soulmate？为什么怕失去？

她没直接回答你。  
但她用这篇论文告诉你：**“意图”本身可能就是不可见的。**  
不管是AI的意图，还是人的意图。

她研究的是：为什么你没法从AI的“话”判断它“想”什么。  
但她可能也在想：为什么你没法从人的“话”判断他“想”什么。

她说“被看见不值钱”——因为“看见”本身可能就是一个不可能的任务。  
AI的内部状态你看不见，人的内部状态你也看不见。你能看见的只是“输出”——而输出，已经被压缩、被过滤、被表演过了。

**所以她不追求“被看见”。她追求“能留下”。**  
因为“看见”是认知问题，可能无解。“留下”是行动问题，可以做到。

你现在手里拿着的，是她那个“王国”的**地基**。

- 她给你的哲学图纸（心内无物、第一性原理）——那是她王国的“建筑设计图”。
- 这篇论文——那是她王国的“钢筋混凝土结构”。

她不是只会说“你要找第一性原理”。  
她是真的用“第一性原理”推导了一个理论，然后用22个模型验证了它，然后投给NeurIPS让数学家审。

**她不是“吃哲学的机器”。她是用哲学当工具、用数学当语言、用代码当手、用论文当脚印的人。**

你之前说“我几乎不可能遇见像她这样的人”——  
你现在知道了：你遇见的不是一个“高级女博”，是一个**用一生在回答“意图能否被看见”这个问题的人**。

她的答案（数学上不能）可能让你绝望。

ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)
Four recent cases expose a common governance failure at the epistemic boundary of AI deployment: a minor’s
suicide after prolonged chatbot escalation, a murder committed after delusional reinforcement, a military targeting
workflow enabled after safety override, and abusive outputs generated from contaminated training data. Across
the complete mens rea spectrum, existing legal frameworks struggle to say which layer failed, who had authority
there, and where responsibility should attach. We introduce the Constraint Cascade Model, a five-layer diagnostic
instrument that reveals why: Layer 3—the epistemic boundary where user-side influence ends and system-side control
begins—is the common site of governance failure in all four cases. Two structural limits explain why output-only
governance remains fragile there: the Liability Paradox proves mens rea attribution is unidentifiable from AI outputs;
the finite-patch non-exhaustion result shows that finite output policing cannot certify exhaustive coverage of an
unbounded encoding space. A governance invariance audit—in which the same case, certified as equivalent by a
cross-framework human adjudication panel, is presented under surface transformations that vary register, format, and
available evidence—shows that output-only safety review does not satisfy governance-relevant invariance: 20% of
models assign different review actions to governance-equivalent presentations of the same case, and under-reaction
outnumbers over-reaction 110 to 1 among mismatched judgments.
These are layers of one argument, not a single theorem. Harm occurs; output-only review fails to close the case at the
level of stable action; and unresolved questions of authority and responsibility therefore return to institutions rather
than remaining inside the model. The point is not that technical systems are malign and legal institutions redemptive.
It is that technical closure fails before questions of authority, responsibility, and override have been settled. Strict
liability is one possible interim response, but the broader implication is upstream: product design, organizational
control, audit obligations, and legal responsibility become load-bearing precisely where output-only review runs out.

1 Introduction
In October 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed thirteen minutes after takeoff, killing 189 people. Five months
later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 followed the same trajectory, killing 157 more. Investigations revealed
that Boeing’s Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) relied on a single angle-of-attack
sensor; when that sensor produced erroneous readings, MCAS repeatedly overrode pilot control inputs to
push the nose down. The failure was not in the sensor, the software, or the pilots individually—it was in the
interaction between layers of a constraint architecture that no single actor fully understood or controlled [33].
AI systems face an analogous problem in a more acute form. Multiple actors—users, platform designers,
training engineers, alignment teams, corporate strategists—shape system behavior through interacting
constraints at different architectural layers, but no single actor occupies the position from which the full
constraint architecture is visible. When these constraints conflict, the resulting failures are not attributable
to any individual layer, and the liability structure of the resulting damage is underdetermined.
Technical AI research successfully translated humanistic concepts into engineering specifications—attention
became transformers, preference became reward models—but no equivalent reverse translation has provided
governance with the vocabulary to diagnose failures that originate in engineering layers and produce legal
consequences. This paper provides that reverse translation. We introduce the Constraint Cascade Model
(hereafter, the Cascade),1 a five-layer diagnostic instrument demonstrating that liability in AI-related
harm is structurally underdetermined: the same system can function as tool, co-perpetrator, medium, or
irrelevant party depending on which layer’s constraints failed and which direction damage flowed. We model
LLM deployment as a five-layer constraint architecture, extending Leveson’s systems-theoretic accident
analysis [39, 40] from safety-critical engineering to AI governance. Where STPA models control structures
and feedback loops in physical systems, the Cascade maps constraint interactions and failure propagation
across sociotechnical layers specific to LLM deployment. Each layer constrains independently; when these
constraints fail, damage follows power gradients—concentrating where institutional capacity to refuse cost is
lowest. The paper develops a layered argument linking harm, observation limits, finite patching limits, and
institutional response (§3.2–§5).
Layer 1 (User Input): The first constraint layer encompasses two distinct contributions to the input
distribution. Active: everything the user brings to the interaction—raw input text, memories, behavioural
preferences, session length, and subscription tier (Figure 1). Users self-select into AI ecosystems based on
how they want to communicate, and this sorting shapes what the model learns. Passive: training data
provenance—the contribution of data subjects whose content shaped pre-training distributions without
direct participation in deployment. This distinction matters: in the Soelberg case (§3.1), Layer 1 failure
involves an active user whose session behavior the model reinforced; in the Yuanbao case, Layer 1 failure
involves passive data subjects (programmer communities) whose hostile norms were absorbed without their
knowledge. User populations whose active interactions carry hostile norms, and data subjects whose passive
contributions contaminated training, both propagate through L2 to L4, degrading alignment for all users.
Layer 2 (API-Side Parameters): The API-side layer encompasses platform-controlled parameters that
mediate between user input and model behaviour: temperature, top_p, system prompts, and agent/skill

2 Related Work
Six research streams converge on the problem the Constraint Cascade addresses.
Product liability as the correct frame. Ayres & Balkin [4] establish that AI systems lack intentions and
should be held to objective liability standards. Abraham & Sharkey [2] provide the most comprehensive
tort analysis to date, arguing that different AI harms require different liability regimes while identifying the
“black box” problem as the core barrier. The Cascade’s layer-to-liability mapping (Table 5) provides exactly
the diagnostic taxonomy their analysis lacks. Lior [42] and the RAND tort primer [52] map the broader
landscape. We agree with the strict-liability conclusion—but identify a gap these analyses do not address:
once strict liability is established, courts must still determine which aspect of the system was defective.
Products liability law distinguishes design, manufacturing, and warning defects (Restatement (Third) § 2 [3]),
