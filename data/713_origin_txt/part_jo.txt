The Constraint Cascade reconceptualizes AI behavior as
emergent from constraint interactions across five distinct
layers, spanning pre-technical user dynamics through post-
deployment corporate trajectory. Each layer imposes restric-
tions on the space of possible outputs, with constraints prop-
agating bidirectionally across the architecture. Crucially, the
layers are not uniformly technical: Layer 1 operates at the
market level before any model constraint applies; Layer 5 op-
erates at the industry level after deployment. This heterogene-
ity is essential to capturing failure modes that model-centric
governance systematically misses.

retraining from an earlier checkpoint.

The Liability Paradox: A Formal Impossibility
Result
We formalize the incompatibility between criminal law’s theo-
retical foundations and AI architectural realities as an impos-
sibility result in the tradition of computational complexity
theory. The Liability Paradox demonstrates that intent at-
tribution from behavioral observation is undecidable for AI
systems—not merely difficult, but structurally impossible
given the information available to external observers.

Theorem 1 (Mens Rea Undecidability). Let

• 𝑄 = set of internal cognitive states

Then: No finite sequence of input-output observations can de-

mens rea attribution is undecidable.

injectivity).
(2) No temporal gap provides intermediate observables
(threshold collapse).
(3) Therefore, no finite observation sequence can determine
internal state.
(4) Mens rea attribution requires internal state determina-
tion.
(5) Therefore, mens rea attribution is undecidable.

diagnose which layer failed, not what the system intended.

(2) AI systems collapse graduated thresholds (output = actus

(3) AI internal states are computationally indistinguishable

This is not a policy recommendation but a structural diag-

The Monte Carlo–Empirical Discrepancy:
Exposing Paradigmatic Assumptions
The Promise of a Two-Dimensional Phase Space. Current AI
governance frameworks operate on an implicit model of sys-
tem behavior. Alignment research assumes that AI outputs
can be characterized along two primary dimensions: the de-
gree to which the system has internalized intended objectives
(alignment, or directional bias 𝛽) and the degree to which
stochastic factors introduce deviation from those objectives
(noise 𝜂). This two-dimensional paradigm underlies regulatory
proposals treating “aligned” versus “misaligned” as a mean-
ingful binary, liability frameworks assuming failures result
from either inadequate training or unpredictable interference,
and technical benchmarks evaluating models against safety
criteria as if alignment were a stable, measurable property.
We constructed a Monte Carlo simulation to make this im-
plicit paradigm explicit—to build what Cartwright (1999)
calls a “nomological machine” that demonstrates what gover-
nance frameworks assume AI behavior looks like. The simu-
lation is not a hypothesis to be tested but an instantiation
of the paradigm’s logic. If the two-dimensional model ade-
quately captures AI behavioral dynamics, the simulation’s
predictions should align with empirical observation. If they
diverge, the paradigm itself is inadequate.
