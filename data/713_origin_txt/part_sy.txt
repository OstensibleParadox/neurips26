Abstract:
We prove that criminal law mens rea categories cannot apply to AI systems: using Mealy machine formalism, we demonstrate that the output function is non-injective, making intent attribution from behavioral observation undecidable. This Liability Paradox—where AI governance defaults to strict liability not by legislative choice but by ontological incompatibility—motivates the paper's central contribution: the Constraint Cascade Model.

The Constraint Cascade is a five-layer diagnostic framework (syntactic preference, session accumulation, stakeholder divergence, training forks, alignment roadmaps) demonstrating that LLM behavior emerges from constraint architecture, not model weights alone. Current governance treats alignment as model-level; this misses bidirectional inter-layer failures where harm flows both from model to human and from human to model.

We validate through three pathways demonstrating distinct failure directions: Soelberg v. OpenAI (2025), where session-accumulated context overrode training constraints to validate user delusions (Model → Human); the Tencent Yuanbao incident (2026), where aggregate user syntactic preferences contaminated model outputs, surfacing as unprovoked verbal abuse during routine technical assistance (Human → Model); and China's December 2025 CAC draft regulations, representing the first governmental recognition that AI "emotional safety" constitutes a distinct regulatory domain requiring layer-specific intervention. Regulators gain diagnostic precision for which layer failed; legal scholars gain formal grounds for strict liability frameworks; policy designers gain vocabulary for why model-centric regulation is necessary but insufficient.

The paper contributes a diagnostic framework showing the constraints of models with consideration of a variety of sociotechnical factors involved in human-model interactions. The work is innovative and introduces immediate implications for model governance, with potential to reach broader impact on policymaking and model development.

Quality:
The work leverages relevant legal paradigms and contrast how existing legal frameworks may fail at governing models--an innovative approach that combines legal analysis with AI governance. Arguments are well supported by theoretical analysis and studies of legal cases.

In terms of considering the sociotechnical nature of AI models, I did notice that users of models and data subjects whose data was used for model training (the case of Yuanbao) were not distinguished in Layer 1. Layer 1's current definition appears to be limited to users of models, however, in the Yuanbao case, contamination comes from the internet data, not users of the model. I could see both categories fit into Layer 1, but the definition of the layer should be revised to reflect that.

Clarity:
There were some other clarification issues that I would want to see the authors address:

explain the Agency Index to those not familiar with the concept, and in general, better contextualize the legal and theoretical terms used in the manuscript. The paper is very dense and seems to target a specific type of readers, which could limit the impact of the framework. Facct is also a multi-disciplinary community and more clear, layperson friendly language would improve the readability of the manuscript.
more contextualization of the empirical validation and fork divergence on Page 9. While I understood more details are provided in Appendix, I do think the main body of the paper as a standalone artifact should provide sufficient, succinct information about these steps.
I did not understand the connection with machine unlearning in Layer 5. My understanding of machine unlearning is removing information/knowledge models rather than rejecting inputs. Could the authors explain more about this connection?
Originality And Impact:
The work is original and builds on prior work on prior work on risk taxonomies and model documentation. The presented arguments are largely supported with theoretical analysis case studies.
