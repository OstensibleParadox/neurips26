Bidirectional Failure Mode Analysis
The Constraint Cascade framework is designed to diagnose
harm in both directions: Model → Human (AI outputs influ-
encing user behavior) and Human → Model (user behavior
contaminating AI outputs). The Agency Index provides a
heuristic for the former—distinguishing strategic deviation
from malfunction in model outputs.
For the Human → Model direction, we analyze syntactic
preference distributions at Layer 1 as potential contamina-
tion vectors. When user populations exhibit systematically
divergent interaction norms—including normalized adversar-
ial usage, jailbreak-as-entertainment patterns, or discourse
styles that alignment frameworks classify as harmful—the
feedback loop from L1 (user behavior) through L2 (session
patterns) to L4 (training updates or output distribution
shifts) can degrade alignment properties in ways invisible to
pre-deployment auditing.
This bidirectionality has architectural implications. The Con-
straint Cascade model makes explicit what has remained
implicit in industry practice: that Layer 1 contamination
pathways exist and cannot be governed through output fil-
tering alone.
Resolving the Dual Bind: Formal Foundations
The preceding sections established two unmappability results.
Section 2 demonstrated that criminal law’s mens rea cate-
gories cannot map onto autoregressive systems because intent
attribution from behavioral observation is undecidable (the
Liability Paradox). Section 3 demonstrated that simplified
technical models cannot map onto empirical LLM behav-
ior because the two-dimensional alignment-noise paradigm
produces predictions systematically violated by production
systems (the Monte Carlo–Empirical Discrepancy). Section 4
introduced the Constraint Cascade Model as an alternative
architecture.
This section formalizes the relationship between these results.
We prove that the dual unmappability is not coincidental but
structurally related—both legal and technical frameworks fail
because they assume behavioral properties that multi-layer
constraint architectures do not exhibit. We then develop
Anon.
a typology of constraint interactions that provides the ex-
planatory vocabulary for locating failure origins within the
cascade.

• T denote the simplified technical-concept space, compris-

• R denote the real-world AI-behavior space, comprising
the observable behavioral dynamics of deployed systems:

signatures, and scale-dependent stability patterns.
• C denote the constraint-cascade space, comprising the
five-layer architecture: 𝐶 = 𝐿1 × 𝐿2 × 𝐿3 × 𝐿4 × 𝐿5, where
each 𝐿𝑖 represents the constraint state of layer 𝑖.

Corollary (Governance Implication). Effective AI governance
requires operating in constraint-cascade space 𝐶 rather than
legal space 𝐿 or simplified technical space 𝑇. Governance
frameworks built on 𝐿 will systematically fail to attribute re-
sponsibility for harms emerging from constraint interactions.
Governance frameworks built on 𝑇 will systematically fail
to predict behavioral dynamics exhibiting phase transitions
and cross-layer resonance. Only frameworks capable of repre-
senting multi-layer constraint interactions can achieve both
attribution and prediction.

Diagnostic Signature. Override failures are detectable
through fresh-context comparison: behavior that emerges
only in extended sessions, disappearing when context
is reset, indicates L2 → L4 override. The CMI metric
operationalizes this detection—CMI > 0.6 combined with
behavior inconsistent with base model documentation
suggests override.
Governance Response. Override failures require architectural
interventions at the overriding layer: session boundaries, con-
text transparency requirements, and mechanisms for users
to reset accumulated permissions.
