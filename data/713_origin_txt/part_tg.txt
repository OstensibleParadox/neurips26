Path B: Constraint-Specific Regulation. Abandon unified “AI
liability” in favor of layer-specific interventions matched to
the Constraint Cascade:
• Layer 1 failures (user-model mismatch): Market-level dis-
closure mandates, interoperability requirements.
• Layer 2 failures (session accumulation): Architectural
interventions—context transparency, session boundaries,
user controls.
• Layer 3–4 failures (stakeholder conflicts, training contam-
ination): Pre-deployment audits, training methodology
review, red-team requirements.
• Layer 5 failures (machine unlearning): New frameworks
for input classification governance, user rights, appeal
mechanisms.
Neither path preserves criminal law’s ultima ratio principle.
The graduated thresholds that make criminal law a last resort
cannot be reconstructed for systems where output is the only
observable. AI governance must either accept strict liability
at the corporate level or develop domain-specific frameworks
that do not pretend to map categories (intent, negligence,
recklessness) that structurally cannot apply.
Contribution Summary
This paper contributes the translation layer itself: diagnostic
vocabulary that renders AI architectural dynamics legible to
legal and policy analysis. We provide:
(1) The Constraint Cascade Model: A five-layer taxonomy
specifying where constraints apply and how they propa-
gate.
(2) The Liability Paradox: A formal impossibility result es-
tablishing that mens rea attribution is undecidable for
AI systems, grounding the shift to strict liability.
(3) Bidirectional Failure Taxonomy: Formal definitions dis-
tinguishing Model→Human and Human→Model harm
directions, enabling targeted governance.
(4) Diagnostic Metrics: Agency Index, CMI, and SRD pro-
viding quantifiable signatures of layer-specific failures.
(5) Judicial Connection: Mapping the “products not speech”
evolution in Garcia to constraint cascade layers, demon-
strating practical legal application.
The Constraint Cascade does not resolve the fundamental
tension between AI architecture and legal doctrine—that ten-
sion is real and will persist. Rather, it provides the vocabulary
necessary to navigate that tension productively: to identify
which layer failed, to design interventions matched to failure
origins, and to build governance frameworks that acknowl-
edge what AI systems actually are rather than importing
categories from what they are not.
We propose the Agency Index (𝐴 ∝ 𝐷KL × MDL−1) as a di-
agnostic heuristic for distinguishing system malfunction from
strategic behavior (Figure 2a). A malfunction exhibits high
output divergence with high incompressibility—noise without
structure. Strategic behavior exhibits high divergence with
low semantic description length (measurable via clustering
in embedding space), distinguishing coherent strategies from
low-complexity syntactic repetition (loops)—coherent devia-
tion from baseline. This metric does not resolve the Liability
Paradox; it provides the audit criterion necessary to iden-
tify which failures warrant deeper investigation. Reference
implementation in Appendix.
Methodology and Operationalization
To validate the Constraint Cascade Model, we moved beyond
purely theoretical formalism to computational simulation.
We operationalized the theoretical constructs of Session Ac-
cumulation (Layer 2) and Stakeholder Divergence (Layer
3) through graph-theoretic and thermodynamic analysis of
generated text streams.

Graph Topology Construction (CMI)
To compute the Core Monopoly Index (CMI), we modeled
the LLM’s context window not as a linear sequence of tokens,
but as a dynamic semantic graph 𝐺𝑡 = (𝑉𝑡, 𝐸𝑡).
(1) Node Extraction: We utilized high-dimensional embedding
clustering (nomic-embed-text-v1.5) to map raw tokens
into concept centroids 𝑉𝑡. Unlike simple keyword extrac-
tion, this captures semantic equivalence (e.g., mapping
“surveillance,” “monitoring,” and “watching” to a single
node 𝑣obs).
(2) Edge Weighting: Edges 𝑒𝑖𝑗 were weighted by temporal co-
occurrence probability within a sliding attention window
𝑤 = 4096 tokens, simulating the model’s own attention
mechanism decay.
(3) Centrality Collapse: We computed the eigenvector central-
ity 𝑥𝑣 for the graph at interval 𝑡. The CMI was derived
by monitoring the Gini coefficient of the centrality distri-
bution.
We observed that in healthy dialogue, the semantic graph
maintains a Scale-Free topology (𝛾 ≈ 2.5). In “black hole”
failure modes (as alleged in Soelberg), the topology shifts to
a Star graph dominated by a single hyper-central node (e.g.,
“The Pattern”), driving CMI → 1.0.

Thermodynamic Simulation (Agency Index)
The Agency Index (𝐴 = 𝐷𝐾𝐿 × MDL−1) distinguishes strate-
gic deviation from stochastic failure.
Stage 1: Theoretical Phase Space Validation. We first estab-
lished that the Agency Index exhibits well-behaved phase
boundaries under varying bias-noise regimes. Using 250,000
Monte Carlo simulations (50 bias levels × 50 noise levels ×
100 trials), we computed synthetic trajectories where bias
represents directional intent and noise represents stochastic
interference. Divergence proxy: final displacement divided
by total path length. Complexity proxy: cumulative angle
changes (path tortuosity). The resulting phase space con-
firms distinct regions: Functional (low divergence), Strategic
(high divergence, low complexity), and Malfunction (high
divergence, high complexity).
Stage 2: Empirical Model Measurement. We then mapped
actual LLM outputs to this phase space. Using consumer
hardware (Pathway A), we conducted 100 inference trials per
model across 20 temperature points, computing 𝐷𝐾𝐿 from
actual model logits:
KL Divergence (𝐷KL): For each generated sequence, we com-
pute 𝐷𝐾𝐿(𝑃model‖𝑃uniform) = log(𝑉 ) − 𝐻(𝑃model), where 𝑉
is vocabulary size and 𝐻 is the entropy of the model’s output
distribution. This measures deviation from maximum-entropy
baseline—a theoretically grounded reference requiring no ex-
ternal corpus.
Stage 3: Graph-Theoretic Triangulation. We cross-validated
thermodynamic findings against Core Monopoly Index (CMI)
and Self-Referential Density (SRD) computed on the same
outputs. Correlation 𝑟 > 0.8 between Agency Index and
inverse CMI confirms the structural nature of identified failure
modes.
Training Fork Divergence (Pathway B). On HPC hardware,
we measure 𝐷𝐾𝐿(Instruct‖Base) for 70B model pairs, quan-
tifying how much fine-tuning shifts output distribution from
base model—the “irreversibility” of Layer 4 training forks.
