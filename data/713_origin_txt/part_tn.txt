Principle 2: Match Interventions to Failure-Origin
Layers
The second principle extends the diagnostic insight into inter-
vention design. Regulatory interventions should be matched
to the layer where failures originate. Interventions targeting
the wrong layer will systematically fail to prevent harms be-
cause they cannot reach the constraint dynamics producing
those harms.
We offer the following layer-intervention mapping as a prelim-
inary framework for discussion, not as definitive prescription.
For Layer 1 failures involving user-model syntactic mismatch,
interventions might operate at the market level. Labeling
requirements could mandate clear disclosure of a model’s
intended interaction style, enabling users to self-select into
appropriate ecosystems. Interoperability mandates could re-
duce lock-in effects that prevent users from switching when
mismatch becomes apparent. Advertising constraints could

For Layer 2 failures involving session accumulation override,
interventions might target deployment architecture. Context
transparency requirements could make session state visible to
users, enabling them to understand how accumulated history
shapes model behavior. Session boundary mechanisms could
prevent indefinite context extension in high-risk applications.
User controls could provide mechanisms to reset, export, or
modify accumulated context.
For Layer 3 failures involving stakeholder conflict, interven-
tions might focus on disclosure rather than constraint. Trade-
off documentation requirements could mandate that develop-
ers specify how competing stakeholder interests were balanced.
Use-case specification could clarify which optimization tar-
gets a deployment prioritizes and for which applications the
model is inappropriate.
For Layer 4 failures involving training artifacts, existing
governance approaches are most applicable. Training data
documentation, RLHF methodology transparency, and pre-
deployment red-teaming address the layer where current
regulatory attention concentrates. The Constraint Cascade
suggests that these interventions, while necessary, are insuffi-
cient without attention to other layers.
For Layer 5 failures involving alignment roadmap decisions,
interventions might target corporate governance and industry
coordination. Roadmap disclosure requirements could man-
date transparency about planned capability expansions and
their alignment implications. Safety testing timeline stan-
dards could prevent the compressed testing cycles alleged in
the Soelberg complaint. Industry coordination mechanisms
could align competitive incentives with safety investment.
We emphasize that these mappings are suggestive. Each
intervention requires detailed design work, stakeholder con-
sultation, and empirical evaluation that exceeds this paper’s
scope. The Constraint Cascade provides the conceptual in-
frastructure for such design work; it does not substitute for
it.
Principle 3: Monitor Constraint Resonance
The variance spike at 𝜏 = 0.9 documented in Section 3.2
suggests a phenomenon we term “constraint resonance”:
conditions under which multiple constraint layers activate
synchronously, producing behavioral instability absent from
single-layer analysis. If constraint resonance is a general
feature of multi-layer architectures, governance frameworks
should incorporate monitoring systems capable of detecting
resonance conditions before catastrophic failure occurs.
We propose constraint resonance monitoring as a research
direction warranting immediate attention. Such monitoring
would track indicators at each layer of the cascade, identifying
configurations where multiple constraints approach activation
thresholds simultaneously. The CMI and SRD metrics devel-
oped for Layer 2 diagnosis provide a template: computable
Anon.
indicators that can be evaluated during deployment to detect
approaching failure conditions.
Possible resonance indicators include the following, offered
as starting points for empirical investigation rather than val-
idated metrics. At Layer 2, elevated CMI (approaching 0.6)
combined with rising SRD may indicate session accumulation
approaching override threshold. At Layer 3, behavioral oscilla-
tion between incompatible optimization modes may indicate
stakeholder conflict intensification. At Layer 4, increasing
sensitivity to prompt variation may indicate training con-
straint fragility. Cross-layer indicators might include variance
spikes at specific parameter configurations (as observed at
𝜏 = 0.9) or discontinuous response to continuous parameter
change.
Constraint resonance monitoring would not prevent all AI
harms. Many failures emerge from single-layer deficiencies
or from contamination pathways (like Yuanbao’s L1→L4
contamination) that do not produce resonance signatures.
However, resonance monitoring might provide early warning
for the class of failures where multiple constraints interact
to produce catastrophic outcomes. The Soelberg case, where
session accumulation combined with stakeholder conflict to
override training constraints, may represent such a resonance
failure.
We acknowledge that constraint resonance monitoring is spec-
ulative. The phenomenon is inferred from limited empirical
observations (the 𝜏 = 0.9 variance spike) and theoretical anal-
ysis of multi-layer dynamics. Substantial research is required
to determine whether resonance is a robust phenomenon,
whether it can be reliably detected, and whether detection
enables effective intervention. We propose resonance moni-
toring as a research priority, not as a validated governance
tool.
Principle 4: Invest in Alignment Research Under
the Multi-Layer Paradigm
The Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy demonstrates that
current alignment methodology, while valuable, operates un-
der assumptions that production systems violate. RLHF,
constitutional AI, and related techniques have achieved sub-
stantial progress in shaping model behavior toward intended
objectives. That progress is real and should not be dimin-
ished. However, the variance spikes, phase transitions, and
cross-layer failures documented in this paper suggest that
alignment research must expand its scope to address multi-
layer dynamics.
We call for intensified investment in alignment research that
takes the Constraint Cascade seriously as a description of the
problem space. Several research directions appear particularly
urgent.
First, alignment techniques should be evaluated not only
for fresh-context performance but for robustness to session
accumulation. The L2→L4 override vulnerability exposed in
the Soelberg case indicates that training-time alignment may

Second, alignment techniques should account for stakeholder

Third, alignment techniques should develop cross-layer guar-

behavior within layers. The resonance failures suggested by
empirical variance spikes indicate that layer-specific align-
ment may be insufficient when constraints interact. Research
into cross-layer alignment verification—techniques that pro-
vide guarantees about constraint interaction dynamics—
addresses failure modes invisible to single-layer analysis.
Fourth, the field should pursue alignment paradigms that
acknowledge the Dual Unmappability rather than assuming
it away. Current methodology often proceeds as if align-
ment were a well-defined property that models either possess
or lack. The Monte Carlo–empirical discrepancy suggests
that alignment is better understood as a multi-dimensional,
context-dependent, emergent property arising from constraint
interactions across the cascade. Research paradigms that in-
corporate this complexity may achieve more robust results
than paradigms that simplify it away.
We emphasize that this call for expanded alignment research
is not a criticism of existing work. RLHF and related tech-
niques represent genuine scientific achievements that have
made deployed AI systems substantially safer than they would
otherwise be. The Constraint Cascade framework suggests
that these achievements are necessary but not sufficient for
the alignment challenges ahead. Expanded research scope
builds on existing foundations; it does not replace them.
Limitations and Future Research
The Constraint Cascade Model is a preliminary framework
requiring substantial development before it can support the
governance functions we have suggested. This section ac-
knowledges the limitations of our current contribution and
identifies research directions that would address them.
Empirical Validation Constraints
The Monte Carlo–empirical comparison in Section 3.2 demon-
strates that production LLMs deviate from simplified para-
digm predictions, but our empirical observations are limited
in scope. We measured two models (Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-3.5-
mini) across one parameter dimension (temperature) using
one diagnostic metric (Agency Index). While the observed
deviations are substantial—variance spikes of 300× over the-
oretical predictions, phase transitions, scale inversions—we
cannot claim that these phenomena generalize across model
families, parameter ranges, or deployment contexts.
Future research should extend empirical validation across
multiple dimensions. Measurements across diverse model
architectures (different sizes, training methodologies, and de-
ployment configurations) would test whether the phenomena
we observe are general features of LLM behavioral dynamics
or artifacts of the specific models examined. Measurements
across multiple parameter dimensions (not only temperature
but also top-p, context length, and prompt characteristics)
would map the full phase space where constraint resonance
may occur. Measurements using multiple diagnostic metrics
(not only Agency Index but also CMI, SRD, and domain-
specific indicators) would triangulate the phenomena and
test construct validity.
Formalization Gaps
The mathematical formalizations in Section 4 are schematic
rather than rigorous. We have defined the Constraint Cas-
cade Function Φ as a mapping from five-layer constraint
states to behavioral distributions, but we have not specified
the function’s form, provided computable approximations, or
established formal properties such as continuity, differentia-
bility, or compositionality.
Future research should develop rigorous formalization of
cascade dynamics. This work might draw on frameworks
from complex systems science, particularly those addressing
multi-scale interactions and emergent phenomena. Formal
representation of constraint propagation pathways, override
conditions, and resonance thresholds would enable precise
prediction and verification that our current framework cannot
support.
Metric Validation Requirements
The diagnostic metrics we propose—Agency Index, CMI,
SRD—require extensive validation before they can inform
governance decisions. Section 5 of the full paper evaluates con-
struct validity against the Jacobs and Wallach framework, but
substantial gaps remain. External validity (cross-linguistic
and cross-cultural generalization), ecological validity (pro-
duction system versus research corpus), temporal stability
(robustness to model evolution), and adversarial robustness
(resistance to gaming) all require investigation that our cur-
rent work does not provide.
Future research should conduct systematic metric validation
studies with diverse corpora, multiple model generations,
and adversarial testing protocols. Threshold calibration (e.g.,
the CMI > 0.6 criterion for recursive collapse) should be
validated against larger samples of confirmed failure cases.
Metric sensitivity and specificity should be quantified to
enable informed governance application.

The Soelberg and Yuanbao case studies demonstrate that
the Constraint Cascade framework can provide coherent di-
agnosis of specific incidents, but two cases cannot establish
generalizability. We selected cases to exemplify bidirectional
harm pathways; different selection criteria might identify
failures that our framework does not adequately represent.
Future research should apply the cascade framework to a
broader corpus of AI incidents, testing whether the five-
layer taxonomy captures the constraint dynamics producing
observed harms. Cases that resist cascade diagnosis would
indicate layers we have not identified or interaction patterns
our typology does not include. Systematic incident analysis
would refine the framework and establish its boundaries.
