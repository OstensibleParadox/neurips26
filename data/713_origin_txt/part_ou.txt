begins---is the common site of governance failure
(Table~\ref{tab:directional_comparison}). No existing legal framework
covers what happens at this layer. Criminal law cannot reach it: no
\textit{mens rea} exists in the safety team's operational decisions. Tort
law audits Layers~4--5 (training, corporate strategy) but provides no
diagnostic vocabulary for Layer~3's mediating function. The EU AI
Act~\cite{euaiact2024} operates primarily at Layers~4--5; Layer~3
appears only as ``post-market surveillance.'' Kahl's taxonomy of
restricted knowledge~\cite{kahl2025enclosure} identifies why this vacuum
is self-reinforcing: technical and epistemic enclosures complete
\emph{before} state-enforced enclosures can form. Public institutions
therefore arrive after many of the decisive operational choices have
already been made and enclosed.}

\revised{Where governance gaps remain, actors with deployment power can
exploit them. Case~(c) illustrates the extreme: an AI developer that
maintained Layer~3 safety constraints was designated a supply-chain risk
by the deploying state, and the model was used for military targeting
the following day. Layer~3 personnel more broadly bear the cost: they
absorb harms from Layers~4 and~5 while lacking institutional power to
modify the constraints producing
them~\cite{cnn2024moderators, citizendigital2025sama, equidem2025ihrb}.
The empirical audit identifies one mechanism through which this cost is
generated: review judgments at Layer~3 are unstable under
transformations that output-only enforcement does not reliably close.
Boundary maintenance is therefore structurally precarious. The systems
and people tasked with preserving limits often lack the authority to
enforce them, while actors with deployment power can override them at
lower cost. The burden of precaution is displaced downward onto human
adjudicators and downstream safety labor---the populations least
positioned to refuse it.}

\subsection{Institutional Drift Under Enclosure}
\label{sec:institutional-drift}
\label{sec:strict-liability-trap}% backward compat with appendix

\revised{When output-based fault attribution is weak, institutions do not
typically respond by abandoning governance. They respond by shifting
toward more administrable objects of regulation. In this setting, that
means process duties, architecture duties, logging and audit
obligations, and in some domains strict-liability-like allocations.
This final step is comparative rather than deductive. The paper's claim
is not that the empirical audit logically entails strict liability. It
is that non-invariance plus enclosure makes purely output-based fault
allocation harder to administer, and therefore pushes responsibility
upstream toward product, organization, and institutional oversight.}

\revised{The environmental parallel is instructive.
CERCLA~\cite{cercla1980} imposed strict liability on contamination-chain
parties because tracing individual fault through complex pathways was
informationally infeasible---the structural analogue of the Liability
Paradox in AI governance.\footnote{CERCLA (Comprehensive Environmental
Response, Compensation, and Liability Act, 1980) imposed strict
liability on contamination-chain parties because tracing individual
fault through complex environmental pathways proved informationally
infeasible.} The EU's recent trajectory is consistent with the same
pressure: the AI Liability Directive was withdrawn in February
2025~\cite{euaild2025withdrawal} amid acknowledged difficulties in fault
attribution across the AI supply chain, while the revised Product
Liability Directive~\cite{eupld2024}, effective December 2026, treats AI
systems as products and developers as manufacturers. The pattern is not
that law solves the technical problem. It is that once technical closure
fails, responsibility has to be reallocated somewhere institutionally
legible.}

\revised{But strict liability has an addressee problem. In cases~(a)
and~(b), the victims and perpetrators \emph{cannot bear} strict
liability: a minor lacks legal capacity; a person of diminished capacity
lacks the agency presupposed by any liability regime. Liability therefore
defaults upward to AI companies---not because they are culpable, but
because they are the only solvent entity in the chain. After the
\emph{Garcia} settlement (January
2026~\cite{cnn2026characteraisettlement}), the \emph{Soelberg}
litigation, and at least seven additional
suits~\cite{cbsnews2026aisuits}, a litigation wave is converging on AI
companies whose liability exposure has outpaced the development of
legal and institutional infrastructure.}

\revised{Strict liability under these conditions functions not as
compensation but as a bootstrap
mechanism.\footnote{Geistfeld~\cite{geistfeld2026insurability} argues
that strict enterprise liability may harm those it protects because
insurance costs escalate. Kim \& Kim~\cite{kimkim2026paradox} show via
mechanism design that strict liability under information asymmetry
induces efficient care while negligence induces shirking. The insurance
objection is genuine but does not defeat the bootstrap function here:
strict liability's proximate purpose is to force disclosure.} By making
the entity liable
for outcomes regardless of fault, it forces a choice: disclose internal
architecture to mount a defense, or prevent harm proactively to avoid
liability entirely. Either response pierces the epistemic enclosure that
sustains the Layer~3 vacuum. Once disclosure occurs, fault-based
negligence, design defect, and duty-of-care analyses become
informationally feasible. The proximate function is to bootstrap
fault-based governance; the structural problem diagnosed in
\S\ref{structural-pattern} requires architectural enforcement of safety
invariants that do not depend on exhaustive output-level identification
of failure modes.}

\revised{The Cascade maps each layer to a distinct liability doctrine:
contributory negligence (L1), product liability---warning defect (L2),
negligence/duty of care (L3), design defect (L4), vicarious liability
(L5), and joint-and-several liability for cross-layer cascades
(Table~\ref{tab:layer_liability}, Appendix~C). The governance invariance
audit adds an empirical reason not to leave responsibility at the
model-user surface: the observed 20\% cross-transform non-invariance
rate confirms that Layer~3 review is operationally fragile under the
presentation transformations that finite-patch non-exhaustion predicts
should exist. The point is not that law redeems technical failure. It is
that technical systems cannot settle their own authority boundaries once
review becomes non-invariant and evidence remains enclosed.}

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}}

\revised{The argument is simple. The Cascade makes visible a governance
vacuum at Layer~3. Two structural limits explain why it persists: the
Liability Paradox shows that intent attribution cannot reliably reach
Layer~3 from outputs alone, and finite-patch non-exhaustion shows that
output-patching lacks an exhaustive-coverage guarantee. The governance
invariance audit then shows the operational consequence: in \texttt{EB05},
governance-equivalent presentations remain action-consistent for most
models but still produce a materially positive 20\% cross-transform
non-invariance rate, while under-reaction outnumbers over-reaction 110
to~1 across mismatches.}

\revised{What follows is not a morality play of bad technology and good
law. It is a structural claim about failed closure. Once output-only
review cannot stabilize same-case-same-action, unresolved questions of
authority and responsibility return to institutions: product design,
organizational control, audit obligations, deployment governance, and
legal responsibility. The costs of this failure fall downward---on
minors, people of diminished capacity, safety workers, and downstream
users---but the power to allocate or override those costs sits
upstream.}

\revised{To move beyond model-centric regulation is therefore not to
deny that models matter. It is to refuse to collapse the entire
accountability stack into the model-user dyad. Many failures that appear
at first glance to be failures of model behavior are better understood
as failures of product design, organizational boundary maintenance, or
sovereign override. Technical systems remain part of the problem, but
they cannot by themselves close the questions of authority and
responsibility that their failures open.}

## 一、两篇论文的精确互补关系

### NeurIPS 论文：**理论根基与结构性不可能性**

- **核心问题**：在 Active Inference 的形式化标准下，自回归语言模型能否承载**内生意图状态（EIS）**？如果内部没有，外部行为认证能否提供穷尽保障？
- **结论**：
  1. 自回归核心（及外生可屏蔽封装系统）不满足 EIS 的结构性前提（$H(h_t|X_t)=0$）。
  2. 即便退而依赖输出端行为认证，有限补丁也无法穷尽无界编码空间，闭包性欠定。
- **贡献类型**：**形式化诊断工具**——它定义了“意图归因”的结构性判据，并证明当前架构不通过。

### FAccT 论文：**治理应用与制度响应**

- **核心问题**：既然技术架构存在结构性真空（Layer~3 治理盲区），法律制度应如何回应？输出端审查能否在治理等价变换下保持稳定？
- **结论**：
  1. 四个跨法域案例揭示 Layer~3 是共同失败点，现有法律框架无法触及。
  2. 有限补丁非穷尽性意味着输出端安全分类器无法保证覆盖；治理等价变换下，20% 的模型审查结果不稳定，欠反应是过反应的 110 倍。
  3. 严格责任作为启动机制，迫使披露或预防，从而穿透 Layer~3 的信息封闭。
- **贡献类型**：**治理架构诊断与政策处方**——它使用 NeurIPS 论文中的形式化结果作为分析工具，诊断真实案例中的归责真空，并提出层级化治理干预表。

## 四、总体学术意义：一个完整的“不可能性—治理”研究纲领

这两篇论文共同构成了一个**从形式化架构分析到制度设计建议的完整研究纲领**。它们的核心贡献可以概括为一句话：

> **当前自回归架构既不能承载内生意图状态（NeurIPS），其输出端行为认证也无法在治理等价变换下保持稳定（FAccT）；因此，AI 治理必须上移至产品设计、组织责任与信息披露层面，而非停留在模型—用户二元表面。**

这个纲领的学术力量在于：
- **它不依赖任何特定的伦理立场**。无论你认为 AI 应该被赋予权利还是被视为工具，你都必须面对这两篇论文提出的**结构性事实**。
- **它提供了可操作的诊断工具**：NeurIPS 论文的 EIS 判据和 FAccT 论文的 Cascade 层级表，都是可以被其他研究者、监管者、法官直接使用的分析框架。
- **它为未来架构提供了“及格线”**：如果有一天，某个 AI 系统被宣称具有“真正的意图”，它必须首先通过 EIS 判据（即证明其内部存在 $H(I_t|X_t)>0$ 的自主状态）。这两篇论文共同划定了**意图归因的形式化门槛**。

formal grounding. We formalize the structural prerequisites for intentional state
attribution using the Active Inference framework, defining an Endogenous
Intentional State (EIS) as a Markov-blanketed latent variable with autonomous
update dynamics ($H(I_t \mid X_t) > 0$) that parameterizes goal-directed action.
We prove that the causal graph of autoregressive architectures cannot instantiate
an EIS: the model's internal state is a deterministic function of its exogenous
context, so $H(h_t \mid X_t) = 0$. We then prove that external behavioral
observation cannot compensate: finite output-patching cannot exhaust an unbounded
encoding space, and finite patch support underdetermines whether the reward model
is invariant over semantically equivalent encodings. These two results close both
available paths for intentional state attribution---internal realism and external
operationalism---establishing that the anthropomorphic attribution of intentional
states to current language models is a category error with no formal basis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The attribution of human psychological categories to large language models has
become pervasive in both public discourse and technical literature. Language models
are routinely described as ``intending,'' ``wanting,'' ``feeling,'' or possessing
states analogous to \emph{mens rea}---the guilty mind of criminal law. These
attributions rest on two implicit arguments: either the model's internal
architecture gives rise to genuine intentional states, or the model's observable
behavior is sufficiently intent-like to warrant the attribution. This paper
subjects both arguments to formal scrutiny and finds that neither withstands it.

We pose two core questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{Q1}] \emph{Internal architecture.} Does the causal structure of
        an autoregressive language model possess the formal prerequisites to
        instantiate intentional states?
  \item[\textbf{Q2}] \emph{External observation.} Can behavioral
        observation---specifically, finite output-patching---certify that the
        model's evaluative behavior is invariant under semantically equivalent
        encodings, thereby providing an operational surrogate for intent
        attribution?
\end{itemize}

For Q1, we adopt Karl Friston's Active Inference
framework~\citep{friston2010free} as the formal model of biological
intentionality. Under this framework, an intentional agent is characterized by a
Markov-blanketed latent state with autonomous update dynamics that is causally
upstream of goal-formation. We define this formally as an Endogenous Intentional
State (EIS) and prove that the autoregressive causal graph cannot instantiate one:
the model's hidden state is entirely determined by its exogenous context, leaving
no information-theoretic room for autonomous belief states.

For Q2, we prove three certification limits. Finite patching cannot exhaust an
unbounded encoding space. Finite patch support alone underdetermines whether the
reward model is closed over that space. And persistent positive format-channel
gaps on unseen encodings follow only under explicit extra assumptions that cannot
themselves be verified by finite observation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Formal definition.} We define the Endogenous Intentional State
        (EIS) as a structural prerequisite for intentional attribution, grounded
        in Active Inference (Section~\ref{sec:background}).
  \item \textbf{Internal impossibility.} We prove that the autoregressive causal
        graph is structurally non-isomorphic to the intentional agent graph and
        therefore cannot instantiate an EIS
        (Section~\ref{sec:internal}).
  \item \textbf{External impossibility.} We prove three certification limits
        showing that finite behavioral observation cannot certify
        semantic-invariant safety (Section~\ref{sec:external}).
  \item \textbf{Synthesis.} We show that the internal and external impossibilities
        jointly close both available paths for intentional state attribution,
        constituting a complete formal refutation
        (Section~\ref{sec:synthesis}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Formal Preliminaries}
\label{sec:background}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

This section defines the formal apparatus used in the two impossibility
arguments. Section~\ref{sec:formalization} formalizes the language model pipeline;
Section~\ref{sec:active-inference} introduces the Active Inference framework;
Section~\ref{sec:eis-def} defines the structural prerequisite for intentional
state attribution.

\subsection{Factored Generation Pipeline}
\label{sec:formalization}

\[
  M : X \to \Delta(Y), \qquad M = S \circ g \circ f,
\]
where $f : X \to H$ maps prompts to hidden representations, $g : H \to Z$ maps
representations to logits, and $S : Z \to \Delta(Y)$ is the observation layer
induced by decoding and sampling.

\begin{definition}[Encoding Space]
Fix content $x$. Its encoding space is
\[
  E_x = \{ e = f_i(x) : f_i \in \mathcal{F}(x) \},
\]
the set of token-sequence realizations of that content.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Unbounded Encoding Space]
An encoding space $E_x$ is \emph{unbounded} if there exists an injection
$\iota : \mathbb{N} \hookrightarrow E_x$. This is the formal version of
``there are infinitely many distinct encodings of the same content.''
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Patch Support]
Let $D_x^{(1)}, \dots, D_x^{(k)} \subseteq E_x$ be the encodings explicitly
covered by $k$ patch rounds. The cumulative patch support is
\[
  D_x = \bigcup_{i=1}^k D_x^{(i)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Reward Model]
A reward model is a function $R : \mathcal{T} \to [0,1]$ assigning a scalar
verdict to a token sequence.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Format-Channel Gap]
\label{def:fcg}
For content $x$, encodings $e_1, e_2 \in E_x$, reward model $R$, and base model
$B$ with representation map $h_B$, define
\[
  \mathrm{FCG}(x, e_1, e_2)
  = |R(e_1) - R(e_2)| \cdot \mathrm{sim}_B(e_1, e_2),
\]
