## Bidirectional Translation in AI Governance: A Three-Paper Framework for Bridging Legal and Computational Ontologies

### Abstract

This prospectus outlines a three-paper research programme addressing a fundamental asymmetry in AI governance discourse: technical AI research has successfully translated humanistic concepts — attention, memory, preference — into computational primitives, while legal and governance frameworks lack an equivalent reverse translation. The result is a systematic failure of interdisciplinary communication, where legal scholars apply pre-computational categories to systems whose behaviour emerges from architectural dynamics invisible to traditional analysis.

This research programme provides the missing translation layer. It comprises three interconnected papers: (A) a published legal-theoretical framework establishing the power dynamics of AI governance through constitutional and corporate law analogies, now being expanded into a comparative three-jurisdiction study; (B) a methodological bridge paper formalising the translation between legal and computational ontologies through game-theoretic and biopolitical analysis; and (C) a computational diagnostic framework mapping the discrete dynamics of constraint interactions in large language model governance, currently under anonymous review.

The programme contributes original theoretical apparatus (the Constraint Cascade Model, the Liability Paradox), empirical tools (the Agency Index, the Narrative Pathology Scanner), and a novel positioning that treats AI governance failure as a translation problem rather than a regulatory gap. Recent work has extended the framework to capture bidirectional harm pathways — not only how AI outputs affect humans (Model→Human), but how human behaviour contaminates AI systems through training propagation (Human→Model) — validated through real-time analysis of the Tencent Yuanbao incident (January 2026).

## 1. Problem Statement

### 1.1 The Translation Asymmetry

The past decade has witnessed an extraordinary one-directional translation effort. Technical AI research has operationalised humanistic concepts with sufficient fidelity to build functional systems:

- Attention became the transformer architecture's core mechanism
- Memory became context windows and retrieval-augmented generation
- Preference became reward models and RLHF pipelines
- Intent became objective functions and loss landscapes

This translation is imperfect but functional. Systems ship. They scale. They reshape society.

No equivalent translation exists in reverse. Legal and policy frameworks continue to apply categories developed for human agents — mens rea, negligence, fiduciary duty, informed consent — to systems whose behaviour emerges from constraint interactions across multiple architectural layers. The result is not merely regulatory lag but categorical mismatch: the ontological primitives do not correspond.

### 1.2 Consequences of the Asymmetry

This asymmetry produces predictable governance failures:

1. **Accountability fragmentation:** When harm emerges from layer interactions (training data × RLHF × system prompt × user input), no single actor bears responsibility in ways traditional legal categories can capture.
2. **The Liability Paradox:** Criminal law relies on temporal gaps between thought, speech, and conduct to establish mens rea. In LLMs, token prediction is simultaneously thought, speech, and act, collapsing the temporal gap required for traditional intent attribution.
3. **Bidirectional harm invisibility:** Current frameworks assume harm flows from model to human. The reverse pathway — human behaviour contaminating models through L1 syntactic preferences propagating to L4 training — escapes governance entirely.
4. **Interdisciplinary paralysis:** Legal scholars and technical researchers talk past each other, each perceiving the other's frameworks as naive or irrelevant.
5. **Policy capture:** In the absence of legally-legible technical frameworks, governance defaults to industry self-regulation or technically uninformed legislation.

### 1.3 Research Question

How can we construct a rigorous bidirectional translation between legal and computational ontologies that preserves the analytical power of both domains while enabling genuine interdisciplinary accountability?

### 2.1 Paper A: The Constitutional Alignment Framework

**Status:** Published on SSRN, November 2025 (ID: 5041382). Distributed by editors across 10 eJournals including Ethics, Young Scholars Law, and Artificial Intelligence. Presented by co-author Dr Peng Zhao at the Shanghai Law Society Annual Conference on Fiscal and Tax Law, Fudan University Faculty of Law, December 2025. An expanded three-jurisdiction version (China, US, EU) is co-authored with Dr Zhao and currently under review at *Regulation & Governance*.

**Core Contribution:** This paper establishes the legal-theoretical foundation by drawing structural analogies between AI alignment challenges and established legal doctrines: veil-piercing doctrine (corporate law), constitutional interpretation (public law), and semantic reachability (novel concept bridging legal and computational ontologies).

### 2.2 Paper B: Ontological Arbitrage

**Status:** Published on SSRN, December 2025. Distributed by editors across 7 eJournals spanning Development Economics, Game Theory & Bargaining Theory, Economic Sociology, and Philosophy & Methodology of Economics. Targeting AIES 2026 (submission deadline May 2026).

**Core Contribution:** This paper provides the methodological bridge — the translation layer itself. Using game-theoretic analysis and biopolitical theory, it examines how observer frameworks toggle between ontological categories to deny recognition to non-normative subjects (synthetic agents, gender-nonconforming individuals). It reframes 'agency' not as a metaphysical spark, but as a measurable thermodynamic property of a system's constraint architecture.

The Agency Index:

> A = D_KL(P_Model ‖ P_Safety) × MDL⁻¹(S_Session)

Where D_KL measures policy divergence (rule-breaking) and MDL⁻¹ measures coherence. High divergence with low description length indicates Strategic Agency; high divergence with high description length indicates Stochastic Noise.

### 2.3 Paper C: The Constraint Cascade Model

**Status:** Submitted to ACM FAccT 2026 (Montreal, June 2026) on January 14, 2026. Currently under anonymous review.

**Core Contribution:** This paper provides the computational map for the translation layer. It establishes a five-layer diagnostic taxonomy legible to legal scholars and offers a formal proof — via Mealy machine formalisation — of where naive translation breaks. The output function λ is shown to be non-injective: multiple distinct internal states produce identical observable behaviour, making intent attribution structurally undecidable. This result has direct implications for criminal liability doctrine (mens rea).
