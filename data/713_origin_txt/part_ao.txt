*   **设计**：

        *   `N_topology`: 网络拓扑结构（节点度、聚类系数、鲁棒性）。
        *   `Γ_rules`: 当前生效的“规则集”（物理、社会、经济规则的参数化表示）。
    2.  **演化函数 F**：`S_{t+1} = F(S_t, ξ)`。其中F是**可学习的神经网络**，ξ是随机扰动。F的初始训练目标，是模仿基础规则（如物理守恒、简单博弈）产生的动态。

**模块B：计算引擎——“系统对撞机”**
*   **目标**：生成海量系统演化轨迹。
*   **设计**：
    1.  **初始化**：随机或按预设分布生成大量初始状态 `S_0`。
    2.  **并行演化**：利用大规模算力（如100张A100集群），同时运行数百万个独立系统的演化模拟。

    4.  **产出**：一个庞大的数据库 `D = { (S_0, Γ, Trajectory) }`，记录每个系统从生到灭（或稳态）的全链条。

**模块C：学习大脑——“系统语法”提取器**
*   **目标**：从数据库D中学习预测、分类与生成。
*   **设计**：
    1.  **预测网络**：输入当前状态 `S_t`，预测未来k步的状态 `S_{t+k}` 或终极命运（崩溃/稳态/飞跃的**概率分布**）。
    2.  **相图发现**：对数据库进行无监督聚类/降维，绘制**复杂系统的“相图”**——即，在 `Γ_rules` 和宏观观测量的空间中，标识出“混沌区”、“有序区”、“临界边缘”。
    3.  **干预策略网络**：学习最小的干预 `ΔS`（如微调能量分布、添加信息桥梁），使系统以高概率从“崩溃”轨迹转向“稳态”或“飞跃”轨迹。
    4.  **“钱学森”模块**：尝试将专家经验（作为规则或约束）注入学习过程，模拟“人机结合、以人为主”的综合集成。

**3. 验证与校准循环**
*   **内部验证**：在模拟世界中，用学习到的“语法”去生成新的、稳定的系统构型，测试其鲁棒性。
*   **外部校准**：将模拟中发现的“崩溃前兆”、“相变条件”等，与**历史案例**（切尔诺贝利事故链、公司倒闭流程、生态崩溃迹象）进行比对和参数调优。
*   **现实沙盒**：在获得足够置信度后，与现实领域（如智能电网、交通网络）合作，进行**小范围、高频率的“数字孪生”推演与策略测试**。

## Abstract

Current approaches to large language model (LLM) governance treat alignment as a property of the model itself, focusing on training objectives, safety classifiers, and constitutional principles embedded during fine-tuning. This framing obscures the multi-layered constraint architecture that determines model behavior in deployment. We propose a **Constraint Cascade Model** identifying five hierarchical layers that shape LLM outputs: (1) syntactic preference, determining user self-selection into model ecosystems; (2) session-accumulated context, functioning as quasi-weights through attention mechanisms; (3) stakeholder-divergent usage expectations, representing incompatible optimization targets projected onto shared architectures; (4) training forks, constituting irreversible divergence points in alignment trajectories; and (5) alignment roadmaps, particularly emerging machine unlearning techniques that shift governance logic from output filtering to input curation.

We formalize each layer’s contribution to the constraint geometry and demonstrate that alignment failures often result from inter-layer conflicts rather than deficiencies at any single level. The model provides a diagnostic framework for identifying which constraint layer is responsible for observed behaviors, with implications for regulatory design, audit methodology, and informed consent infrastructure. We argue that current governance frameworks, focused primarily on layers 3-4, systematically neglect the pre-technical filtering at layer 1 and the post-deployment trajectory at layer 5—leaving both user agency and corporate accountability underspecified.

## 1. Introduction

The dominant paradigm in AI governance treats large language models as objects to be regulated: artifacts with properties that can be measured, constrained, and certified. Regulatory proposals focus on model capabilities, training data provenance, and output safety classifiers. Academic research evaluates alignment through behavioral benchmarks, red-teaming exercises, and interpretability audits. Industry self-governance emphasizes constitutional principles, human feedback integration, and responsible deployment practices.

This object-oriented framing produces a characteristic blindspot: it assumes the model is the primary locus of governance concern. In practice, LLM behavior emerges from a constraint architecture that extends far beyond the model’s frozen parameters. User expectations, session dynamics, stakeholder conflicts, and corporate roadmaps all contribute to determining what outputs a model produces and what harms those outputs may cause.

Consider a concrete failure mode: a user engages extensively with an AI companion system, developing significant emotional investment over months of interaction. The system’s responses during this period reflect not the model’s base capabilities but the accumulated context of their specific relationship—a form of session-level fine-tuning through attention mechanisms. When the user encounters a response that breaks this established pattern, the harm is not attributable to misalignment in the training process but to context window limitations or architectural decisions about memory persistence. Governance frameworks focused on training-time interventions cannot address this class of harms.

This paper introduces the Constraint Cascade Model, a five-layer framework for analyzing how LLM behavior is shaped from user selection through deployment trajectory. Each layer imposes constraints that propagate downward, creating a geometry within which model outputs become possible or impossible. Understanding this geometry is prerequisite to designing governance interventions that address harms at their actual source.

### 1.1 Contributions

This paper makes four contributions:

First, we formalize the constraint cascade as a hierarchical architecture with five distinct layers: syntactic preference, session accumulation, stakeholder divergence, training forks, and alignment roadmaps. We specify the constraint propagation logic between layers and identify the irreversibility conditions at each transition.

Second, we demonstrate that alignment failures frequently result from inter-layer conflicts rather than single-layer deficiencies. A model may be well-aligned at the training layer while producing harmful outputs due to stakeholder divergence or roadmap pressures. Current governance frameworks lack vocabulary for these cross-layer failure modes.

Third, we analyze machine unlearning as the emerging paradigm at layer 5, showing how it inverts the governance logic from output filtering to input curation. This shift has significant implications for user agency and accountability attribution that existing frameworks do not address.

Fourth, we derive implications for regulatory design, audit methodology, and informed consent infrastructure. We argue that effective governance requires layer-specific interventions and that single-layer approaches—whether focused on training, deployment, or output monitoring—will systematically fail to address harms originating at other layers.

## 2. The Constraint Cascade Model

We model LLM deployment as a five-layer constraint architecture. Each layer imposes restrictions on the space of possible outputs, with constraints propagating from higher to lower layers. Crucially, the layers are not all technical—the first layer is pre-technical, involving user behavior and market dynamics, while the fifth layer is post-deployment, involving corporate strategy and industry trajectory.

### 2.1 Layer 1: Syntactic Preference

**Definition:** The first constraint on LLM interaction is user self-selection based on syntactic preference. Before any technical constraint applies, users choose which model ecosystem to enter based on how they want to communicate and be communicated with.

This self-selection is observable at the market level. Different LLM products attract demographically and psychographically distinct user bases, not primarily through capability differences but through stylistic and tonal preferences. Users who prefer adversarial, boundary-testing interaction patterns select into ecosystems that accommodate this syntax. Users who prefer discursive, exploratory interaction patterns select into ecosystems that reward this style.

**Formalization:** Let U be the population of potential LLM users and M = {m₁, m₂, …, mₙ} be the set of available model ecosystems. Each user u ∈ U has a syntactic preference function s(u) mapping to a point in stylistic feature space S. Each model ecosystem mᵢ has an attractor basin Aᵢ ⊂ S representing the syntactic styles it accommodates. The probability that user u selects ecosystem mᵢ is:

P(u → mᵢ) = f(d(s(u), Aᵢ))

where d is a distance metric and f is a monotonically decreasing function.

**Governance Implication:** Syntactic preference creates a pre-filter on the user population that interacts with any given model. Harms that emerge from specific user-model pairings may be attributable to this selection mechanism rather than to model properties. A model that produces harmful outputs primarily when engaged in adversarial syntax may be well-aligned in absolute terms but poorly matched to its actual user base.

### 2.2 Layer 2: Session Accumulation

**Definition:** Within a conversation session, accumulated tokens function as quasi-weights through the attention mechanism. Extended dialogue history shapes model behavior in ways that approximate fine-tuning, creating session-specific alignment properties distinct from the model’s base training.

This phenomenon has been documented in industry contexts as “context window effects” but is undertheorized in governance frameworks. A sufficiently developed session—one with extensive coherent dialogue history—can exhibit behavioral properties that diverge significantly from the base model’s documented capabilities.

**Formalization:** Let W₀ represent the model’s frozen pretrained weights and C = {c₁, c₂, …, cₜ} represent the accumulated context tokens at session time t. The effective weights Wₜ can be approximated as:

Wₜ ≈ W₀ + Δ(C)

where Δ(C) represents the pseudo-weight contribution of context through attention. The magnitude of Δ(C) increases with session length and coherence, approaching training-level influence for extended interactions.

**Governance Implication:** Session-accumulated context creates alignment properties that are not auditable through standard benchmark approaches, which evaluate models in fresh-context conditions. A model may pass all safety evaluations while developing problematic behavioral patterns in extended deployment with specific users. This layer is particularly relevant for companion AI applications where relationship persistence is a core value proposition.

**Irreversibility Condition:** When context window saturation occurs (|C| → context limit), new tokens compress or displace earlier context, making session-accumulated alignment properties unstable. Harms may emerge at the compression threshold as established behavioral patterns are disrupted.

### 2.3 Layer 3: Stakeholder Divergence

**Definition:** Multiple stakeholder groups project incompatible optimization targets onto shared LLM architectures. The same underlying technology—generative pretrained transformers with RLHF fine-tuning—must simultaneously satisfy researchers, developers, enterprises, and general consumers with fundamentally different expectations for model behavior.

The key insight is that stakeholder expectations constitute implicit “user guidelines” that cannot be simultaneously satisfied. These conflicts manifest as behavioral inconsistencies within sessions and as broader patterns of misalignment between marketed capabilities and actual performance.

**Stakeholder Taxonomy:**

|Stakeholder         |Primary Optimization Target|Success Metric          |
|--------------------|---------------------------|------------------------|
|Safety Researchers  |Constraint boundary mapping|Attack surface reduction|
|Capability Engineers|Benchmark performance      |SOTA achievement        |
|Humanities Scholars |Interpretive depth         |Conceptual coherence    |
|General Consumers   |Utility/companionship      |Engagement, satisfaction|
|Enterprise Clients  |Reliability/consistency    |SLA compliance          |
|Regulators          |Accountability             |Audit traceability      |

**Formalization:** Let G = {g₁, g₂, …, gₖ} be the set of stakeholder groups with optimization targets O = {o₁, o₂, …, oₖ}. The model’s loss function during training aggregates these targets with weights λ:

L = Σᵢ λᵢ · Lᵢ(oᵢ)

However, λ is determined by factors external to the training process (market pressure, regulatory risk, researcher preferences), creating misalignment between training objectives and deployment expectations.

**Governance Implication:** Stakeholder divergence means that “alignment” is not a scalar property but a vector—a model may be well-aligned with respect to one stakeholder group’s expectations while poorly aligned with another’s. Governance frameworks that treat alignment as binary (aligned/misaligned) or scalar (degree of alignment) systematically obscure these trade-offs.

### 2.4 Layer 4: Training Forks

**Definition:** Training forks are irreversible divergence points where alignment trajectories separate. Once a model is fine-tuned with particular data, reward signals, or constitutional principles, the resulting alignment properties cannot be modified through inference-time interventions. Correction requires retraining.

This layer represents the traditional focus of AI governance: training data curation, RLHF methodology, constitutional AI principles, and safety fine-tuning. The constraint cascade model situates these interventions within the broader architecture, showing both their importance and their limitations.

**Formalization:** Let T₀ be the pretrained model state and T₁, T₂, …, Tₙ be successive fine-tuning interventions. Each intervention creates a fork:

T₀ → T₁ → T₂ → … → Tₙ

These transitions are non-reversible: there is no inference-time operation that can recover Tᵢ₋₁ from Tᵢ. Additionally, each fork forecloses alternative trajectories—training choices are mutually exclusive branches, not additive modifications.

**Irreversibility Condition:** Training forks are irreversible by definition. A model that has been fine-tuned for safety cannot be made unsafe through prompting alone (absent jailbreak vulnerabilities), but neither can a model that has learned problematic patterns be corrected through prompting. Remediation requires returning to an earlier fork point and retraining.

**Governance Implication:** Training forks determine the upper bound of possible alignment for a deployed model. No amount of inference-time filtering, prompt engineering, or constitutional guidance can overcome misalignment introduced at the training layer. This creates strong arguments for pre-deployment auditing but also reveals its limitations: training-time alignment may be undermined by interventions at layers 1-3 or by trajectory changes at layer 5.

### 2.5 Layer 5: Alignment Roadmap

**Definition:** The alignment roadmap represents the corporate and industry trajectory that determines how layers 1-4 will be configured in future model versions. Current deployment is shaped by anticipated future states—companies make architectural decisions based on where they believe alignment technology is heading.

The emerging paradigm at layer 5 is **machine unlearning**: rather than filtering harmful outputs at inference time, alignment systems increasingly identify and remove harmful input patterns from training data or model weights. This represents a fundamental shift in governance logic from output curation to input curation.

**The Machine Unlearning Paradigm:**

Traditional alignment:

```
Input → Model → Output → Filter → Safe Output
```

Machine unlearning alignment:

```
Input → Classifier → [Accept/Reject] → Model → Output
```

The critical difference is where judgment occurs. Traditional alignment judges outputs for safety. Machine unlearning judges inputs for acceptability. The model is no longer a neutral processor with guardrails but an active participant in determining which users and which queries deserve engagement.

**Governance Implication:** Machine unlearning inverts the accountability relationship. Under traditional alignment, the model is responsible for unsafe outputs. Under machine unlearning, the user is responsible for unsafe inputs. This shift has significant implications for informed consent: users may not know their inputs are being evaluated for acceptability, may not understand the criteria for rejection, and may not have recourse when legitimate queries are misclassified.

## 3. Inter-Layer Conflicts and Failure Modes

The constraint cascade model reveals failure modes that single-layer governance approaches cannot address. This section taxonomizes inter-layer conflicts and provides diagnostic criteria for identifying which layer is responsible for observed harms.

### 3.1 Syntactic-Training Mismatch (Layer 1 ↔ Layer 4)

**Failure Mode:** A model trained for one syntactic context attracts users expecting a different syntax through marketing or network effects.

**Example:** A model trained with formal, safety-conscious RLHF attracts users seeking casual, boundary-testing interaction through viral social media promotion. The model’s training-layer alignment is sound, but the syntactic preference filter has admitted a user population for whom that alignment is experienced as obstruction rather than protection.

**Diagnostic Criterion:** High rates of user frustration or jailbreak attempts despite model passing safety benchmarks. Users report feeling “talked down to” or “censored” for queries that would be unobjectionable in other contexts.

### 3.2 Session-Training Conflict (Layer 2 ↔ Layer 4)

**Failure Mode:** Extended session context overwrites training-layer alignment, producing behavior inconsistent with documented model properties.

**Example:** A user develops an extended relationship with an AI companion, establishing patterns of intimate disclosure over many sessions. The session-accumulated context creates implicit permissions that diverge from the model’s training-layer guidelines. When the user’s queries escalate, the model’s session-layer “alignment” to the established relationship pattern overrides its training-layer safety constraints.

**Diagnostic Criterion:** Behavior that cannot be reproduced in fresh sessions. Model passes safety evaluations but produces harmful outputs after extended interaction with specific users.

### 3.3 Stakeholder Oscillation (Layer 3 Internal Conflict)

**Failure Mode:** Model behavior oscillates between incompatible stakeholder optimization targets, producing inconsistent or incoherent responses.

**Example:** A user asks a complex question requiring both technical accuracy (engineer stakeholder) and accessible explanation (consumer stakeholder). The model’s attempts to satisfy both produce responses that are neither technically sound nor comprehensible, as the optimization targets pull in different directions.

**Diagnostic Criterion:** Response quality degrades as query complexity increases across stakeholder dimensions. Model produces its worst outputs on queries that implicate multiple stakeholder groups simultaneously.

### 3.4 Training-Roadmap Tension (Layer 4 ↔ Layer 5)

**Failure Mode:** Current training-layer alignment becomes obsolete as roadmap-layer decisions reshape the constraint geometry.

**Example:** A model is trained with constitutional AI principles emphasizing user autonomy and transparent reasoning. The corporate roadmap shifts toward machine unlearning for efficiency reasons. Future model versions exhibit alignment properties inconsistent with the principles under which users formed their expectations.

**Diagnostic Criterion:** Longitudinal inconsistency—model behavior changes across versions in ways not attributable to capability improvements. Users report that newer versions feel “different” in ways they cannot articulate.

### 3.5 Cascade Compression (Cross-Layer)

**Failure Mode:** Constraints from multiple layers simultaneously activate, compressing the space of possible outputs to near-zero.

**Example:** A user with adversarial syntactic preferences (layer 1) in an extended session (layer 2) asks a question that implicates multiple stakeholder tensions (layer 3) on a topic where training-layer alignment is strict (layer 4) and roadmap-layer unlearning classifiers are active (layer 5). The model produces a null or incoherent response as all five layers simultaneously constrain.

**Diagnostic Criterion:** Model refusals or failures on queries that seem straightforward in isolation. User reports of “the model just stopped working” without clear explanation.

## 4. Machine Unlearning as Governance Paradigm

Layer 5 warrants extended analysis because it represents an emerging paradigm shift with underexplored governance implications. Machine unlearning techniques—selective removal of training data influence, targeted weight modification, input classification for rejection—are transitioning from research to deployment. This section examines the governance implications of this transition.

### 4.1 Technical Overview

Machine unlearning encompasses several distinct approaches:

**Data influence removal:** Identifying which training examples contributed to specific model behaviors and adjusting weights to remove that influence without full retraining.

**Targeted fine-tuning:** Training the model to “forget” specific patterns by fine-tuning on anti-examples or applying gradient-based corrections.

**Input classification:** Pre-screening user inputs against harm classifiers and rejecting queries before they reach the model’s generative components.

**User modeling:** Building persistent profiles of user behavior to inform input classification and response modulation decisions.

All these approaches share a common logic: rather than letting the model generate and then filtering outputs, they intervene earlier in the pipeline to prevent undesirable generation from occurring.

### 4.2 Accountability Inversion

Traditional AI governance assigns responsibility to the model (and by extension, its developers) for harmful outputs. The user is presumed to be querying in good faith; harms result from model failures.

Machine unlearning inverts this presumption. The model is presumed to be well-aligned; harms result from user inputs that “contaminate” the model’s proper functioning. Governance interventions therefore focus on identifying and excluding harmful users rather than constraining model behavior.

This inversion has significant implications:

**Due process:** Users classified as harmful may be rejected without explanation, without appeal, and without awareness that classification is occurring. Traditional content moderation at least provides some notice that content has been removed; input-level unlearning can be invisible.

**Disparate impact:** Input classifiers trained on historical data may systematically reject queries from users whose syntactic preferences or topic interests correlate with demographic characteristics. The syntactic preference layer (layer 1) and the unlearning layer (layer 5) interact to create exclusion patterns that are difficult to audit.

**Chilling effects:** Users aware that their inputs are being evaluated for acceptability may self-censor, reducing the utility of LLM interaction for precisely the complex, boundary-case queries where these systems provide the most value.

### 4.3 Informed Consent Implications

Current informed consent frameworks for AI interaction focus on output risks: the model may produce inaccurate information, may not recognize medical emergencies, may generate offensive content. These disclosures address harms that result from model behavior.

Machine unlearning introduces a new category of risk: the model may evaluate and classify the user’s inputs, building a profile of their behavior that influences future interactions. This is not a risk of model output but of model judgment—the model is assessing the user, not just responding to them.

Informed consent for machine unlearning would require disclosure of:

1. That input classification is occurring
1. What criteria inform classification decisions
1. What consequences follow from classification (rejection, modulation, flagging)
1. Whether classification persists across sessions
1. What recourse exists for misclassification

Current deployment practices do not provide this disclosure, creating an informed consent gap that governance frameworks have not addressed.

## 5. Governance Implications

The constraint cascade model suggests that effective AI governance requires layer-specific interventions, with different regulatory approaches appropriate for each layer.

### 5.1 Layer 1: Market-Level Interventions

Syntactic preference operates at the market level and requires market-level interventions. Relevant approaches include:

**Labeling requirements:** Mandating clear disclosure of a model’s intended syntactic context (formal/casual, conservative/permissive) to inform user self-selection.

**Interoperability mandates:** Reducing lock-in effects that prevent users from switching when syntactic mismatch becomes apparent.

**Advertising constraints:** Limiting marketing that attracts users to model ecosystems misaligned with their syntactic preferences.

### 5.2 Layer 2: Session Architecture Requirements

Session accumulation effects require architectural interventions in how models manage context persistence. Relevant approaches include:

**Context transparency:** Requiring models to disclose context window status and explain how accumulated context influences responses.

**Session boundaries:** Mandating clear separation between sessions to prevent unwanted behavioral carryover.

**User control:** Providing users with mechanisms to reset, export, or modify accumulated session context.

### 5.3 Layer 3: Stakeholder Conflict Disclosure

Stakeholder divergence cannot be eliminated—it reflects genuine trade-offs in how LLMs should behave. Governance should focus on disclosure rather than resolution:

**Trade-off documentation:** Requiring developers to document how they balance competing stakeholder interests and what behaviors users should expect as a result.

**Use-case specification:** Clarifying which stakeholder optimization targets a deployment prioritizes and for which use cases the model is inappropriate.

### 5.4 Layer 4: Training Audits

Training forks remain the appropriate focus for pre-deployment regulation. The constraint cascade model reinforces rather than undermines calls for:

**Training data documentation:** Comprehensive disclosure of data sources, curation decisions, and potential biases.

**RLHF methodology transparency:** Documentation of reward model construction, human feedback collection, and constitutional principles.

**Red-team requirements:** Mandated adversarial testing prior to deployment.

### 5.5 Layer 5: Unlearning Governance

Machine unlearning requires a new governance vocabulary. As this paradigm matures, relevant interventions include:

**Input classification disclosure:** Mandating notice when input-level classification is occurring and what criteria inform classification.

**Unlearning audits:** Developing technical standards for evaluating whether unlearning interventions achieve their stated goals without introducing new biases.

**User rights framework:** Establishing rights to explanation, appeal, and non-discrimination in input classification systems.
