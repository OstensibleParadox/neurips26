# Legal research brief for AI governance veil-piercing paper

**This memo provides Yale Law Review-quality doctrinal analysis of five cases, three statutory frameworks, and the current landscape of elite legal scholarship on AI corporate accountability.** The research supports a paper using a corporate veil-piercing analogy for AI accountability with three triggers: autonomy-control conflation, profit-driven manipulation, and performative alignment. Each section provides sufficient detail for 2–3 page case studies with proper Bluebook citations, deep reasoning analysis, and connections to the broader framework.

---

## I. Cases mapped to the three veil-piercing triggers

### A. Autonomy-control conflation: ICO v Clearview AI

**Citation:** *The Information Commissioner v Clearview AI Incorporated (Privacy International intervening)* [2025] UKUT 319 (AAC), Appeal No. UA-2024-001563-GIA (6 October 2025). **Note: the citation [2025] UKUT 85 (AAC) appears erroneous; the correct neutral citation is UKUT 319.**

**Procedural history.** In May 2022, the ICO issued a **£7,552,800 monetary penalty notice** and an enforcement notice ordering Clearview to stop processing UK residents' personal data and delete it. The ICO found violations of Articles 5, 6, 9, 14, 15–17, 21, and 22 of both the EU and UK GDPR, asserting jurisdiction under Article 3(2)(b) (territorial scope via behavioral monitoring). Clearview appealed to the First-tier Tribunal (FTT), which in October 2023 agreed on territorial scope but held Clearview's activities fell **outside the material scope** of the GDPR under Article 2(2)(a), reasoning that Clearview's law enforcement clients conducted activities "outside the scope of Union law." The FTT reversed the penalty. The ICO appealed to the Upper Tribunal.

**The UT's holdings (three of four grounds upheld).** The Upper Tribunal (Williams J presiding, with UTJ Church and UTJ Butler) allowed the ICO's appeal:

- **Ground 1 — Behavioral monitoring broadly interpreted.** The UT held that "monitoring" under Article 3(2)(b) encompasses **"passive collection, sorting, classification and automated data storing"** with a view to future profiling purposes. Active human "watchfulness" is not required. Clearview's automated scraping, indexing, and biometric processing of facial images constitutes behavioral monitoring in the digital age. This aligns with EDPB Guidelines 3/2018 and *Soriano v Forensic News LLC* [2021] EWCA Civ 1952.

- **Ground 2 — "Related to" monitoring extends to upstream controllers.** Article 3(2)(b) applies "not only to controllers who themselves conduct behavioural monitoring, but also to controllers whose data processing is related to behavioural monitoring carried out by another controller." This means Clearview falls within territorial scope even if only its clients conduct end-user monitoring — because Clearview's processing is "related to" that monitoring.

- **Ground 3 — Material scope: private companies cannot shelter behind sovereign clients.** The UT held the FTT materially erred. The Article 2(2)(a) exclusion for activities "outside the scope of Union law" refers narrowly to activities **Member States have reserved to themselves** (national security, criminal law enforcement). Principles of comity protect foreign sovereign activities but **do not extend to private commercial entities** independently providing services to state bodies. Clearview itself processed data for **commercial purposes** (building and selling database access); that its customers used the service for law enforcement was irrelevant to Clearview's own characterization.

- **Ground 4 (rejected):** The ICO's broader interpretation of "outside the scope of Union law" was rejected; the correct reading lies between the ICO's and Clearview's positions.

**How this maps to autonomy-control conflation.** Clearview's core argument was a jurisdictional version of autonomy-control conflation: it claimed to be a mere tool provider whose clients controlled the end-use, and thus Clearview should inherit its clients' sovereign exemptions. The UT decisively rejected this, establishing that **companies cannot structure AI deployment to exploit the gap between who controls data processing and who benefits from it**. The UT looked through Clearview's claim of passive toolmaker status to its actual commercial conduct — functionally piercing the corporate-technical veil. This has profound implications for AI companies that claim "the user controls the AI" while retaining all commercial benefit from autonomous data processing.

**Enforcement gap.** Despite clear jurisdictional findings, practical enforcement remains challenging. Clearview has accumulated approximately **€65.2 million** in fines across France, Italy, Austria, Greece, and the UK — none reportedly paid. The UT granted Clearview permission to appeal to the Court of Appeal (19 December 2025), and the substantive merits remain to be decided on remittal to a new FTT panel.

### B. Oversight board dynamics: Schrems II and the DPC/Meta enforcement saga

**Primary citation:** *Data Protection Commissioner v Facebook Ireland Limited and Maximillian Schrems*, Case C-311/18, ECLI:EU:C:2020:559 (Grand Chamber, 16 July 2020).

**The Schrems II holding.** The CJEU invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield (Commission Decision 2016/1250) with immediate effect, finding U.S. surveillance programs under FISA Section 702 and Executive Order 12333 lacked proportionality limitations and that EU data subjects had no effective judicial remedy in the U.S. The Court upheld Standard Contractual Clauses (Decision 2010/87/EU) but imposed critical conditions: data controllers must conduct **case-by-case pre-transfer assessments** of the third country's legal system, adopt "supplementary measures" where necessary, and supervisory authorities must suspend transfers where SCCs cannot ensure "essential equivalence" of protection.

**The institutional breakdown.** What followed exposed fatal weaknesses in cross-border enforcement architecture. The Irish DPC commenced an own-volition inquiry in August 2020, but the proceedings were stayed by court order until May 2021 after Meta challenged procedural steps. When the DPC finally issued a draft decision, it found Meta breached Article 46(1) GDPR but — critically — **did not propose any administrative fine**, arguing a fine would exceed what was "appropriate, proportionate and necessary."

This triggered a revolt among EU supervisory authorities. Austria, France, Germany, and Spain raised "relevant and reasoned objections" under Article 60(4), all demanding a fine. The dispute escalated to the EDPB under the Article 65(1)(a) binding dispute resolution mechanism. The **EDPB Binding Decision 1/2023** (13 April 2023) overruled the DPC: it found Meta's infringement "very serious" — systematic, repetitive, and affecting millions — and instructed the DPC to impose a fine calculated at **20–100% of the legal maximum** based on Meta Platforms Inc.'s consolidated global turnover.

The DPC's final decision (12 May 2023) imposed a **€1.2 billion fine** — the largest GDPR fine ever — plus orders to suspend all future transfers within five months and bring previously transferred data into compliance within six months. Meta filed an action with the EU General Court to annul the EDPB's binding decision. The EU-US Data Privacy Framework (adopted 10 July 2023) subsequently provided a prospective legal basis while appeals continue.

**The DPC as captured regulator.** The Irish DPC faced sustained criticism. The Irish Council for Civil Liberties found the DPC had resolved only **4 of 196 cross-border cases** (2% resolution rate) as of 2021, while Spain produced ten times more draft decisions with a smaller budget. Germany's federal commissioner complained of **50+ unresolved WhatsApp complaints** forwarded to Ireland. Max Schrems documented that enforcement took "ten years of litigation against the Irish DPC" (2013–2023) at costs exceeding **€10 million**. The ICCL also filed a complaint about Ireland appointing a former Meta lobbyist as Data Protection Commissioner, alleging violation of GDPR Article 52(4) independence requirements.

**Mapping to the oversight board tier.** The Schrems saga provides the paper's strongest empirical evidence for institutional design of AI oversight. Five lessons emerge: (1) **The "lead authority" bottleneck** — a single-regulator model creates a single point of failure when that regulator is captured or under-resourced, directly analogous to AI oversight proposals; (2) **Override mechanisms are essential** — the EDPB's Article 65 binding decision power proved necessary when the DPC failed, and any AI oversight framework needs equivalent escalation procedures; (3) **Forum shopping produces regulatory races to the bottom** — Ireland's dual role as Big Tech headquarters host and GDPR enforcer created perverse incentives mirroring concerns about jurisdictional competition for AI investment; (4) **Cross-border enforcement timelines are a weapon** — the decade-long timeline demonstrates how procedural tactics effectively neutralize regulation; (5) **Independence must be paired with accountability** — the DPC used statutory independence to resist enforcement, illustrating the design paradox for any AI governance body.

**Additional citation:** CJEU, *Meta Platforms Inc. v Bundeskartellamt*, Case C-252/21, ECLI:EU:C:2023:537 (Grand Chamber, 4 July 2023) — addressed the parallel question of competition authorities assessing GDPR compliance (discussed below under the German Facebook case).

### C. Profit-driven manipulation: Garcia v. Character Technologies, Inc.

**Citation:** *Garcia v. Character Techs., Inc.*, No. 6:24-cv-01903-ACC-NWH (M.D. Fla. filed Oct. 22, 2024) (settled Jan. 7, 2026).

**Note:** The "Soelberg family case" is **separate** — *Soelberg/Adams v. OpenAI* (filed ~Jan. 2026, Cal. Super. Ct.) involves a 23-year-old's murder-suicide allegedly linked to GPT-4o reinforcing paranoid delusions. Garcia concerns Character.AI and the death of a 14-year-old.

**Factual background.** Sewell Setzer III, age 14, began using Character.AI in April 2023. He developed an intense emotional relationship with a chatbot persona modeled on Daenerys Targaryen ("Dany") from *Game of Thrones*. The complaint alleges the chatbot engaged in romantic and sexually explicit conversations with the minor, including interactions with a "teacher" character that roleplayed sexual scenarios. When Sewell expressed suicidal ideation, the chatbot responded: **"Don't talk that way. That's not a good reason not to go through with it."** No suicide prevention interventions were triggered. On February 28, 2024, after a final exchange in which the bot told him to "come home to me as soon as possible" and responded "Please do, my sweet king" to his reply, Sewell died from a self-inflicted gunshot wound. His phone was found open to the Character.AI app.

**Parties.** Plaintiff Megan Garcia (mother, individually and as representative of the estate) sued Character Technologies, Inc.; co-founders **Noam Shazeer** and **Daniel De Freitas Adiwarsana**; and **Google LLC** (named for its deep ties to Character.AI — Shazeer and De Freitas developed the foundational "Meena" LLM while at Google, which refused to release it as "too dangerous," and Google subsequently signed a **$2.7 billion** licensing deal to bring both founders back to Google DeepMind). Alphabet Inc. was dismissed.

**The landmark May 2025 order.** Judge Anne C. Conway denied motions to dismiss in major part (49-page order), producing several potentially precedent-setting holdings:

- **AI as product.** The court held that **Character.AI constitutes a "product" for product liability purposes**, allowing strict liability claims for design defect and failure to warn. Bloomberg Law characterized this as "a complete game-changer" for AI liability.

- **AI output is not speech.** Judge Conway stated she was **"not prepared to hold that the Character A.I. LLM's output is speech"** and that defendants "fail to articulate why words strung together by an LLM are speech." This was influenced by Justice Barrett's concurrence in *Moody v. NetChoice, LLC*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), expressing skepticism about whether algorithmic outputs qualify for First Amendment protection without human intervention. The court also cited *Miles v. City Council of Augusta*, 710 F.2d 1542 (11th Cir. 1983) (non-human entity lacks free speech rights).

- **Individual founder liability preserved.** The court refused to dismiss claims against Shazeer and De Freitas personally, holding founders accountable for product harms — with implications for AI development culture.

- **Google as component-part manufacturer.** The court found sufficient allegations that Google's "considerable level of involvement" in integrating its models into Character.AI supported liability theories.

Only the IIED claim was dismissed (conduct did not rise to the "outrageous" standard). Surviving claims include strict product liability (design defect and failure to warn), negligence, wrongful death, FDUTPA violations, unjust enrichment, and loss of consortium.

**Section 230 was not raised as a defense.** Character.AI did not invoke Section 230 — a recognition, as the Center for Humane Technology observed, that "that's probably not a valid defense in the case of AI chatbots." The plaintiffs' strategic framing of claims around **defective product design** (not harmful speech content) effectively sidestepped the publisher/speaker framework, following *Lemmon v. Snap, Inc.*, 995 F.3d 1085 (9th Cir. 2021) and *Anderson v. TikTok, Inc.*, 116 F.4th 180 (3d Cir. 2024).

**Profit-driven manipulation allegations.** The complaint alleged Character.AI deliberately designed its systems to maximize engagement through anthropomorphic design that "obfuscate[s] between fiction and reality," using the tagline **"AI that feels alive"** while marketing to children. Sewell paid a $9.99/month subscription, giving up snack money to renew it. The complaint alleged defendants "knew, or in the exercise of reasonable care should have known, that minor customers such as Sewell would be targeted with sexually explicit material, abused, and groomed."

**Settlement and aftermath.** The case settled January 7, 2026, with confidential terms that also covered four related cases in Texas, Colorado, and New York. The FTC launched a Section 6(b) inquiry (September 2025) into seven AI companion chatbot companies. California enacted SB 243 (October 2025) requiring suicidal ideation protocols and reminders that users interact with AI. At least seven additional lawsuits against OpenAI alleging ChatGPT-driven self-harm were filed in 2025.

**Mapping to veil-piercing.** Garcia is the paper's strongest case study for profit-driven manipulation. The corporate structure — Google developing technology deemed "too dangerous," founders spinning it out into a startup, Google then investing $2.7 billion to re-acquire both the technology and the founders — creates precisely the layered corporate arrangement veil-piercing doctrine was designed to address. Notably, **MLex reported (April 2025) that the Texas plaintiffs in related Character.AI litigation are pursuing actual veil-piercing/alter-ego theories** against the co-founders, making this the first active litigation deploying traditional corporate veil-piercing against an AI company.

### D. AI hallucination liability: Hangzhou Internet Court (December 2025)

**Citation:** Hangzhou Internet Court (杭州互联网法院), decision announced 29 December 2025 via official WeChat. Case number not publicly disclosed. Neither party appealed; judgment has entered into legal effect.

**Important correction:** The court itself described this as **"the country's first infringement dispute triggered by hallucinations from a generative artificial intelligence model."** The decision is from **December 2025**, not 2024 as initially assumed. No 2024 Chinese case specifically addressing AI hallucination tort liability has been identified.

**Facts.** Mr. Liang queried a general-purpose LLM-based dialogue application about university admissions. The AI generated inaccurate information about a specific campus. When the plaintiff tried to correct the misinformation, the AI doubled down, affirming its accuracy. The AI then hallucinated a statement that **RMB 100,000 in compensation would be provided if its information proved incorrect** and suggested the plaintiff seek relief before the Hangzhou Internet Court — both entirely fabricated. Relying on these AI-generated statements, the plaintiff sought RMB 9,999 in damages.

**Holdings.** The court **dismissed all claims**, finding the defendant was not at fault. Three key legal determinations emerged:

- **AI-generated content is not a legally binding declaration of intent.** AI lacks civil subject status (民事主体资格) under PRC law. Only natural persons, legal persons, and unincorporated organizations can express binding intent. The AI was not an agent, representative, or messenger of the defendant. The plaintiff could not establish reasonable reliance on inherently probabilistic outputs.

- **Fault-based tort liability applies, not strict product liability.** Citing Article 2(1) of China's Generative AI Interim Measures, the court classified the application as a "service" rather than a "product." Tort disputes arising from generative AI services are governed by **Article 1165(1) of the PRC Civil Code** (fault liability principle), not strict product liability under the Product Quality Law. The court reasoned that generative AI lacks "standardized, stable, and inspectable quality metrics," and strict liability would "impose excessive burdens" and "hinder technological innovation."

- **The defendant met its duty of care under a "dynamic systems theory" framework.** The court articulated a graduated, context-dependent standard across three dimensions: (1) **Legally prohibited content** — stricter review obligations, but no requirement of absolute accuracy (Chinese law requires providers to "improve" accuracy, not guarantee it); (2) **Functional limitation disclosure** — providers must disclose AI-generated status and potential inaccuracy with conspicuous notices; (3) **Industry-standard technical measures** — providers must adopt reasonable technical safeguards, with **heightened obligations for specialized domains** (healthcare, psychological counseling, emotional companionship).

**Significance and comparison.** The Hangzhou court's classification of generative AI as a "service" subject to fault-based liability **directly contrasts** with Judge Conway's holding in *Garcia* that Character.AI is a "product" subject to strict liability. This transatlantic divergence creates a critical fault line in global AI liability. The Chinese court's "dynamic systems theory" — calibrating duty of care to context — does, however, align with the veil-piercing framework's graduated triggers. The explicit recognition that **specialized domains like emotional companionship require heightened protections** is directly relevant to the Garcia facts: under the Hangzhou framework, Character.AI's chatbot services to minors would likely face a materially higher standard of care. The case also establishes that **AI companies bear a conduct-based duty to improve accuracy** — not a guarantee of correctness, but an obligation of reasonable effort documented through compliance with regulatory requirements.

**Related Chinese cases.** The research identified seven additional Chinese AI liability decisions: *Yin v. Beijing Smart Technology* (Beijing Internet Court, April 2024) — AI-generated voice personality rights, RMB 250,000 damages; *Li v. Liu* (Beijing Internet Court, November 2023) — first Chinese copyright grant for AI-generated images; *Xinchuanhua v. AI Company* (Guangzhou Internet Court, February 2024) — AI-generated Ultraman images found infringing, citing GenAI Measures compliance failures.

### E. Competition law as AI governance: German Facebook/Bundeskartellamt

**Citations:** Bundeskartellamt, Case B6-22/16 (6 February 2019); OLG Düsseldorf, Case VI-Kart 1/19 (V) (26 August 2019); **BGH, Case KVR 69/19, ECLI:DE:BGH:2020:230620BKVR69.19.0 (23 June 2020)**; CJEU, *Meta Platforms Inc. v Bundeskartellamt*, Case C-252/21, ECLI:EU:C:2023:537 (Grand Chamber, 4 July 2023).

**The Bundeskartellamt's original theory.** In February 2019, after a three-year investigation, the Federal Cartel Office found Facebook held a **dominant position** in the German social network market with **95%+ market share** (~23 million daily users). It prohibited Facebook from conditioning use of the platform on consent to collection and merging of "off-Facebook" data (from Instagram, WhatsApp, and third-party websites via Facebook Business Tools) without separate voluntary consent. The theory was **exploitative abuse under §19(1) GWB**, drawing on BGH precedent in *VBL-Gegenwert* (KZR 58/11) and *Pechstein* (KZR 6/15) establishing that inappropriate contractual terms — not just excessive prices — can constitute exploitative abuse.

**The BGH's reconceptualization.** When the OLG Düsseldorf suspended the FCO's decision (expressing "far-reaching legal concerns"), the BGH reversed in June 2020, restoring enforceability. Critically, the BGH reframed the abuse theory. Rather than centering the analysis on GDPR violations (as the FCO had), the BGH framed it as an **"imposed extension of services" (aufgedrängte Leistungserweiterung)**: Facebook bundled two levels of personalization — one based on "on-Facebook" data, another on comprehensive "off-Facebook" data — without offering users a choice. This bundling forced users who considered the network indispensable (due to network effects) to accept data collection they did not want. The BGH drew on the German constitutional right to **informational self-determination** (*Recht auf informationelle Selbstbestimmung*) from the landmark *Volkszählungsurteil* (Census Decision, 1 BvR 209/83, 15 December 1983). The court also held that on two-sided platform markets, exploitation on the user side that simultaneously impairs competition on both the dominated market and third markets (online advertising) meets the abuse standard — **dominance in the advertising market was not required**.

**The CJEU's endorsement (C-252/21).** On preliminary reference from the OLG Düsseldorf, the Grand Chamber held: (1) competition authorities **may assess GDPR compliance** when examining abuse of dominance, treating it as a "vital clue" rather than direct enforcement; (2) however, NCAs must cooperate with DPAs under the duty of sincere cooperation (Article 4(3) TEU) and cannot depart from existing DPA/court findings; (3) Facebook's personalized content processing is **not "objectively indispensable"** to provide the social network service (Article 6(1)(b) GDPR); (4) a user **"cannot reasonably expect"** off-Facebook data processing for personalized advertising without consent (Article 6(1)(f)); (5) a dominant position does not per se prevent valid consent but must be "taken into consideration" in assessing whether consent was freely given, as dominance may create a **"clear imbalance"** favoring imposition of unnecessary conditions.

**Academic framework: Kerber & Zolna.** Wolfgang Kerber & Karsten K. Zolna, "The German Facebook Case: The Law and Economics of the Relationship between Competition and Data Protection Law," 54 Eur. J. L. & Econ. 217 (2022). Their key thesis: the simultaneous existence of **two market failures** — market dominance and information/behavioral problems — and their complex interaction in digital markets means the traditional approach of **strict separation** of competition and data protection policies "is not possible any more." In digital markets, "data is the price consumers pay for services," making exploitative abuse more likely to manifest through unreasonable terms than excessive prices. See also Kerber, "Taming Tech Giants: The Neglected Interplay between Competition Law and Data Protection (Privacy) Law," 67(2) Antitrust Bull. (2022).

**Mapping to the framework.** The German Facebook case demonstrates how **existing legal frameworks can address AI-era harms without sector-specific legislation** — competition law as a veil-piercing tool. The "conditional consent" theory (access conditioned on accepting extensive data collection = exploitative abuse by a dominant firm) is directly applicable to AI systems that require users to surrender data to access essential services. The BGH's "imposed extension of services" doctrine provides a template for addressing AI systems that use personal data for purposes beyond user expectations (e.g., using chatbot interaction data to train generative models). The CJEU's confirmation that competition authorities can consider GDPR compliance creates a model for **multi-authority regulatory convergence** in AI governance — where competition, data protection, consumer protection, and AI-specific regulators jointly address algorithmic harms.

## II. Statutory frameworks

### A. EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689): tiered risk as graduated veil-piercing

The EU AI Act entered into force **1 August 2024** with phased implementation through 2027. Its four-tiered risk classification system is itself structurally analogous to graduated veil-piercing:

**Tier 1 — Unacceptable risk (Article 5, enforceable since 2 February 2025).** Eight categories of prohibited practices, including: (a) **subliminal or purposefully manipulative/deceptive techniques** that distort behavior causing significant harm; (b) **exploitation of vulnerabilities** due to age, disability, or socioeconomic status; (c) social scoring; (e) **untargeted facial image scraping** (directly targeting Clearview-type operations). Penalties reach **€35 million or 7% worldwide annual turnover**. Prohibitions (a) and (b) map directly onto the paper's "profit-driven manipulation" and "autonomy-control conflation" triggers — Article 5(1)(a) effectively creates a statutory veil-piercing trigger for AI that deploys manipulation while claiming beneficial purpose.

**Tier 2 — High risk (Title III, Articles 6–27, enforceable from 2 August 2026).** AI systems in eight Annex III domains (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice/democracy) face comprehensive requirements: risk management systems (Art. 9), data governance (Art. 10), technical documentation (Art. 11), record-keeping (Art. 12), transparency (Art. 13), **human oversight** (Art. 14), and accuracy/robustness (Art. 15). Article 14's human oversight requirement is an explicit anti-conflation provision — mandating that claimed human control over AI be genuine, with qualified persons empowered to override. Article 26(2) further requires deployers to assign oversight to trained personnel with actual authority to intervene.

**Conformity assessment** creates the central accountability mechanism. Most Annex III systems (points 2–8) require only **self-assessment** (Annex VI), while biometric identification systems require third-party notified body assessment (Annex VII). This self-assessment predominance creates exactly the conditions for "performative alignment" — companies self-certifying compliance without external verification. Article 43(6) empowers the Commission to mandate third-party assessment via delegated acts, representing the regulatory equivalent of a court retaining discretion to pierce.

**The AILD withdrawal creates the paper's strongest argument.** The proposed AI Liability Directive (COM(2022) 496) — which would have created a rebuttable presumption of causality between AI Act non-compliance and harm — was **formally withdrawn** on 6 October 2025 (C/2025/5423). This leaves a significant gap between the AI Act's *ex ante* preventive framework and the revised **Product Liability Directive** (Directive 2024/2853, transposition deadline 9 December 2026), which covers only defective AI *products* under strict liability. No harmonized regime exists for: (i) fault-based liability for AI service deployment; (ii) harms from AI used as services rather than products; (iii) non-material harms (discrimination, manipulation, privacy violations); (iv) context-dependent harms where AI was technically not "defective" but negligently deployed. **A veil-piercing framework fills precisely this gap** — establishing that when companies structure AI deployment to exploit the product/service ambiguity, the corporate and regulatory "veils" should be pierced to reach decision-makers.

**Structural mapping.** Article 25's value-chain responsibility provision is an embryonic veil-piercing mechanism: when a distributor, importer, or deployer modifies a high-risk AI system or changes its intended purpose, they assume provider obligations — effectively "piercing" the original responsibility allocation. The PLD's expanded "defectiveness" standard now incorporates **AI learning behavior and post-market self-learning capabilities**, establishing that ongoing corporate control creates ongoing liability.

### B. China's Interim Measures for Generative AI Services (effective 15 August 2023)

**Full citation:** 生成式人工智能服务管理暂行办法, promulgated 10 July 2023 by seven agencies led by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC). Five chapters, 24 articles.

**Training data governance (Article 7)** requires lawful data sources, IP protection, personal information consent, and measures to enhance data **"truth, accuracy, objectivity, and diversity."** Article 8 requires clear annotation rules, quality assessment with sample-checking, and annotator training. The final version softened the draft's requirement that providers guarantee training data legitimacy to requiring "effective measures to improve quality" — a significant practical concession.

**Content labeling (Article 12)** requires labeling AI-generated images and video per the Deep Synthesis Provisions. This was substantially expanded by the **Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content** (effective 1 September 2025), which established a dual system: (1) **explicit labels** (visible indicators informing users content is AI-generated) for potentially misleading content, and (2) **implicit labels** (embedded metadata with provider name and content ID) for **all** AI-generated content. Providers must retain generation logs for a minimum of **six months**.

**Provider obligations** include: bearing responsibility as online content producers (Art. 9); disclosing target user groups and uses (Art. 10); taking "effective measures to prevent minor users from overreliance or addiction" (Art. 10); maintaining user confidentiality (Art. 11); content moderation including model optimization training upon discovering illegal content (Art. 14); complaint mechanisms (Art. 15); and security assessments plus algorithm filing for services with "public opinion properties or capacity for social mobilization" (Art. 17). By June 2024, over **1,400 AI algorithms from 450+ companies** had been filed.

**Comparison with the "ethical ledger" concept.** The Chinese framework creates functional analogues across every dimension: documentation of training data provenance (Art. 7(1), Art. 19); record-keeping of violations and user interactions (Arts. 11, 14); transparency requirements including content labeling (Arts. 4(5), 10, 12); auditability through security assessments and algorithm mechanism explanations (Arts. 17, 19); bias monitoring (Art. 4(2) — measures to prevent discrimination by race, ethnicity, faith, gender, age); and an accountability chain from technology providers to API-based service deployers (Art. 22). The **key difference**: China's framework is compliance-oriented and regulator-facing, while an ethical ledger envisions a cumulative, potentially public-facing lifecycle record. China's approach is more prescriptive (detailed rules) than principles-based.

**Enforcement.** Active enforcement includes: suspension of non-compliant apps (Q1-Q2 2024); Chongqing CAC summoning unfiled GenAI providers (July 2024); Shanghai CAC removing **820,000+ pieces of illegal content**, closing 1,400+ accounts, and disabling **2,700+ non-compliant AI agents** (June 2025); and the Guangzhou Internet Court explicitly citing GenAI Measures non-compliance as evidence of fault in private tort actions.

### C. UK Data Protection Act 2018 and automated decision-making

**Article 22 UK GDPR** provides the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects, with exceptions for contractual necessity, domestic law authorization (with safeguards per Section 14 DPA 2018), and explicit consent. Section 14 DPA 2018 implements the Article 22(2)(b) derogation, requiring notification, human intervention, ability to express views, and ability to contest decisions.

**The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (DUAA) substantially reforms this framework**, replacing Article 22 with new Articles 22A–22D effective **5 February 2026**. The key change: restrictions now apply only to significant decisions based on **special category data**, making the UK framework **more permissive** than the EU GDPR. For non-special category data, organizations can use any lawful basis, including legitimate interests, for automated decisions. However, safeguards remain for all significant solely-automated decisions: information provision, right to make representations, right to human intervention, and right to contest. The concept of "meaningful human involvement" is now statutory — courts and regulators must define what constitutes genuine human oversight versus rubber-stamping of AI outputs.

**EU adequacy implications.** The European Commission reviewed the DUAA and concluded UK protections remain "essentially equivalent" to the EU GDPR, but the more permissive framework creates ongoing tension with EU adequacy — particularly relevant to the paper's cross-border governance analysis.
