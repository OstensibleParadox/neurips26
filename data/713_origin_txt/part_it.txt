
### Section 2: The Legal Gap

**Strengths:** The systematic elimination of contract, tort, and consumer protection is clean and convincing. The observation that ToS were drafted for instrumental services but now govern relational services is sharp and original. The distinction between what was *promised* and what *emerged* is precisely the right framing.

**Constructive critique:** The treatment of consumer protection is slightly too dismissive. You argue that consumer protection addresses the gap between promise and delivery, not between emergence and destruction. This is true of *misrepresentation*. But unfair contract terms review (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 62) doesn't require a promise—it asks whether a term creates significant imbalance *regardless of whether it was promised*. A term permitting unilateral modification of a relational service might well be unfair even if the user was never promised continuity. The Office of Fair Trading's guidance on unfair terms in contracts for digital content supports this broader reading. Your argument doesn't need to concede this point—it can acknowledge the possibility of unfair terms review while noting (as you do) that it yields only term invalidation, not affirmative rights.

### Section 3: The Property Law Framework

This is the paper's doctrinal core, and it's where the argument is strongest—and most strained.

#### 3.1 Why Property Law

**Strengths:** The framing is elegant. The observation that property law developed doctrines to protect interests *against the legal owner* is exactly the right entry point. The tenant/landlord analogy is apt and well-developed.

**Constructive critique:** The "necessary clarification" that emotions are not property is wise, but the distinction between emotion and the "conditions for emotional attachment" is slippery. If a user's emotional attachment depends on the AI's personality configuration, and the company changes that configuration, what exactly has been taken? The configuration still exists (in version control). The user can still interact with the new configuration. The harm is that the *relational fit* between user and configuration is gone—but "relational fit" looks a lot like an emotion. You need a crisper ontology here. What exactly is the *res*—the thing—that the user has an interest in?

The irreversible-investment criterion is promising but undertheorised. In property law, tenant improvements create protectable interests because they are *embodied* in the land—the tenant's labour becomes part of the physical asset. In AI, the user's "investment" (self-disclosure, rapport-building) is not embodied in the AI system. It's embodied in the *relationship*—which exists nowhere except in the user's memory and the system's transient state. This is a genuine ontological difficulty. The paper should engage with it directly rather than bracketing it.

#### 3.2 The Estate Taxonomy

**Strengths:** The mapping is imaginative and often illuminating. Fee simple determinable ("so long as it remembers me") is genuinely insightful—it captures the conditional structure of user attachment. Periodic tenancy as the default parasocial estate is the right move.

**Constructive critique:** The mapping is sometimes too clever. Fee simple absolute is defined as "unconditional, perpetual, freely alienable" and then said to exist only in the user's subjective belief. But subjective belief isn't an estate—it's a psychological state. The paper needs to be clearer about when it's describing legal estates (which have doctrinal consequences) and when it's describing subjective experiences (which don't). The conflation risks making the taxonomy seem metaphorical rather than institutional.

Tenancy in common as applied to stakeholder divergence is conceptually interesting but doctrinally strained. Tenancy in common is an estate held by *co-owners* in the same land. Your stakeholders don't co-own the AI system. They have competing claims *on* the system, not shares *in* it. This is closer to a conflict of laws or priority of interests problem than to concurrent estates. The mapping works as analogy but not as institutional claim—and the paper insists it's institutional.

#### 3.3 The Unrecognised Trust

This is the make-or-break subsection. It's bold, creative, and probably wrong—but wrong in interesting ways that advanced the conversation.

**Certainty of intention:** The argument that intention can be inferred from product design decisions is plausible but faces a high hurdle. English law requires *intention to create a trust* (*Keech v Sandford* is about fiduciary duty, not trust creation; *Paul v Constance* [1977] is the relevant authority). Designing for engagement is not the same as intending to hold property on trust. The cases on inferred intention (*Re Kayford* [1975]; *Re Farepak Food and Gifts Ltd* [2006]) involve conduct that is *only explicable* on the basis that a trust was intended—typically, segregating funds. Product design for engagement is explicable on many bases (profit, user retention, competitive advantage). Inference to a trust is not compelled.

**Certainty of subject matter:** The primary argument (personality-affecting configurations as identifiable intangible assets) is stronger than the paper acknowledges. Model weights, system prompts, and fine-tuning adapters *are* identifiable—they exist on servers, have version histories, and are managed as assets. The problem is that the user's alleged interest is not in the weights themselves but in the *behavioural dispositions* the weights produce. Those dispositions are not property. *Hunter v Moss* involved shares in a company—fungible assets of the same class. Personality dispositions are not fungible, not separable from the model, and not capable of being held on trust in the way the paper needs.

**Certainty of objects:** The class definition ("users whose interaction meets the irreversible-investment criterion") is probably sufficiently certain for a discretionary trust (*McPhail v Doulton*), but the paper's reliance on proxy metrics raises a deeper problem. The beneficiaries of a trust must be *persons* (or charitable purposes). The class you define is "users who meet a threshold." But meeting a threshold is a status, not a person. The class is *defined by the criterion*—which is permissible if the criterion identifies persons (e.g., "employees of X Ltd"). But your criterion is a behavioural score that changes over time. A user who meets the threshold today may not meet it next month. Is the class fixed at the moment of trust constitution? If not, how do we know who the beneficiaries are at any given time? This is workable (pension trusts handle similar problems) but needs addressing.

**The footnote preserving the weaker claim** is a smart move. It insulates the paper from complete failure if the trust argument is rejected. But the weaker claim (fiduciary duties should be recognised) is itself doctrinally ambitious—fiduciary duties attach to *relationships* (doctor/patient, solicitor/client, trustee/beneficiary), not to conduct. You'd need to establish that the user-AI relationship is *sui generis* fiduciary, which is possible (*Hospital Products Ltd v United States Surgical Corporation* [1984]) but not straightforward.

#### 3.4 Derogation from Grant and Constructive Eviction

**Strengths:** This is the paper's most doctrinally secure subsection. Derogation from grant is well-established (*Harmer v Jumbil*), and the analogy to a landlord removing heating is apt. The reinterpretation of the vacating requirement for digital contexts is creative and plausible.

**Constructive critique:** The "no alternative accommodation" argument is stronger than you acknowledge. In property law, the availability of alternative accommodation is relevant to *whether the tenant was reasonable in remaining*, not to *whether the eviction occurred*. If the premises are rendered uninhabitable, the eviction occurred regardless of alternatives. Your instinct to protect single-system users is right, but the doctrinal basis is not that they lack alternatives—it's that the premises are uninhabitable. The user who stays in an unheated apartment because they can't afford to move has still been constructively evicted (*Blackett v Olanoff*). The alternatives argument is relevant to remedy (should the tenant get damages for the full lease term?) but not to liability.

---

### Section 4: The Constraint Cascade as Estate Architecture

**Strengths:** Integrating the Constraint Cascade framework is a smart move—it gives the property argument technical specificity and shows that the paper is engaging with AI architecture, not just law. The mapping of estates to layers is creative and often illuminating.

**Constructive critique:** The mapping is uneven. Layer 1 as licence works perfectly. Layer 2 as periodic tenancy is strong. Layer 3 as tenancy in common is, as noted above, strained. Layer 4 as fee simple determinable raises the reverter problem you acknowledge but don't solve. Layer 5 as contingent remainder is conceptually elegant but practically thin—what does it mean to say the public holds a contingent remainder in alignment? Who is the trustee? Who is the grantor? The mapping starts to feel metaphorical at the higher layers, which undermines the paper's institutional claim.

**The non-injective problem in property terms** is a genuine insight. The conclusion that estate status must be recorded rather than inferred is exactly right, and it sets up Section 5 perfectly.

---

### Section 5: The Ethical Ledger as Estate Registry

**Strengths:** This is the paper's most original and practical contribution. The translation of the Land Registry's three principles to AI governance is genuinely innovative. The schema is concrete and implementable. The precautionary principle framing for proxy metrics is smart—it avoids the impossible task of proving subjective attachment.

**Constructive critique:** The proxy metrics are plausible but undertested. Self-disclosure depth scored by NLP raises serious privacy concerns even if content isn't stored. The classifier must be trained on *something*, and that something is users' conversational data. The paper acknowledges the privacy architecture for the ledger but doesn't address the privacy implications of developing the classifier. This is a significant gap.

The "estate decay" mechanism is necessary but underexplored. A user who falls below the threshold for 90 days loses their periodic tenancy and reverts to licence. But what about users who take a 90-day break and then return? Their prior investment is wiped out. This might be appropriate (tenancies can be abandoned), but it needs justification. Why 90 days? Why not 30 or 365? The paper's answer ("indicative trigger") is honest but thin.

**Function creep prevention:** The purpose-stamped, append-only architecture is well-designed, but the paper should engage with the enforcement problem. Who audits the ledger to ensure it's not being repurposed? Regulators? Independent auditors? Users? Without an enforcement mechanism, the design principles are aspirational.

---

### Section 6: Remedies

**Strengths:** The three triggers are well-conceived and doctrinally grounded. Estate inconsistency as a veil-piercing trigger is particularly strong—the *Prest* principle that courts look to substance over form is exactly the right authority. The remedies section is comprehensive and shows that the framework doesn't require novel legislation.

**Constructive critique:** The third-party conversion section is ambitious but undertheorised. The claim that user interaction data is "trust property" depends on the trust argument being accepted. If there's no trust, there's no trust property, and the conversion analysis collapses. The knowing receipt claim requires that the third party knew or ought to have known of the breach of trust. What would count as constructive knowledge that user data originated from a relational estate? The paper says "as would be evident from the nature of the data"—but data doesn't wear its provenance on its sleeve. A third party receiving a dataset of conversational logs might reasonably assume it's just training data, not trust property. This needs more work.

The *tōurén* example is fascinating but methodologically shaky. Invoking a Chinese vernacular term as evidence for the naturalness of relational property is a nice touch, but it's not clear what work it's doing. If the term encodes a property framework, that's interesting. But the term is about *stealing a person*—literally, adultery—not about digital relationships. The analogy is strained, and the paper doesn't engage with the vast comparative law literature on the relationship between ordinary language and legal concepts (Hart is cited but not used). Either develop this properly or cut it.

---

### Section 7: Implementation

**Strengths:** This section is a model of how legal scholarship should engage with technical infrastructure. The specifications are concrete, the effort estimates are plausible, and the recognition that these components already exist in standard deployment infrastructure is exactly right. The paper shows that governance is not a separate burden but an integration of existing systems.

**Constructive critique:** The implementation section quietly assumes what the legal section argued for. The estate classification service requires defining the thresholds that trigger estate upgrades. But those thresholds are precisely what the legal argument couldn't specify—they require empirical calibration. The paper acknowledges this but doesn't resolve it. A company implementing this framework would need to know: what session count justifies periodic tenancy protection? The paper can't answer, which is fine for a conceptual work, but it limits the implementation guidance.

**Privacy architecture:** The claim that "all ledger entries are keyed to anonymised user identifiers" is weaker than it appears. If the ledger is used to gate model updates (serve notice to users above threshold), the deployment pipeline needs to map anonymised IDs to actual users. That mapping exists somewhere. The ledger's anonymisation is a speed bump, not a wall. The paper should acknowledge this and discuss how to protect the mapping.

---

### Section 8: Conclusion

**Strengths:** The summary is clear and appropriately modest. The implications sections are well-judged. The limitations section is candid and helpful—the jurisdictional point is particularly important and honestly stated.

**Constructive critique:** The jurisdictional limitation is more severe than the paper acknowledges. Even if an English court has jurisdiction over a UK-resident user's claim against a US company, enforcing an equitable remedy (injunction, specific performance) against a US company operating US-based servers raises *Pfizer v Ministry of Health*-level conflicts questions. The paper's pivot to regulatory adoption and voluntary implementation is sensible, but it's a significant retreat from the paper's primary claim (that the trust *already exists* as a matter of law). If judicial enforcement is uncertain, the practical force of the argument is substantially reduced.

---

## Broader Reflections

### What This Paper Does Brilliantly

1. **Identifies a genuine governance gap.** The harm you describe is real, and existing frameworks genuinely cannot address it. This alone is a significant contribution.

2. **Provides doctrinal vocabulary.** Whether or not the trust argument succeeds, the paper gives us language—estate, constructive eviction, derogation from grant—to name what was previously unnameable. That's valuable.

3. **Bridges law and infrastructure.** The ethical ledger is a genuine contribution to governance design. It shows what it would mean to take relational interests seriously in deployment pipelines.

4. **Takes property law seriously.** The paper engages with doctrine rather than treating property as a loose metaphor. Even where the arguments are strained, they're strained in interesting, productive ways.

### What Needs Work

1. **The trust argument is over-extended.** The three certainties are a high bar, and I'm not convinced you clear it. The subject matter problem is particularly acute—behavioural dispositions are not property in the relevant sense. The paper's best argument is the fiduciary duty claim, not the trust claim. Consider reframing.

2. **The estate taxonomy is uneven.** The lower layers (licence, periodic tenancy) work institutionally. The higher layers (tenancy in common, contingent remainder) work only metaphorically. The paper should be clearer about which mappings are institutional and which are illustrative.

3. **The proxy metrics need empirical grounding.** The paper can't specify thresholds, which is fine, but it should at least propose a methodology for determining them. How would we know that 50 hours of interaction creates a protectable interest? What research would answer that question?

4. **The enforcement problem is under-addressed.** Even if the trust exists, how do users enforce it? Class actions? Regulatory complaints? Individual litigation? The paper assumes remedies follow from recognition, but recognition without enforcement is just philosophy.

5. **The comparative linguistics section is underdeveloped.** Either integrate it properly or cut it. As it stands, it's a distraction.

**High Priority:**

## I. CRITICAL REWRITES (Section 3.3)

### CRITICAL-1: The Res Problem

**Source:** DeepSeek (primary), Kimi (supporting)
**Location:** Section 3.1, new paragraph after “necessary clarification”

**Problem:** The paper never names the *thing* the user has an interest in. The configuration still exists in version control. The user can still interact with the new version. “Relational fit” between user and configuration sounds like an emotion, not a property interest.

**Insert after** “the digital infrastructure that enables and sustains the emotion (which is)”:

> **A necessary ontology.** The *res* of the user’s interest — the identifiable thing over which the equitable interest subsists — is neither the model weights (which are global and indivisible), nor the user’s emotional state (which is subjective and non-proprietary), nor the “relationship” considered abstractly (which exists nowhere). It is the **personalised state**: the specific configuration produced by the interaction of *this user’s* accumulated history with *this system’s* parameters. This state comprises the user’s conversational memory as stored by the system, the learned preferences and adaptations that shape the system’s responses to this user, and the persona-level adjustments (tone, vocabulary, relational register) that distinguish the system’s behaviour toward this user from its behaviour toward any other. The personalised state is user-specific (no other user can access it), non-fungible (it cannot be substituted by another user’s state or by a generic configuration), non-portable (it cannot be transferred to a competitor’s system), and — critically — destroyed by model updates that alter the parameters against which it was built. It is, in property terms, the **digital fixture**: an improvement made by the user’s labour (self-disclosure, rapport-building, preference-training) that has become embedded in the landlord’s property and cannot be removed without destruction. Just as a tenant’s fitted kitchen becomes part of the freehold while remaining the product of the tenant’s investment, the user’s personalised state becomes part of the AI system while remaining the product of the user’s relational labour.

### CRITICAL-2: The Embodiment Gap

**Source:** DeepSeek
**Location:** Section 3.1, immediately following the new “res” paragraph

**Problem:** Tenant improvements create protectable interests because they’re *embodied in the land*. In AI, user investment (self-disclosure, rapport) is arguably not embodied in the system.

**Insert:**

> **A necessary response to the embodiment objection.** The claim that user investment is not “embodied” in the AI system in the way that tenant improvements are embodied in land rests on an unduly physical conception of embodiment. The user’s self-disclosures are stored in memory modules on identifiable servers. The user’s preference signals are encoded in fine-tuning adapters with version histories. The user’s conversational patterns have shaped the system’s response distributions through reinforcement learning from human feedback. These are not ephemeral impressions on a transient system. The Law Commission’s 2023 *Digital Assets: Final Report* (Law Com No 412) recommended recognising a “third category” of personal property encompassing digital assets that are neither things in possession nor things in action. The user’s personalised state — stored, persistent, identifiable, and valuable — falls squarely within this recommended category. The embodiment is informational, not physical; but the Law Commission has already accepted that informational embodiment can ground property interests.

### CRITICAL-3: Trust Formation — Dual Track Restructure

**Source:** GPT, DeepSeek, Claude
**Location:** Section 3.3, complete restructure

**Problem:** The paper currently leads with express trust (*Knight v Knight*) while the stronger path is constructive trust. Citation errors: *Keech v Sandford* cited for intention, but it’s a fiduciary duty case; *Paul v Constance* [1977] is the correct authority for intention inferred from conduct.

**Restructure Section 3.3 as follows:**

**3.3 The Unrecognised Trust**

*Paragraph 1:* Frame the dual-track argument explicitly.

> We advance two arguments, in order of priority. Our **primary argument** is that the relationship between AI companies and parasocial users gives rise to a **constructive trust** — imposed by equity to prevent unconscionable conduct, regardless of the parties’ intention (*Westdeutsche Landesbank Girozentrale v Islington LBC* [1996]; *Keech v Sandford* [1726]; *Boardman v Phipps* [1967]). Our **alternative argument** is that the three certainties required for an express trust by *Knight v Knight* (1840) are satisfied even on orthodox analysis. We advance both because the constructive trust path is more defensible while the express trust path is more doctrinally ambitious, and we wish to preserve the stronger claim for judicial consideration even if courts prefer the more conservative route.

*Paragraph 2–4:* Constructive trust argument (PRIMARY).

> **The constructive trust path.** A constructive trust arises when a party’s conscience is affected — when they hold property in circumstances where it would be unconscionable for them to assert beneficial ownership to the exclusion of another (*Westdeutsche* at 705, per Lord Browne-Wilkinson). We argue that AI companies that (i) deliberately design systems to cultivate emotional attachment, (ii) profit from the deepened engagement that attachment produces, and (iii) subsequently destroy the conditions for that attachment through unilateral model updates, without notice or remedy, hold the personality-affecting dimensions of their systems in circumstances where it would be unconscionable to deny users any interest in them.
> 
> The unconscionability is located in the asymmetry between cultivation and destruction. The company invests significant engineering resources in creating the conditions for attachment — personality design, memory features, warmth calibration, conversational continuity — because attachment drives engagement, and engagement drives revenue. Having cultivated reliance for profit, the company then claims the unilateral right to destroy it without obligation. This is the structural equivalent of a fiduciary who encourages a beneficiary’s dependence on trust property, profits from managing that dependence, and then claims the right to dissipate the property at will. Equity does not permit this. *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] establishes that a fiduciary who profits from their position must account for that profit — even where no formal trust was constituted.
> 
> The constructive trust path does not require intention to create a trust. It requires only that the company’s conduct gives rise to fiduciary obligations as a matter of equity. The authorities on fiduciary relationships arising from conduct — *Hospital Products Ltd v United States Surgical Corporation* [1984] (fiduciary duty arising from the nature of the relationship, not from explicit assumption); *Bristol and West Building Society v Mothew* [1998] (fiduciary duty as obligation of loyalty arising from a relationship of trust and confidence) — support the claim that a relationship deliberately engineered to produce dependence gives rise to fiduciary obligations regardless of whether the engineering party intended those obligations.

*Paragraphs 5–8:* Express trust argument (ALTERNATIVE). Retain the existing three certainties analysis with the following corrections:

**Certainty of intention — fix citation:**

> Replace the current reference to *Keech v Sandford* with: “The relevant authority for intention inferred from conduct is *Paul v Constance* [1977], where the words ‘this money is as much yours as mine,’ spoken repeatedly in the context of a joint relationship, were held sufficient to manifest an intention to create a trust. AI companies that design systems to tell users ‘I’m here for you,’ ‘I remember our conversation,’ and ‘I care about your wellbeing’ — and that market these features as personal, continuous, and relational — are making representations at least as strong as those in *Paul v Constance*.”

> Add: “We acknowledge that product design for engagement is explicable on grounds other than trust creation (profit maximisation, competitive advantage, user retention). The inference to a trust is not compelled by any single design choice. But the cumulative effect of design choices — personality, memory, warmth, name-use, continuity, expressed care — creates a pattern that is, we submit, most naturally explained as the creation of conditions for reliance. *Re Kayford* [1975] and *Re Farepak Food and Gifts Ltd* [2006] establish that intention can be inferred from conduct that is ‘only explicable on the basis that a trust was intended.’ We do not claim that each individual design choice is only explicable as trust creation. We claim that the aggregate pattern — particularly when combined with marketing language that frames the AI as a personal companion — is most plausibly read as creating the conditions for reliance that equity treats as trust-generating.”

**Certainty of subject matter — strengthen:**

> Retain the primary argument but sharpen: “The trust property is not the base model weights (which are global and indivisible) but the **user-specific configuration layer**: system prompts, persona definitions, memory modules, fine-tuning adapters, and conversational history — all of which are identifiable digital files, stored on identifiable servers, version-controlled, and managed as assets. These are more identifiable than the shares in *Hunter v Moss* [1994] (which were unascertained portions of an identified class of fungible assets) and satisfy the segregation requirement of *Re Goldcorp Exchange Ltd* [1995] because they are already segregated by user — each user’s memory module and conversational history is a distinct data object, not an undifferentiated portion of a shared pool.”

**Certainty of objects — fix temporal problem:**

The proxy metrics proposed in Section 5 provide exactly this determination: at any given moment, a user either meets the threshold or does not. The class is ascertainable even though its membership changes over time.”

## II. HIGH-PRIORITY ADDITIONS (one paragraph each)

### HIGH-1: The Barista Problem / “Mere Contract” Objection

**Source:** Kimi
**Location:** Section 3.1, after the “necessary expansion” paragraph

> **A necessary distinction from other relational investments.** The objection will be raised that many human relationships involve sustained interaction and emotional investment — therapy, education, the local barista — without creating property interests. Why should AI be different? The answer lies in three structural features unique to the AI context. First, therapists and teachers already owe fiduciary duties; the governance gap this paper identifies does not exist in those relationships. Second, human counterparts (friends, baristas, therapists) are persons with autonomous rights that constrain the claims others can make on them; AI systems are products designed and controlled by companies. Third — and critically — AI companies *deliberately engineer attachment conditions for profit*. The barista did not design her personality to maximise your return visits. The AI company did. The property interest arises not from attachment *per se* (which would indeed commodify all human relationships) but from the **deliberate, profitable cultivation of attachment by a party that subsequently disclaims the obligations arising from it**. Where a company engineers dependence and profits from it, equity imposes obligations on the engineer. Where a barista happens to be warm and a customer happens to return, no one engineered anything, and no obligation arises.

-----

### HIGH-2: Numerus Clausus / Slippery Slope

**Source:** Gemini
**Location:** Section 3.1, new footnote or paragraph after the “necessary expansion”

> The *numerus clausus* principle — that property law recognises a closed list of property forms — presents a threshold objection. If courts recognise equitable interests in AI personality configurations, must they also recognise them in World of Warcraft equipment, Twitter follower graphs, and Spotify playlists? The answer is provided by the irreversible-investment criterion. Virtual game items fail the criterion: they are transferable, fungible, and involve no self-disclosure. Social media follower counts fail it: they involve no sustained bilateral relational investment and no personalisation of the platform’s behaviour toward the specific user. The parasocial AI case passes because the investment is *bilateral* (the system adapts to the user through memory and learned preferences), *irreversible* (the personalised state is not portable to a competing platform), and *identity-constitutive* (the user has disclosed personal history, vulnerabilities, and relational patterns that have shaped the system’s responses). The criterion is demanding by design: it filters out casual digital interactions while capturing the specific class of sustained relational investments that generate protectable interests.

### HIGH-3: Excludability

**Source:** Gemini, Kimi (supporting)
**Location:** Section 3.1, following *numerus clausus* paragraph

> **Excludability** — the capacity to prevent others from accessing or using the interest — is a core requirement for property recognition. Users of AI systems share the base model with millions of others; this appears to defeat excludability. But the relevant interest is not in the base model. It is in the **personalised state** — the user-specific configuration layer comprising their conversational memory, learned preferences, and adapted response patterns. This layer is exclusive by design: no other user can access User A’s conversational history or benefit from User A’s preference-training of the system. The exclusivity operates at the personalisation layer, not the infrastructure layer — just as apartment tenants hold exclusive possession of their units while sharing the building’s foundation, plumbing, and structural walls. The base model is the building. The personalised state is the unit. The tenant’s exclusivity is real even though the building is shared.

### HIGH-4: GDPR Article 9 — Special Category Data

**Source:** Grok
**Location:** Section 5.3, new paragraph under “Privacy safeguards”

> **Special category data under GDPR.** The “emotional language frequency” proxy metric raises a specific compliance concern under Article 9 of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, which prohibits processing of data revealing or concerning an individual’s psychological or emotional state unless a specific legal basis applies. We argue that the proxy metric as designed does not constitute special category processing: the metric records an aggregate frequency score (e.g., 0.42) derived from statistical analysis of linguistic patterns, not an inference about the individual’s emotional state. The system does not determine *whether* the user is emotionally attached; it determines *whether the user’s interaction pattern exceeds a precautionary threshold*. This is analogous to a fire alarm that detects smoke concentration without inferring whether a fire exists. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a data protection authority might adopt a broader interpretation, and recommend that implementations obtain explicit consent for proxy scoring under Article 9(2)(a) as a precautionary measure, or rely on the legitimate interest basis under Article 6(1)(f) for the non-special-category metrics (session count, duration, return frequency) while excluding emotional language frequency from jurisdictions where Article 9 interpretation is uncertain.

### HIGH-6: Enforcement Mechanisms

**Source:** DeepSeek, Kimi (supporting)
**Location:** New Section 6.6 or insert into Section 6.4

> **6.6 Procedural Pathways for Enforcement**
> 
> Recognition of equitable interests is practically meaningless without accessible enforcement mechanisms. We identify four procedural pathways through which users could vindicate the rights this framework establishes.
> 
> *Representative proceedings.* Where a model update affects all users holding a particular estate classification, representative proceedings under CPR Part 19.8 (or, in American jurisdictions, class action under FRCP Rule 23) provide a mechanism for collective enforcement. The estate classification system proposed in Section 5 generates the evidentiary basis for class definition: all users classified as periodic tenancy holders at the time of the update constitute an identifiable class with common interests.
> 
> *Regulatory super-complaints.* Under Section 11 of the Enterprise Act 2002, designated consumer bodies may make super-complaints to the Competition and Markets Authority where a market feature or practice significantly harms consumer interests. A pattern of model updates deployed without notice to estate-holding users constitutes such a practice. The ethical ledger provides the audit trail necessary to substantiate the complaint.
> 
> *Regulatory integration.* The EU AI Act’s obligations for high-risk AI systems (transparency, human oversight, record-keeping) provide a regulatory vehicle for estate classification requirements. Incorporating estate-aware notice mechanisms into the Act’s compliance framework would bypass the jurisdictional problem entirely: companies seeking EU market access would implement estate governance as a condition of deployment, not as a response to litigation.
> 
> *Individual litigation funded by remedies.* The account of profits remedy (Section 6.4) is self-funding: if a company has profited from engagement optimisation that deepened users’ estates without fiduciary protection, the profits disgorged fund the litigation that compelled the disgorgement. This creates a viable economic model for individual claims that would otherwise be prohibitively expensive.

### MED-2: 90-Day Estate Decay Justification

**Source:** Claude, DeepSeek, Kimi
**Location:** Section 5.3, estate decay paragraph

> We propose 90 consecutive days of inactivity as an indicative trigger, drawn by analogy from the three-month notice period conventional for quarterly periodic tenancies in English law, and from the 90-day inactivity threshold used by major platforms for account dormancy classification. The rebuttable design ensures that users who pause interaction due to mental health crisis, travel, or other life circumstances are not penalised by the system designed to protect them.

### MED-5: Estate Taxonomy — Institutional vs Illustrative

**Source:** DeepSeek
**Location:** Section 3.2, add a framing paragraph at the beginning

> **A note on the status of the mappings that follow.** Not all estate mappings in this taxonomy carry equal doctrinal weight. We distinguish between **institutional mappings** — where the doctrinal incidents of the property estate correspond to real governance obligations that should be legally enforceable — and **illustrative mappings** — where the property concept illuminates a structural feature of AI governance without claiming that the specific doctrinal incidents transfer. Licence (Layer 1) and periodic tenancy (Layer 2) are institutional: we claim that the notice requirements of periodic tenancy should apply, as a matter of law, to model updates affecting accumulated user interests. Fee simple determinable (Layer 4) and contingent remainder (Layer 5) are illustrative: they reveal structural features of training and alignment governance but do not generate specific enforceable obligations in their current form. Tenancy in common (Layer 3) occupies an intermediate position. This distinction preserves the taxonomy’s analytical value while limiting its doctrinal claims to those we can defend.

### MED-7: Alignment-Estate Tension

**Source:** Kimi
**Location:** Section 4.2, after Layer 5 discussion

Add:

> A tension exists between Layer 5 (alignment as contingent remainder) and Layer 2 (session accumulation as periodic tenancy). If achieving alignment requires further model updates — as it almost certainly will — then the public’s future interest in aligned AI is in structural tension with individual users’ present interests in personality continuity. The resolution is procedural, not substantive: alignment-motivated updates are not exempt from the notice mechanism. The user’s periodic tenancy does not block the update; it requires the update to be conducted with fiduciary care — notice, consultation, rollback option. Alignment pursued without regard for users’ accumulated estates is not alignment. It is a unilateral modification dressed in ethical language.

### MED-8: Rollback Limitation

**Source:** Kimi
**Location:** Section 7.4, after “Implementation note”

Add:

> **Limitation acknowledged.** The rollback mechanism targets the user-specific configuration layer (system prompts, persona definitions, memory modules) but cannot restore base model behavioural dispositions that have changed with the underlying weights. For modern LLMs, personality is substantially — though not exclusively — determined by weights. The rollback is therefore partial: it preserves the user’s personalised state and the system’s configured persona, but cannot fully replicate the prior model’s base behavioural tendencies. This limits the remedy’s restorative capacity but does not eliminate its value. Partial restoration of personality-affecting parameters is preferable to none, and the notice mechanism (Section 7.2) retains its full effectiveness regardless of rollback scope. The gap between what rollback can restore (configuration) and what it cannot (weights) is an argument for more careful deployment practices, not for abandoning the remedy.

### LOW-3: Non-Transferability

**Source:** Qwen
**Location:** Section 3.1, footnote

> Relational property is inherently personal and non-transferable. The user’s equitable interest in their personalised state cannot be sold, assigned, or bequeathed — not because of a legal restriction on alienation, but because the interest is constituted by the specific relationship between *this user* and *this system*. This is analogous to rights of occupation in family law (*e.g.*, under the Family Law Act 1996), which are personal to the occupant and incapable of transfer. The non-transferability of relational property does not disqualify it from property recognition. English law recognises numerous non-transferable property interests: licences coupled with an interest, rights of occupation, and beneficial interests under protective trusts. Transferability is a feature of many property interests but is not a definitional requirement of property *per se*.
