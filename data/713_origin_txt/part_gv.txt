Bidirectional Translation in AI Governance: Language Games, Formal Limits, and the Grammar of Accountability
Abstract
When a criminal lawyer says "intent" and a machine learning engineer says "objective function," they are not mistranslating the same concept. They are, in Wittgenstein's sense, playing different language games — employing terms whose meanings are constituted entirely by their use within distinct forms of life. AI governance discourse fails not because translation is technically difficult, but because it presupposes a shared semantic ground that does not exist. The legal concept of mens rea and the computational concept of an optimisation target are not two descriptions of one thing; they are moves in incommensurable games, and the attempt to map one onto the other produces not imprecision but categorical error.
This prospectus outlines a three-paper research programme that takes this Wittgensteinian diagnosis seriously. Rather than proposing yet another interdisciplinary glossary, the programme examines where and why translation between legal and computational language games breaks down, provides formal tools for diagnosing the breakpoints, and identifies the structural conditions under which limited mutual legibility becomes possible. It comprises three interconnected papers: (A) a published legal-theoretical framework examining how constitutional and corporate law analogies both enable and distort AI accountability, now being expanded into a comparative three-jurisdiction study; (B) a game-theoretic and biopolitical analysis of how observer frameworks exploit ontological ambiguity to deny recognition; and (C) a computational diagnostic framework — currently under anonymous review at ACM FAccT 2026 — that maps the discrete dynamics of constraint interactions in large language models and proves formally that intent attribution across the legal-computational boundary is structurally undecidable.
The programme contributes original theoretical apparatus (the Constraint Cascade Model, the Liability Paradox), empirical tools (the Agency Index, the Narrative Pathology Scanner), and a positioning that treats AI governance failure as a problem in philosophy of language — specifically, in the limits of translation between language games — rather than a regulatory gap awaiting a technical fix. Recent work has extended the framework to capture bidirectional harm pathways — not only how AI outputs affect humans (Model→Human), but how human behaviour contaminates AI systems through training propagation (Human→Model) — validated through real-time analysis of the Tencent Yuanbao incident (January 2026).

1. Problem Statement
1.1 The Translation Asymmetry
Wittgenstein argued that meaning is not a property of words but of their use within a form of life. A term like "attention" means what it means because of the role it plays in a particular language game — the web of practices, institutions, and shared judgments that give it purchase. When technical AI research borrows humanistic vocabulary, it does not translate these concepts; it reconstitutes them within an entirely different language game:

"Attention" becomes the transformer architecture's core mechanism — a mathematical weighting operation bearing no relation to the phenomenological or legal senses of the word.
"Memory" becomes context windows and retrieval-augmented generation — a storage architecture, not a faculty.
"Preference" becomes reward models and RLHF pipelines — an optimisation target, not a psychological state.
"Intent" becomes objective functions and loss landscapes — a gradient direction, not a mental element.

This reconstitution is functionally successful. Systems ship. They scale. They reshape society. But the success is internal to the computational language game. The borrowed words have acquired new grammar — in Wittgenstein's technical sense — and their old grammar, the one that underwrites legal and moral reasoning, does not travel with them.
The governance crisis emerges when legal and policy frameworks attempt reverse translation: applying categories developed for human agents — mens rea, negligence, fiduciary duty, informed consent — to systems whose behaviour is constituted by an alien grammar. This is not regulatory lag. It is what Wittgenstein would recognise as a grammatical confusion: the attempt to use words according to the rules of one game while operating within another. The ontological primitives do not correspond because they were never the same primitives; they merely shared surface-level vocabulary.
1.2 Consequences of the Asymmetry
This asymmetry produces predictable governance failures:

Accountability fragmentation: When harm emerges from layer interactions (training data × RLHF × system prompt × user input), no single actor bears responsibility in ways traditional legal categories can capture.
The Liability Paradox: Criminal law relies on temporal gaps between thought, speech, and conduct to establish mens rea. In LLMs, token prediction is simultaneously thought, speech, and act, collapsing the temporal gap required for traditional intent attribution.
Bidirectional harm invisibility: Current frameworks assume harm flows from model to human. The reverse pathway — human behaviour contaminating models through L1 syntactic preferences propagating to L4 training — escapes governance entirely.
Interdisciplinary paralysis: Legal scholars and technical researchers talk past each other, each perceiving the other's frameworks as naive or irrelevant.
Policy capture: In the absence of legally-legible technical frameworks, governance defaults to industry self-regulation or technically uninformed legislation.

1.3 Research Question
Under what conditions — if any — can legal and computational language games achieve mutual legibility sufficient for genuine accountability, and what are the formal, structural limits of such legibility?
This question takes seriously the possibility that the answer is: not fully. The Liability Paradox (Paper C) provides a formal proof of one such limit. The research programme's contribution is not a universal translation protocol but a rigorous mapping of where translation succeeds, where it fails, and why — grounded in both philosophical analysis and empirical validation on deployed AI systems.
